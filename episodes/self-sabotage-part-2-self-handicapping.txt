Self-Sabotage, Part 2: Self-Handicapping

COLD OPEN

Picture a student the night before a test. He knows the test matters. He has the notes. And instead of studying, he stays up until two in the morning playing a game he doesn't even like that much. The next day he walks in and tells everyone, "I didn't study at all. I was up till two."

Now picture yourself. Something important is coming. Maybe a lesson you know the principal might walk in on. Maybe a Sunday where you're leading worship. And the night before, you find yourself doing everything except the one thing that would make it go well. Then, the next morning, you hear yourself say, "I didn't really get to prepare."

Here's the question for this episode. Why would a smart person do that on purpose? And not just once, but over and over?

THE PROMISE

In this episode you'll learn what self-handicapping is, where the research started, what fifty years of studies found about who does it and what it costs, and what actually lowers it, both in you and in your students.

Last time, at the start of part one, we touched on this idea briefly. You said that was the part you wanted more of. So this whole episode stays on that one thing.

SECTION ONE: THE ORIGINAL EXPERIMENT

Here's the first idea. Self-handicapping is putting an obstacle in your own way before a performance, so that if you fail, you have an excuse that isn't "I'm not good enough."

Let me say that again more simply. You build the excuse before the test, not after.

The term comes from two psychologists, Edward Jones and Steven Berglas, in 1978. Berglas was a young researcher working with Jones, who was already a big name in social psychology. Their starting point was a puzzle about alcohol. Why do some people who are clearly capable drink heavily right before the moments that matter most? Their guess was that the drinking was doing a job. It gave the person a story. If I fail, it was the drink. If I succeed, I succeeded even with the drink, so I must be really good.

So they built an experiment. College students came into a lab and worked on a set of problems. Here's the clever part. Some students got problems that were solvable. Others got problems that were mostly unsolvable, the kind where you're basically guessing. Then every student was told the same thing: you did great.

Think about what that does. The students with solvable problems knew why they succeeded. Their success felt earned. The students with unsolvable problems had just been told they did great, but they had no idea why. Their success felt like luck. The researchers called that "noncontingent success." Success that doesn't seem connected to anything you did.

Then the students were told there would be a second round of problems. Before it, they could choose one of two drugs as part of a supposed study on drugs and performance. One drug, they were told, would help performance. The other would hurt it. In reality the drugs were fake.

So who chose the drug that would hurt them?

The students whose first success felt like luck. They were more likely to choose the drug that would make them worse. And that makes a strange kind of sense. If you've just been told you're brilliant, and you don't know why, the next test is terrifying. You might be exposed. So you take something that gives you a cover story. If I do badly, it was the drug. My "brilliant" label survives.

One more detail that turned out to matter later. In that first study, the effect showed up in men. The women didn't show the same pattern. Hold onto that. We'll come back to it.

So that's the first idea, in one line. Self-handicapping is building your excuse ahead of time, and it shows up most when your sense of being capable feels shaky.

SECTION TWO: WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IN REAL LIFE

Here's the second idea. There are two kinds of self-handicapping, and one of them is so ordinary you might not notice you're doing it.

Later researchers, including Mark Leary and James Shepperd in the mid nineteen eighties, split it into two types.

The first type is behavioural. You actually do something that makes success harder. You don't practice. You stay up late. You leave it to the last minute. You pick a goal so hard nobody could hit it. You drink, like in the original puzzle. The obstacle is real.

The second type is claimed. You don't actually do anything harmful. You just say something. "I'm so tired today." "I've had a headache all morning." "I'm really anxious about this." "I didn't get much time to prep." The obstacle might be real or might not, but the point is that you put it out there before the performance, so everyone, including you, has a ready explanation if it goes badly.

Claimed handicaps are cheaper. They don't hurt your actual performance, at least not directly. But they still do something. They shape how you see yourself, and they shape how other people see you.

Now, why do this at all? The research points to two benefits, and you can probably feel both of them.

Benefit one: protection. If you fail, the failure is about the obstacle, not about you. Psychologists call this discounting. The bad result gets discounted because there's another cause.

Benefit two: enhancement. If you succeed in spite of the obstacle, you look even better. Psychologists call this augmenting. "I didn't even study, and I still got a B." That's a bigger win than "I studied for a week and got a B."

A researcher named Dianne Tice found something interesting in the early nineties. People with lower self-esteem tended to self-handicap mostly for protection. They were trying not to look bad. People with higher self-esteem tended to self-handicap more for enhancement. They were trying to look even better. Same behaviour, different motive.

Another thread in this research found that audience matters. People handicap more when someone they care about will see the result. If nobody will know how you did, the excuse is less needed. When someone important is watching, the excuse becomes valuable.

That one is worth sitting with for a second. Think about when you prepare least. Is it when it's just you? Or is it actually before the moments where someone you respect will see the result?

So here's the second idea in one line. Self-handicapping can be something you do, or something you say, and either way it buys protection if you fail and extra credit if you succeed.

SECTION THREE: WHAT IT COSTS

Here's the third idea. Self-handicapping works in the short term, and it costs you in the long term. And the cost tends to feed on itself.

The best study on this is from Miron Zuckerman and two colleagues in 1998. They followed university students across a semester. They measured how much each student tended to self-handicap. Then they watched what happened.

Students who self-handicapped more ended up with lower grades. That part's not surprising. If you don't study, you do worse.

But here's the part that matters. Doing worse led to more self-handicapping later. The student does badly, feels more threatened, and next time builds an even bigger excuse. They also found worse mood and worse adjustment over time. So it's not one bad night. It's a loop. Handicap, do worse, feel more threatened, handicap more.

Then in 2014, a team led by Malte Schwinger, a German educational psychologist, gathered dozens of studies on students and asked a simple question. Across all this research, is self-handicapping linked to lower achievement? The answer was yes. Not a giant link, but a real and consistent one. Students who handicap more, do worse. Across ages, across countries, across subjects.

And remember, the whole point of a handicap is to protect your sense of being capable. So the irony is sharp. The thing you do to protect your sense of ability is slowly lowering your actual ability. Not because you lack talent, but because you keep skipping the practice that would build it.

There's one more cost that's easy to miss. Other people notice. Research on how people judge self-handicappers has found that observers often like them less, and see them as less competent and less motivated, once they catch on. The excuse that was supposed to protect your image can hurt it, especially if people see you do it again and again.

So here's the third idea in one line. Self-handicapping protects you today and costs you over a season, and the costs make the next handicap more likely.

HALFWAY RECAP

Let's stop and pull together what we have so far.

One. Self-handicapping is building an excuse before a performance, and it was first shown in a lab where people whose success felt like luck chose a drug they thought would hurt them.

Two. It comes in two flavours. Doing something that makes success harder, or saying something that sets up the excuse. Either way it buys protection if you fail and bonus credit if you succeed.

Three. Over time, it lowers performance, and lower performance makes people handicap more. A loop.

Now let's ask the question you actually care about. Who does this most? And what turns it down?

SECTION FOUR: WHO DOES IT MOST

Here's the fourth idea. The people who self-handicap most aren't lazy. They're people who care a lot about looking capable and are unsure whether they really are.

In 2021, Schwinger's team published a much bigger review. They pulled together about a hundred and sixty studies with around eighty thousand people. That's a lot of people. And they looked at what goes along with self-handicapping. Let me give you three groups of findings, and I'll keep them simple.

First, self-beliefs. People with lower self-esteem, lower confidence that they can do the task, and a belief that ability is fixed, tend to handicap more. That last one ties to Carol Dweck's work on mindset. If you think ability is something you either have or don't, then every test is a verdict on who you are. That raises the stakes. And high stakes make an excuse more attractive.

Second, emotions. People with more fear of failure and more test anxiety handicap more. That fits everything so far. The handicap is a fear management tool.

Third, goals. This one is the most useful for a teacher. People who care most about outperforming others, or about looking smart compared to others, handicap more. People who care most about learning and improving, handicap less. Researchers call those performance goals and mastery goals.

And on the protective side, people who are more conscientious, more organised and steady, handicap less. People who had done well before tend to handicap less too.

What about gender? Remember the first study, where the effect showed up in men and not women? That pattern has mostly held. The big review found men self-handicap more than women. Some studies suggest it's especially true for the behavioural kind, actually doing the harmful thing. Some research also found that women tend to judge self-handicapping in others more harshly. So it may be less socially acceptable for them, which is one more reason they do it less.

Now let's bring this into your classroom. Think about the students who come to you. Many of them have a long track record of school not going well. Many have been told, directly or indirectly, that they're not school people. So when you hand a student a sheet, what does that sheet mean to him? It might not mean "practice." It might mean "here's another chance to prove you're dumb."

So he puts his head down. Or says he's tired. Or says "this is stupid." Or does it in thirty seconds with random answers. It's tempting to read that as laziness or defiance. Sometimes it is. But the research suggests another reading. That might be a handicap. If he doesn't try, he can't really fail. "I didn't try" is safer than "I tried and couldn't."

And here's a thing that seems like it would work but often doesn't. Telling that student, "You're so smart, you can definitely do this." It sounds kind. But think back to the drug study. Success that the person can't explain made the next test scarier. Praise for being smart that the student doesn't believe, or can't connect to anything he did, may raise the stakes rather than lower them. Now he has a label to protect.

So here's the fourth idea in one line. The people who handicap most are the ones who care about looking capable and fear they aren't, and that describes a lot of students who've had a rough time with school.

SECTION FIVE: WHAT LOWERS IT

Here's the fifth and last idea. Self-handicapping drops when the threat drops. When a performance stops feeling like a verdict on who you are, the excuse isn't needed.

The best classroom work on this is from Tim Urdan and Carol Midgley, out of the University of Michigan, starting in the nineties. They didn't just look at students. They looked at classrooms. Specifically, at what students thought their teacher cared about.

In some classrooms, students felt the message was: what matters is how you compare. Who got the highest mark. Who's smart and who isn't. They called that a performance goal structure. In other classrooms, students felt the message was: what matters is that you're learning, improving, understanding. Mistakes are part of it. They called that a mastery goal structure.

Students in classrooms that felt performance focused reported more self-handicapping. Students in classrooms that felt mastery focused reported less. That's correlational, so we can't say it's the whole story. But it fits everything else, and it points at something a teacher actually controls.

What does a mastery classroom sound like? Here are three things it tends to include. Just three.

One. Private feedback, not public comparison. No posting scores. No "who got the most right."

Two. Credit for improvement and effort that's tied to strategy. Not "you're smart." More like "you went back and checked that one, and it worked."

Three. Chances to redo. If a mistake can be fixed, the mistake is less dangerous. If it's final, the handicap is worth more.

Here's a related idea from Martin Covington, who wrote about what he called self-worth theory. He said effort is a double edged sword for students. Trying hard is good, because teachers reward it. But trying hard is also risky, because if you try hard and still fail, the only explanation left is low ability. So for a student who doubts himself, not trying is a way to keep his self-worth safe. Covington's point is that we have to make trying safe before students will do it.

There's also some research on something called self-affirmation. This comes from Claude Steele's work and later studies. The idea is simple. Before a threatening task, people spend a few minutes writing about something they value that has nothing to do with the task. Family, faith, a skill, a relationship. Some studies found this lowered self-handicapping. The theory is that when your worth is anchored in something bigger, one test can't threaten it as much. The research on this is promising but not huge, so treat it as something worth trying, not a sure thing.

Now let me say what probably would not work, because that's useful too.

Lecturing a student about not trying. It confirms the stakes are high, and that you're watching. That's the exact condition that increases handicapping.

Calling it out publicly. "You're just making excuses." Audience raises the threat.

Making the next task harder to prove a point. That's a bigger test, with a bigger reason to hide.

And here's what might help in the moment. Something like, "Do the first two. I want to see how you think, not whether it's right." That shrinks the test. It moves the goal from proving to showing. It doesn't guarantee anything. Some days it won't land. But it's pointed in the direction the research points.

So here's the fifth idea in one line. Self-handicapping drops when performance stops feeling like a verdict, and a teacher can shrink that verdict feeling with private feedback, credit for strategy, and second chances.

HOW IT LANDS IN YOUR WEEK

Let's bring this home. Not the students now. You.

You've told me that one of the things you want to grow in is making quick moves without asking if there's a better one. In chess, and in life. I want to offer a different angle on that. Sometimes a quick, careless move isn't just impulsiveness. Sometimes it's a handicap. If I move fast and lose, I didn't really lose. I just didn't think. If I sit and think hard and still lose, that's a real loss. So moving fast can be a way to keep the game from counting.

I'm not saying that's always what's going on. You'd know better than I would. But it's worth asking.

Same thing with prep. Think about the times you've arrived at something important less prepared than you wanted. A lesson. A meeting. A Sunday. Was it really time? Or was there a small part of you that preferred "I didn't get to prepare" over "I prepared and it still wasn't great"?

And the claimed kind. Do you ever hear yourself, before something matters, telling your wife or a colleague, "I'm so tired today," or "I didn't get much time on this"? Sometimes that's just honest. Sometimes it's a little insurance policy.

At home, it could show up in projects. Starting something big at nine at night, so if it doesn't get done, it's because it was late. Or setting a goal so huge that falling short is guaranteed and therefore doesn't really count.

In faith, it might be the most interesting of all. Self-handicapping is all about protecting the image of being capable. But the gospel doesn't ask you to protect that image. It starts by saying you aren't capable on your own, and that's fine, because your worth was never resting on your performance. That's basically the self-affirmation finding, but much deeper. When your worth is anchored somewhere a test can't touch, you don't need the excuse.

ONE THING TO TRY

Here's the one thing. Only one.

Before something this week that matters to you, ask one question. "If this goes badly, what excuse am I setting up?"

Then remove one piece of it. Just one. If the excuse is "I didn't prep," do fifteen minutes of prep. If the excuse is "I was tired," go to bed at your lights out time the night before. If it's a chess game, make your next move only after you ask, "Is there a better one?"

Then, afterwards, notice how it felt to have no excuse. It might feel more exposed. That's the point. Exposure is where the real learning happens.

What would not work here is trying to remove every excuse at once, or turning it into a big project. That just becomes a new, bigger test. One question. One excuse removed.

CLOSING RECAP

Three sentences.

Self-handicapping is building your excuse before the test, and it's strongest in people who care about looking capable and quietly doubt they are.

It protects you in the moment but lowers performance over time, and lower performance feeds more handicapping.

It drops when performance stops feeling like a verdict on who you are, which is something a teacher can shape in a classroom, and something faith can shape in a life.

I'll leave you with a line from Alistair Begg, Truth For Life, in a sermon about inadequacy. "Since dependence is the objective, weakness is the advantage."
