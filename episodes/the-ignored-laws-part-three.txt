This is the last part of the ignored laws series. In part one, we opened the three drawers. The moral law, which still binds us. The civil laws for Israel as a nation, whose general equity, their wisdom, still applies. And the ceremonial laws for worship and purity. Part one. Not abolish, but fulfil.

In Matthew chapter five, in the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus says something that surprised his listeners. Don't think that I've come to abolish the Law or the Prophets. I haven't come to abolish them, but to fulfil them. And he goes further. Not the smallest letter, not the smallest stroke of a pen, will disappear from the law until everything is accomplished.

That's strong. And then, right after, Jesus does something interesting. He goes through some of the laws, and every time, he goes deeper, not looser. You've heard it said, don't murder. But I tell you, anyone who is angry with his brother is in danger. You've heard it said, don't commit adultery. But I tell you, anyone who looks with lust has already done it in his heart.

So whatever fulfil means, it doesn't mean Jesus is lowering the bar. He's raising it. He's showing that the law was always about the heart.

It helps to know who Jesus was talking to. In the same chapter, he says that unless your righteousness goes beyond that of the Pharisees and the teachers of the law, you won't enter the kingdom. That would have shocked people. The Pharisees were the most careful law keepers around. They built fences around the law. If the law said rest on the Sabbath, they wrote long lists of exactly what counted as work, so nobody could even get close to breaking it. They were serious people.

But the fences were all on the outside. You could keep every one of their rules and still be proud, harsh and greedy inside. Jesus aimed at the inside. Not more fences. A new heart. Which is a problem for all of us, because you can't fix your own heart by trying harder. That's exactly where the rest of the story comes in.

So what does fulfil mean? The word has the sense of filling something up, bringing it to its goal, completing it. Think of a promise that's fulfilled. The promise isn't thrown away. It's kept. It's done what it was meant to do.

Let me give you a picture from your construction class. When you pour a concrete footing, you build forms first. Plywood, stakes, bracing. The forms are essential. Without them, the concrete just slumps into a puddle. They give it its shape. But once the concrete has set, you strip the forms off. Not because they were wrong. Because they did their job. And notice. The shape stays. The footing keeps exactly the shape the forms gave it. You just don't need the plywood anymore.

That's a good picture for much of what Jesus does with the law. Some of it was formwork. It held the shape until the real thing was poured. Once Christ came, the forms came off, but the shape remains.

Here are three ways the New Testament says Jesus fulfils the law. Only three.

First, he kept it. Completely. Every command, from the inside out, with a perfect heart. Every time you or I fall short, he didn't. That's not just an impressive fact. It's for us. His perfect obedience is counted to everyone who trusts him.

Second, he paid its penalty. The law doesn't just give commands. It says what happens when we break them. Death, distance from God. On the cross, Jesus took that penalty on himself. The law's verdict against us was carried out on him.

Third, he is the real thing the law pointed to. The sacrifices, the priests, the temple, the purity washings. Colossians chapter two calls these things a shadow of things to come, and says the substance belongs to Christ. A shadow is real, but it's not the thing itself. When the person walks around the corner, you stop staring at the shadow.

One line for part one. Jesus didn't abolish the law, he filled it up, by keeping it, by paying its penalty, and by being the reality it pointed to.

Part two. What happens to each drawer.

Now let's take that and walk through the three drawers, because Christ fulfils each one a little differently.

The ceremonial drawer. The sacrifices, the priesthood, the temple, the food and purity laws. This is the formwork. The book of Hebrews is basically one long explanation of this drawer. The animal sacrifices could never really take away sin, it says. They pointed to the one sacrifice that could. So when Jesus died, the sacrifices were finished. When he rose and ascended as our high priest, the priesthood was finished. When he declared all foods clean, and the gospel went to the nations, the food boundary was finished. The forms came off. You don't keep these laws, not because they were bad, but because the concrete has set.

The civil drawer. The laws for running Israel as a nation, with its courts and land and punishments. That nation, as a political state under God's direct law, ended. So the specific penalties and procedures don't bind us. But as we said in part one, their general equity remains. The railing on the roof. The protection of the poor. Fair wages. The reasons inside those laws come from God's character, and his character doesn't change. So you read the civil laws like case studies. What was God protecting here? How does that wisdom apply in Ontario?

And one honest note. The drawers aren't always perfectly neat. Take the Sabbath. It's in the Ten Commandments, the moral drawer, but it also had ceremonial features in Israel, like specific sacrifices and penalties. Reformed churches, following the Westminster Confession, have generally taught that the principle stays, one day in seven given to worship and rest, while the day itself moved to the Lord's Day, the day of the resurrection. Christians differ on the details. But notice the method. You ask which part is the formwork and which part is the shape. That question will serve you well whenever the drawers get tangled.

And the moral drawer. Love God, love your neighbour, summed up in the Ten Commandments. This one doesn't expire. Jesus deepened it, as we just heard. But something does change. Not the law. Your relationship to it.

Here's how the Westminster Confession puts it, in its chapter on the law of God. It says that true believers are not under the law as a covenant of works, to be justified or condemned by it. But the law is still of great use to them, as a rule of life.

Let me put that in plain words. The moral law is no longer a ladder you have to climb to get to God. Jesus already climbed it for you. You're not trying to earn your place by keeping it. And it can't condemn you, because Jesus took its condemnation. But the law is still the shape of a good life. It still tells you what love looks like. It's no longer a ladder. It's a map.

Think about the difference in your own home. Your kids don't obey the house rules to become your kids. They're already your kids. But the house rules still matter. They describe what it looks like to live well in your family. A kid who thinks he has to earn his place will either be anxious all the time or give up. A kid who knows he belongs can actually learn to live by the rules, because they're not a test anymore.

One line for part two. The ceremonial forms came off, the civil wisdom remains, and the moral law stays, not as a ladder to earn God, but as a map for living.

Part three. Three jobs the law still does.

So what does the law actually do now, for a Christian? John Calvin, the reformer, laid out what he called three uses of the law in his Institutes of the Christian Religion. People have argued about the numbering ever since, but the three jobs themselves are clear. Let me give them to you with Calvin's own pictures.

First, the law is a mirror. Calvin's image. You look at a mirror to see what's on your face. You don't use the mirror to wash your face. The law shows you your sin. It shows you that the snap at the kid in second period wasn't just a bad day. It was anger, the very thing Jesus said is murder at the root. That's not pleasant. But it's necessary. Without the mirror, you'd never know you needed washing. And the mirror sends you to the only place that can wash you. To Christ.

Second, the law is a restraint. Calvin talks about it like a halter on an animal. Even for people who don't love God, the law, through its warnings and consequences, holds back the worst of human behaviour. Society needs this. Your classroom needs this. You don't expect every kid to have a changed heart before you ask him to stop throwing things. Rules with consequences keep the room safe enough for anything good to happen. That's the law doing its second job, in the world at large.

Third, and Calvin called this the principal use, the main one for believers, the law is a lamp. He points to the psalm that says, your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path. For someone who already belongs to God, the law shows the way. Not to earn anything. To walk well. When you're not sure what love requires in a situation, the law gives it shape. Tell the truth. Honour your parents. Don't covet. Keep the Sabbath rest. Those aren't hoops. They're lights on the trail.

Calvin also said something bracing. He said that even for believers, the law is like a whip to a lazy donkey, to get us moving. Not to beat us, but to stir us. You know the feeling. You read a command, and it pokes you. Oh. I haven't called my mother in weeks. That's the law doing its third job.

Here's a picture from your chess. After a game, a lot of players run it through an engine. The engine shows every blunder in red. That's a mirror. It's humbling. But no serious player analyzes a game to punish himself. He analyzes it to get better. He looks at the blunder, understands it, and next game he checks for that kind of mistake before he moves. That's the mirror turning into a lamp. And here's the key. He can only analyze his losses honestly if losing doesn't destroy him. A player who's terrified of being bad at chess will avoid looking at his mistakes. A player who's secure will look straight at them. Grace makes you secure enough to look in the mirror.

One line for part three. The law is a mirror that shows your sin, a restraint that holds back evil in the world, and a lamp that shows believers the way.

Part four. Grace first, then the law.

Here's something you may have read a hundred times and never noticed. Turn to Exodus chapter twenty, where the Ten Commandments are given. What's the first line? It isn't, you shall have no other gods. It's this. I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.

Rescue first. Then rules.

God didn't say to Israel, keep these commandments and I'll get you out of Egypt. He got them out first. The Red Sea was already behind them. Then he said, here's how my rescued people live.

That order changes everything. The law was never meant to be the way into God's family. It was always the way of life for people who were already in.

The Heidelberg Catechism, a Reformed teaching document from fifteen sixty three, is built on the same order. It has three parts. People often sum them up as guilt, grace and gratitude. First, how great our sin and misery is. Second, how we're set free from it, through Christ. Third, how we thank God for that freedom. And here's the telling detail. Where do the Ten Commandments show up? In the third part. Under gratitude. The commandments are taught as the shape of a thankful life.

So now look at the two voices again. The first voice, you failed, try harder, takes the law out of the gratitude section and puts it back at the front, as a ladder. That's why it crushes you. It's the law without the rescue. The second voice, relax, it doesn't matter, takes the law out altogether. That's why it leaves you unchanged. It's rescue without the life that's supposed to follow.

The gospel voice is different from both. It says, yes, that was sin. The mirror isn't lying. And yes, you're forgiven, completely, because Jesus kept the law you broke and took the penalty you earned. And now, because you're forgiven, go and make it right. Not to earn anything. Because that's what rescued people do.

There's one more piece that makes this possible. In the Old Testament, God promised through Jeremiah that a day would come when he would write his law on his people's hearts. Not just on stone tablets. On hearts. That's the work of the Holy Spirit in a Christian. The law moves from outside you, pressing in, to inside you, pulling you forward. You start to want what it describes. Paul says in Romans chapter thirteen that love is the fulfilling of the law. When the Spirit fills a heart with love for God and neighbour, the commandments stop feeling like fences and start feeling like the natural shape of that love.

You know this from running. When you first started, a training plan probably felt like a set of orders. Run four kilometres today. Rest tomorrow. Easy pace, even though you want to go fast. But the longer you've run, the more the plan has become something you want, because you love what it produces. You don't keep the easy pace because the plan is watching you. You keep it because you've come to understand it and want what it's building. The plan didn't change. You did. That's a small picture of what the Spirit does with God's law. Same commands. New heart.

How it lands in your actual week.

Let's take that Tuesday and walk through it again, with all three jobs of the law and the right order.

The snap in second period. First, let the mirror work. Don't excuse it. Don't let the second voice say, he had it coming. It was anger, and it hurt a kid who's already been hurt plenty. Call it what it is.

Then go straight to grace. Not to a long session of beating yourself up. The mirror isn't for staring into all night. It's for seeing, and then going to the sink. So you pray something simple in the truck. Lord, I was angry and I hurt him. Thank you that you've forgiven me in Christ. That's not cheap. It cost Jesus everything. But it's complete.

And then let the lamp show the next step. What does love require now? Probably a short, honest word with that student tomorrow. Something like, hey, yesterday I was louder and sharper than I should have been. That was on me. I'm sorry. The rule still stands, but how I said it wasn't right. That's not weakness. For a kid in your Shifting Gears class, an adult who apologizes without dropping the standard might be something he's never seen. It's the law and the gospel in one sentence.

Now your classroom more broadly. Your students live under the ladder version of rules all the time. Rules as tests they keep failing. Some of them have given up on rules entirely, because the ladder felt impossible. You can show them something different. Relationship first, then rules. You belong in this room. And because you belong, here's how we live in this room. That's the Exodus order, applied to a shop full of teenagers. It won't make every kid follow the rules. But it changes what the rules mean.

Think about how often your kids say, I'm a bad kid, or, teachers hate me. They've decided their identity from their record. The ladder told them who they are. You can't preach the gospel to them in a public school classroom, and you don't need to. But you can live out its order. A kid who knows he belongs in your shop, even after a bad day, is a kid who can afford to look at his mistakes. That's the same freedom the gospel gives you, worked out in a smaller way, in a room full of sawdust.

And your church work. As someone in leadership, you'll meet both voices in other people. The person crushed by the law, who thinks God is waiting to punish him. And the person who uses grace as an excuse to never change. You can help both with the same truth. Grace first, then the law as gratitude. To the crushed person, you say, you're forgiven, really. To the drifting person, you say, forgiven people live differently, so let's talk about how.

And finally, the ordinary stuff. Marketplace. Your truck. Your marriage. The moral law shows up in all of it. Honesty in a sale, describing the flaw before the buyer finds it. Rest on the Lord's day, even when the truck needs work. Patience at home when you're tired. Every one of those is the lamp. Not a hoop. A light on the trail.

One thing to try.
