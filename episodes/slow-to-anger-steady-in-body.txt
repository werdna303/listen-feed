Slow to Anger, Steady in Body: Practicing Calm Because of God

Picture a weekday morning in your classroom. It's the second block. You've just finished explaining the task, and a student, call him J, pushes his chair back, says something sharp under his breath, and looks right at you to see what you'll do. Everyone else looks too. In that half second, your body has already started to answer. Your jaw tightens. Your shoulders come up. Your breath goes shallow and high. Your voice is loading a reply that's a little louder and a little faster than you'd choose.

Now picture the same moment, but this time your shoulders stay down. Your breath is slow. You pause for one beat, and you say something short and calm that you've said a hundred times before. The room settles, because you settled first.

That second version is what you said you want. In your words: "I want to practice the Christian slowed to anger steadiness because of God." And you asked a great follow up question. Is there some kind of training, maybe something like martial arts practice, that you could do in the garage, maybe with a YouTube trainer, to train your body language? You mentioned the kid who takes the jacket off the hook thousands of times.

Here's the promise for this episode. Slowness to anger is a fruit of trusting God, and you can also train your body so that, when the moment comes, your body is on the same side as your faith.

We'll take that in four ideas. First, why slowness to anger is about God before it's about technique. Second, a Japanese idea called zanshin, which is the relaxed alert awareness you were reaching for. Third, why repetition, the jacket on the hook kind, actually works. And fourth, a simple garage practice: breath, posture, and rehearsed calm replies.

Let's start.

Idea one. Slowness to anger starts with God.

James chapter one, verse nineteen and twenty. "Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God."

Notice the order. Quick to hear. Slow to speak. Slow to anger. James puts listening first. The speed of your anger is tied to the speed of your mouth, and the speed of your mouth is tied to how well you listen.

And notice the reason he gives. Not "because anger looks bad," and not "because you'll lose control of the class." The reason is that human anger doesn't produce the righteousness of God. In other words, your anger can't do God's work for Him. When you get hot with a student, part of what's happening is that you feel like you have to fix this right now, by force, or it won't get fixed. Anger is often a small act of trying to be in charge of an outcome.

Now Proverbs sixteen, verse thirty two. "Whoever is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city."

That's a striking comparison. A soldier who takes a city is the picture of strength. And Proverbs says the man who rules his own spirit is stronger. So slowness to anger is not weakness. It's not being a pushover. It's strength under control.

And here's where it starts with God. Over and over the Old Testament describes God Himself this way. Exodus thirty four, when God tells Moses His own name: "The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness." Nahum one, verse three: "The Lord is slow to anger and great in power."

So when you practice being slow to anger, you're not borrowing a classroom management trick. You're reflecting the character of your Father. He has every right and every power to be fast to anger with you, and He isn't. He's patient with you. The steadiness you want to show a struggling student is a small copy of the steadiness God has shown you.

And that's where trust comes in. Galatians five lists patience as a fruit of the Spirit. Fruit grows. It isn't manufactured by gritting your teeth. And it grows best in the soil of trust. When you believe God is actually in charge of your classroom, of your day, of that student's heart, you don't have to win every exchange in the moment. You can afford to be slow. A man who trusts God can afford to pause.

Think about where your quick anger usually comes from. Very often it's fear wearing a costume. Fear that the class will slip away from you. Fear of looking weak in front of the other students. Fear that this kid will never change. Fear that you're failing. Trust answers those fears. God is sovereign over the second block. Your worth isn't on trial every time a student mouths off. You are a servant, not the savior of the room.

And we have a picture of this in Jesus Himself. First Peter chapter two, verse twenty three. "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly."

Listen to the hinge of that verse. He did not revile in return, but continued entrusting himself to God. The steadiness came from the entrusting. Jesus was not calm because He didn't care, or because He was numb. He was calm because He had handed the outcome to His Father. That's the pattern for you. When a student says something cutting, the question underneath is, "Who's going to make this right?" And the answer of faith is, "God will, in His way and His time. My job right now is to be faithful, not to win."

So here's idea one in one line. Slowness to anger grows out of trusting God, because a man who knows God is in charge can afford to pause.

Idea two. Zanshin, or relaxed alert awareness.

You remembered there was a Japanese word for relaxed alert awareness. You're right. The word is zanshin. It's spelled Z A N S H I N.

Literally it means "remaining mind." It comes from Japanese martial arts like kendo, which is sword fighting with bamboo swords, and kyudo, which is archery. It's usually described as a state of relaxed alertness.

Here's the picture that helped me. In Japanese archery, the archer releases the arrow, and then he doesn't immediately drop his arms and walk off. He holds his posture for a moment after the release, calm and aware. The mind remains. In kendo, after a strike, the fighter returns to a ready stance and stays alert. Zanshin is the mental and physical readiness that stays with you before, during, and after an action.

Notice the two words together. Relaxed and alert. Most of us think of those as opposites. When we're relaxed, we're a bit checked out. When we're alert, we're tense. Zanshin says you can be both at once. Loose shoulders, soft hands, slow breath, and eyes that see the whole room.

Now, why does this matter for anger? Because anger usually shows up when we're either checked out or clenched. When you're checked out, a student's comment catches you by surprise, and surprise turns quickly into irritation. When you're clenched, you're already halfway to a fight before anything happens. Zanshin sits in between. You're not surprised, because you were paying attention. And you're not clenched, because you're relaxed. So when J pushes his chair back, you've already seen it coming, and your body has nothing to tighten from.

There's a Christian way to say something close to this. Scripture talks a lot about being watchful and sober minded. First Peter five, verse eight: "Be sober minded; be watchful." That's not anxious watchfulness. It's calm, clear eyed attention. A shepherd watching sheep. Not panicked, not asleep.

So I'd put it this way for you. Zanshin is the body and attention part. Trust in God is the heart part. Trust says, "I don't have to be afraid." Zanshin says, "So I can stay loose and paying attention." The faith gives the reason. The practice gives the shape.

Here's idea two in one line. Zanshin means relaxed alertness, loose in the body and clear in the eyes, and it's the shape that trust in God can take in your posture.

Idea three. Why repetition works. The jacket on the hook.

You mentioned the kid who takes the jacket off the hook thousands of times. That's from the twenty ten version of The Karate Kid, with Jackie Chan as Mr. Han. He has the boy take his jacket off, hang it up, take it down, put it on, drop it, pick it up, over and over. The boy thinks it's pointless chores. Then Mr. Han starts throwing punches at him, and the boy blocks them with the same motions. The jacket drill had been building the movement into his body without him knowing. The older original movie did the same thing with "wax on, wax off."

It's a movie, so it's exaggerated. But the core idea is real. What you repeat calmly, in low pressure moments, becomes available to you in high pressure moments.

Here's why. Under stress, your body falls back on what's most practiced. When your heart rate jumps, the thoughtful, planning part of your brain gets less room to work, and habits take over. That's why a teacher who has rehearsed a calm reply can find it under pressure, and a teacher who hasn't rehearsed one ends up with whatever comes out. And what comes out is usually louder and faster than you want.

There's research on this. Back in the nineties, a team of researchers did a large review of studies on mental practice, which means rehearsing a skill in your head without physically doing it. The review was called "Does Mental Practice Enhance Performance?" The answer was yes. Mental rehearsal improved performance. Not as much as real physical practice, but it clearly helped.

So for you, the lesson is: rehearse in your head, and also rehearse with your body and your actual voice. Say the words out loud. Stand the way you want to stand.

Now, one important thing. You said, "maybe not that, probably not that extreme." Good. You don't need thousands of reps of anything. You need a small number of the right reps, done often. Five minutes, most days. That's enough to start moving the default.

So here's idea three in one line. What you rehearse calmly when nothing is happening becomes what your body reaches for when something is happening.

Now idea four. The actual garage practice.

I'm going to give you three drills. Breath, posture, and words. Each one takes a couple of minutes. Together, about five to ten minutes.

Drill one. Breath.

There's a study out of Stanford, published in twenty twenty three, by a team led by Melis Yilmaz Balban, with Andrew Huberman and David Spiegel. They had about a hundred adults practice for five minutes a day for one month. Some did mindfulness meditation. Others did different breathing exercises. One of the breathing exercises was called cyclic sighing. The study found that all the groups improved, but the breathing groups improved mood more than the meditation group, and cyclic sighing did the best of all. It improved mood the most and slowed people's resting breathing rate the most.

Here's how cyclic sighing works. Breathe in through your nose. Then take a second, shorter sip of air on top of that, to fill your lungs all the way. Then let it out slowly through your mouth, a long, unhurried exhale. That's one round. Do that for five minutes.

The key is the long exhale. A slow exhale tends to calm the body down. And here's the neat part for the classroom. One or two of these sighs is almost invisible. You can do them while a student is talking. Double inhale through the nose, long quiet exhale. Nobody knows. But your body gets the message: we're not in danger here.

So in the garage, five minutes of cyclic sighing. And then in class, one or two sighs at the moment you feel the heat rising.

Drill two. Posture.

This is your zanshin drill. Stand in the garage with your feet about shoulder width apart. Knees soft, not locked. Let your shoulders drop away from your ears. Unclench your jaw. Let your hands hang loose, or rest them open in front of you. Eyes up and soft, taking in the whole wall, not staring at one spot.

Now hold that for a minute. Breathing slowly.

Then do a check. Where's the tension? For most men under stress, it's the jaw, the shoulders, and the hands. So run that little scan: jaw, shoulders, hands. Loosen each one.

Now here's where it becomes like the jacket drill. Picture the moment. J pushes his chair back and says something. And you, standing in the garage, notice your body want to tighten, and you go through the scan instead. Jaw, shoulders, hands. Breathe. Stay loose. Eyes on the whole room.

Repeat that a few times. Picture a few different moments. A student refusing work. A student being rude to another student. Something at home, too. Your wife says something that stings when you're tired. Picture it, feel your body start to clench, and return to relaxed and alert.

That's your jacket on the hook. Small, calm, repeated.

If you want a YouTube trainer for this, I'd suggest searching for beginner follow along videos in a slow, controlled martial art. Tai chi for beginners is a good fit, because the whole art is about relaxed, balanced, slow movement with attention. Another option is a beginner karate kata follow along, where you learn a short pattern of movements and repeat it. You're not training to fight. You're training to move with a quiet, steady body. Pick one trainer, follow one short video, and repeat that same video for a few weeks. Same video every time is better than a new one each day, because the point is repetition.

Drill three. Words.

This is the one most teachers skip, and it might matter most. Decide ahead of time on two or three calm replies. Short. Same tone every time. Then say them out loud in the garage, in the posture you just practiced, with a slow exhale first.

Here are some examples. You'd make your own, but something like:

"I hear you. Let's talk in a minute."

Or, "That's not okay. Take a breath and we'll try again."

Or simply, "I'm not going anywhere."

Notice those are short. In a heated moment, short words are easier to say calmly, and easier for a student to hear. Long explanations tend to speed up and get louder as you go.

Alistair Begg made a point about this kind of thing in a Truth For Life devotional on James chapter one. He noticed that people who talk a lot often get angry fast. Speed of words and speed of temper travel together. So rehearsing short replies isn't just good classroom technique. It's following James. Slow to speak, slow to anger.

When you practice saying your calm line in the garage, say it at the speed you want to say it in class. Slower than feels natural. Lower than feels natural. Then pause after it. That pause is zanshin. The archer holding his posture after the arrow is gone.

Now let's put the three drills together into the moment itself. When the heat comes in class, here's the sequence. See it. Breathe. Soften. Speak.

See it means you notice what's happening, both in the student and in yourself. "He's frustrated. And I'm getting hot." Just naming it to yourself slows things down a little.

Breathe means one cyclic sigh. Double inhale, long exhale.

Soften means the scan. Jaw, shoulders, hands.

Speak means your rehearsed line, slow and low, and then a pause.

See, breathe, soften, speak. That whole thing can take three or four seconds. In a heated moment, three or four seconds is a long time. It's often the difference between a reply you regret and a reply that settles the room.

And here's something worth knowing. The goal is not to never feel anger. Anger is a feeling God gave us, and sometimes it's right. If a student is being cruel to another student, you should care about that. Ephesians four says, "Be angry and do not sin." The goal is not to feel nothing. The goal is to be slow, so that what you do with the anger comes from a settled place instead of a hot one. Steady doesn't mean soft. Sometimes the steadiest thing you can do is say a firm "no" with a calm face.

So here's idea four in one line. Breath, posture, and words, each practiced for a couple of minutes in the garage, give your body a calm default to fall back on.

Now let's talk about how this lands in your actual week.

In the classroom. Your classroom is small, which is a gift and a challenge. It's a gift because you can see everyone. It's a challenge because every reaction you have is magnified. With a handful of students, they're all watching you all the time. So your body language is teaching all day long, even when you're not talking. When you stay relaxed and alert, you're showing them what steadiness looks like, maybe for the first time. Many of your students have seen a lot of adults get loud. A teacher who stays calm is a kind of quiet sermon.

A practical idea. Choose one moment each day for zanshin. Maybe the transition into the second block, or right after lunch, whatever is usually the hardest. Before that moment, do two cyclic sighs and run the scan: jaw, shoulders, hands. Walk into that block relaxed and alert, on purpose.

At home. This isn't only for the classroom. When you get home tired, the same patterns show up. Small irritations with your wife can come out sharper than you mean, simply because your body is still carrying the day. The garage practice can be your bridge. Five minutes of breathing in the garage before you walk in the door is a gift to your wife. You come in having already set down some of the day.

And in your faith. Tie the practice to prayer. While you're doing the slow exhale, you could pray something short. "Lord, you are slow to anger. Make me like you." Or simply, "You're in charge. I can wait." That turns the breathing drill into a small act of trust. The breath slows the body. The prayer reminds the heart why it can slow down.

And here's an honest note. You'll still get angry sometimes. You'll still have days when your voice gets sharp. That's not the end of the practice. That's part of it. When it happens, the zanshin move is to notice, reset, and continue. And the Christian move is to confess it, receive grace, and if it's right, say sorry to the student or to your wife. A teacher who apologizes calmly teaches as much as one who never slips.

This week, do five minutes in the garage, most days. Cyclic sighing for two minutes. The posture scan for two minutes, picturing one hard classroom moment. And then say your one calm line out loud three times, slowly. Just one line. Pick it tonight.
