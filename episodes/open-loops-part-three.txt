Part one. A list has no when.

Let's start with something that might sting a little, for a man who loves lists. A to do list, by itself, is a list of wishes.

That's not an insult to lists. Lists are great for capturing things, so they're out of your head. But think about what a list item actually says. Call the parent. Start the shelving. Run. It says what. It doesn't say when. It doesn't say where. And without a when and a where, every item on the list is waiting for a moment that never officially arrives. It sits there, and each time you look at it, you have to decide all over again whether now is the time. And most of the time, the answer is, not right now.

Think about how your construction class runs. If you just had a whiteboard that said, shed, fence repair, picnic tables, nothing would get built. What makes the shop work is that each job gets a day. Monday we frame the walls. Wednesday we put on the sheathing. The list of projects tells you what exists. The schedule tells you what happens. You'd never run the shop off the project list alone. But a lot of us run our own lives that way.

And there's a hidden cost to a list with no whens. Every time you read it, you make a small decision about every item on it. Not this one. Not now. Maybe later. Not this one either. Those little decisions are tiring in their own way, and worse, they train you to skim past your own list. After a few weeks, the items you keep skipping become wallpaper. You stop seeing them at all, but they still nag, like the loops from part one.

There's a German psychologist named Peter Gollwitzer who has spent much of his career on exactly this gap. The gap between meaning to do something and actually doing it. And he gave a name to the fix. He called it an implementation intention. That's a mouthful, so let me make it simple. It's an if then plan. If it's this situation, then I'll do that thing.

Not, I'll run more this week. But, when my alarm goes on Monday, I'll put on the running clothes I left by the bed and walk out the door. Not, I'll call that parent. But, Tuesday at lunch, after I eat, I'll call from the empty classroom.

Gollwitzer's idea is that a plan like this hands control over to the situation. You don't have to remember. You don't have to rouse yourself. When the moment shows up, the plan kicks in, almost automatically. The situation becomes the cue. Lunch ends, and the phone call is just what happens next.

Does it work? In two thousand six, Gollwitzer and a colleague named Paschal Sheeran pulled together close to a hundred studies on these if then plans, involving thousands of people. And what the study found was a solid boost. People who made if then plans reached their goals noticeably more often than people who only had the goal. The effect was in the medium to large range, which in psychology is a real result.

Now, the honest friend part. In the years since, research methods have gotten stricter, and newer analyses that try to correct for the habit of only publishing the studies that worked suggest the true effect is smaller than that first estimate. Smaller, but still there. So don't think of an if then plan as a magic switch. Think of it as a real nudge, one that shows up again and again across many kinds of goals.

And notice how well this fits what you heard in part one. Remember the Consider it done study, where making a specific plan quieted the nagging thoughts about an unfinished goal? Same family of ideas. A plan with a when and a where does two things. It quiets the loop now, and it makes the loop more likely to close later.

One line for part one. A list says what, a calendar says when, and a plan with a when and a where is far more likely to happen.

Part two. Two studies worth remembering.

Let me give you two studies that make this concrete. One about a flu shot, and one about exercise.

The first is from two thousand eleven. A team led by Katherine Milkman worked with a big utility company. The company was offering free flu shots at clinics on site. Everybody got a reminder letter with the clinic times. But some people got a letter with one extra line. A little blank space that said, write down the date you plan to go. And some got a letter with space for the date and the time.

That's it. No lecture. No scary statistics. Just a blank to fill in.

What did the study find? The group that wrote down a date and a time got the flu shot more often than the group that just got the reminder. Not hugely, a few percentage points, but this was a cheap little line on a letter, sent to thousands of people. Just writing down a date, without a time, didn't make a clear difference. It was the specific date and time that mattered. And the effect was biggest where the shot was hardest to get, at places where the clinic only ran on a single day. When there was only one chance, having that chance on your calendar made the difference.

Think about that. A date alone didn't do much. A date and a time did. That's the difference between someday this week and Tuesday at lunch.

The second study is about exercise. From two thousand two, by Sarah Milne, Sheina Orbell and Paschal Sheeran. They wanted to get young adults to exercise more. So they tried two approaches. One group got the motivational treatment. Information about heart disease, about why exercise matters, the whole pep talk. And it worked, sort of. It made people want to exercise more. Their intentions went up.

But here's the kicker. Their actual exercise didn't really budge. Wanting more didn't lead to doing more.

Another group got the same motivation, plus one extra step. They were asked to write down when and where they would exercise in the coming week. And that group's actual exercise went up dramatically. Motivation set the goal. The plan made it happen.

I think that's one of the most useful findings for your whole life. You probably don't need more motivation. You care about running. You care about your students. You care about getting the truck sorted and the garage organized. Motivation isn't your bottleneck. The bottleneck is the when.

And you see this in your students all the time. The motivational speaker comes to the school. The kids are fired up. A week later, nothing has changed. Because nobody turned the fire into a time and a place.

One line for part two. Wanting more doesn't change much, but a written date and time does.

Part three. Decide once, while you're fresh.

Now, why does this work so well? Why does a calendar entry beat a good intention? Here's where I want to talk about the second half of today's title. Decide once, while you're fresh.

Go back to that Monday morning. At nine thirty on Sunday night, you wanted to run. At six on Monday morning, a different version of you was in charge. Tired, cold, comfortable. And that version of you got to reopen the question. That's the problem. Not that you're weak. That the decision was still open, and it got handed to the worst possible judge.

You may have heard a popular explanation for this. The idea was that willpower is like a gas tank. Every decision you make burns a little fuel, and by the end of the day you're running on empty, so you make worse choices. There's a famous study often used to support this, about judges in Israel deciding parole cases, where the judges seemed to grant parole much more often right after a meal break, and much less often as the session went on.

I have to be honest with you about both of these. The gas tank idea, which psychologists called ego depletion, took a big hit in two thousand sixteen. Martin Hagger led a huge effort where more than twenty labs ran the same experiment, carefully, and found basically no effect. And the judges study has been challenged too. Critics pointed out that the order of cases wasn't random, and other things could explain the pattern. So I'm not going to tell you your brain runs out of willpower like a truck runs out of gas. That story is shaky.

But you don't need it. There's a simpler truth that holds up just fine, and you've lived it. In the moment, the thing right in front of you wins. The warm bed. The phone. The couch after a long day. Economists call this present bias. Future benefits feel faint. Present comfort feels loud. When you're tired, or stressed, or comfortable, the question should I do the hard thing now almost always gets answered with, not now.

So here's the trick. Don't ask the question in the moment. Ask it earlier, when you're fresh and you can see clearly what matters. Answer it then. Write the answer on the calendar. And then, when the moment comes, there's no question left to ask. It's just Tuesday at lunch. It's just six o'clock at the end of the street.

There's an old story about this, from Homer. Ulysses wanted to hear the song of the sirens, the song that lured sailors to crash on the rocks. So, while he was still clear headed, he had his crew tie him to the mast and plug their own ears with wax. He told them, no matter what I say later, don't untie me. He knew that the future version of himself, hearing the song, would beg to steer for the rocks. So the clear headed version decided for him.

Your calendar can be your mast. The fresh you on Sunday afternoon ties the tired you to the plan.

And you already do this in chess. Think about your opening preparation. You don't figure out your response to the first few moves during the game, with the clock ticking and your opponent staring at you. You decide those moves at home, calm, with time to think. Then in the game, you just play them. You save your thinking for the positions that really need it. That's deciding once, while you're fresh.

So when are you fresh? For most people it isn't late at night. For you, I'd guess it's early. You're up at five thirty for devotions. That's often the clearest your head will be all day. Before the emails, before the hallway, before the kid who walks in already angry. Your values are close to the surface then. You've just been reminded of what matters. That's a good time to look at the day and decide what it's for. Not to do it all, just to decide it. Five minutes with the calendar after your devotions can do more than an hour of planning at ten at night.

Your students have the opposite problem. A lot of their big decisions get made at the worst possible moment. Do I go to school today gets decided at seven forty, in bed, with the phone in hand. Do I do the assignment gets decided at eleven at night. Part of what you can teach them is just this idea. Make the decision earlier, when you're clearer, and then let the plan carry you.

One line for part three. In the moment, comfort wins, so make the decision earlier, when you're fresh, and let the calendar hold it.

Part four. A calendar you can trust.

A calendar only works if it's honest. If your calendar is full of things that never happen, your brain learns to ignore it. Just like a smoke alarm that goes off every time you make toast. After a while, you don't even look up.

So here are three habits that keep a calendar honest. Only three.

First, put fewer things on it, and make them real. Remember the planning fallacy from last time. You'll be tempted to schedule a whole Saturday of jobs, because the Sunday night you is optimistic. Don't. Put the two or three things that truly matter in a week on the calendar, with honest amounts of time. A calendar with three appointments you keep is worth more than one with twelve you skip. Every time you keep an appointment with yourself, you teach your brain the calendar means business.

Second, treat appointments with yourself like appointments with other people. If the principal asked to meet you Tuesday at lunch, you'd be there. You wouldn't decide at eleven fifty that you didn't feel like it. So when you put call the parent on Tuesday at lunch, give it the same respect. It's a meeting. The other person just happens to be you, and the student who needs that call.

Third, never move something to later. If something on the calendar truly can't happen, and that will be true sometimes, you're a teacher with a family, things blow up, then don't just delete it or push it to someday. Move it to a specific new slot. Tuesday's call becomes Thursday at lunch. That keeps it a plan, and not a wish. Later is where good intentions go to die.

And here's a fourth thing, a bonus, because you're a teacher. Leave some open space. A teacher's day blows up all the time. A fight in the hallway. A kid in crisis. A surprise meeting. If every minute of your calendar is booked, one blowup knocks over every domino after it, and by Wednesday the whole week's plan is in pieces, and you feel like the calendar failed. Leave a little slack. An empty block on Thursday afternoon can catch whatever spilled from Tuesday. A calendar with some air in it bends instead of breaking.

And here's one more thing that ties back to part one. The calendar is where the when lives, but it shouldn't be where every task lives. Your list is still the place to catch everything. The calendar is for the few things that need a time to happen. Think of the list as the parts bin, and the calendar as the job you're doing this Saturday. You pull a few things from the bin and give them a slot.

One line for part four. Keep the calendar honest, few real appointments, kept like a meeting, and moved to a specific new time, never to later.
