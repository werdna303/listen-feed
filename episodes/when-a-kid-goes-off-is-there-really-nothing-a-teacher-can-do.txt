Listen. When a kid goes off, is there really nothing a teacher can do?
For your run. About 30 minutes.

Cold open

Picture a regular morning in your room. The taxis have dropped everyone off. Desk sheets are out. One student comes in already wound up. Maybe something happened at home, maybe in the taxi, maybe nothing you will ever know about. Within ten minutes he is swearing, the chair gets shoved, and another kid starts feeding it. You look over at your John Howard partner, and the thought that goes through your head is one you have heard in a lot of staff rooms. "There is nothing we can actually do. The system ties our hands."

That is the question for today. You asked it like this: is it actually true that with the current system there is nothing teachers can do when kids are going off and out of control?

Here is the promise. By the end of this run you will know that the honest answer is no, that is not true, but the things you can do are different from the things people usually mean when they say it. Most people mean, "I can't make the kid leave and not come back." That part is mostly true. But there are four big levers you do have, and in a small room like yours they are actually stronger than suspension would be.

We will go through four ideas. One, what the Ontario rules actually say you can and cannot do. Two, what you can do in the moment, while it is happening. Three, the paper trail and getting admin involved, which is where a lot of the real power sits. And four, what research says works in small, high needs rooms like yours, before anything starts.

Let's go.

Section one: what the rules actually say

Here is the first big idea. In Ontario, a teacher cannot suspend. Only a principal can. But a teacher is not powerless in the discipline system. You are the one who starts it.

Let's walk through it slowly.

Ontario's rules for student behaviour sit in the Education Act, in a section called Part Thirteen, plus a regulation and a few ministry memos. The one you will hear named most is Policy and Program Memorandum number one forty five. People call it PPM 145. That memo is about progressive discipline.

Progressive discipline means the response steps up over time and fits the kid and the situation. It starts small. A conversation. A reminder of expectations. A call home. A restorative conversation. A change of seat or schedule. A referral to a support worker. Then, if it is serious or keeps happening, it can move up to suspension, and in the most serious cases, expulsion.

Now here is the part that is easy to miss. Most of those steps are yours. The conversation, the call home, the restorative circle, the change in routine, the referral. Those are teacher moves. You have restorative practices training. That training is a progressive discipline tool, right there in the ministry's own list. So when someone says "teachers can't do anything," what they usually mean is "teachers can't do the top step." That is true. But the top step is a small part of the ladder.

Now, the top step. Only a principal can suspend, and a suspension can last from one day up to twenty school days. Before suspending, the principal has to weigh what the rules call mitigating factors. Can the student control the behaviour? Does the student understand the consequences? Is there recent trauma or something in the student's history? For a student with an Individual Education Plan, the principal must also ask whether the behaviour is linked to the disability, whether the accommodations were actually given, and whether suspension would likely make the behaviour worse.

Think about your room for a second. Section twenty three. Care and Treatment. Almost every one of your students would trip at least one of those factors. Trauma, a disability, behaviour that is part of why they are placed with you in the first place. So in a room like yours, suspension is often legally harder to justify. That is not the system being broken. That is the system recognizing that sending a kid home to the very situation that is driving the behaviour usually does not help.

And there is research behind that instinct. Across many studies, being suspended is linked to worse outcomes, not better behaviour. Kids who are suspended are more likely to be suspended again, more likely to fall behind, and more likely to leave school. The studies cannot always separate cause from effect, because the kids being suspended are often already struggling. But nobody has found good evidence that suspension teaches the lesson people hope it teaches. For a kid who already wants out of school, a day at home can feel like a reward.

So here is section one in a line. You cannot suspend, but you own most of the discipline ladder, and in a Care and Treatment room the top rung is the least useful one anyway.

Section two: what you can do in the moment

Here is the second big idea. When a kid is escalating, your job is not to win. Your job is to match your response to where the kid is in the escalation. And there is a simple map for that.

You may have seen this in CPI training, from the Crisis Prevention Institute. Their model has four stages, and each stage has a matching staff response. I will give you all four, then we will slow down on the first two, because that is where you have the most control.

Stage one is anxiety. The kid is changing. Fidgeting, pacing, going quiet, getting short with people. The matching response is supportive. Calm voice. Open body language. Listen.

Stage two is defensive. Now the kid is arguing, challenging, refusing, swearing at you. The matching response is directive. Clear, simple, respectful limits. And this is the key phrase from the model: avoid power struggles.

Stage three is risk behaviour. Now someone could get hurt. Throwing things, going after another student, going after you. The response is safety. Get other kids clear. Get help. Physical intervention only if it is truly necessary and only if you are trained in it.

Stage four is tension reduction. The kid has burned out. Energy drops. Sometimes they cry, sometimes they go silent, sometimes they act like nothing happened. The response is to reconnect. Quiet conversation. Then later, debrief with your team.

So that is four stages. Anxiety, defensive, risk, tension reduction. Supportive, directive, safety, reconnect.

Now let's slow down on the first two, because this is where the "nothing I can do" feeling usually comes from. Most blowups that end in someone throwing a chair passed through stage one and stage two first. And at those two stages, the adult's next move matters a lot.

At stage one, the move is to notice early and lower the temperature. Walk over. Crouch down or sit beside, not stand over. Low voice. "Hey, you seem off this morning. Want to start with the easy page?" Or give a job. "Can you take this to the office for me?" That is not rewarding bad behaviour. That is buying the kid's nervous system time.

At stage two, the kid is pushing. Here is what tends to work. Short sentences. One direction at a time. Offer two choices you can live with. "You can work at your desk or at the side table." Then step back and give time. Do not stand there waiting for compliance, because that turns it into a show.

Here is what tends not to work, even though it feels like it should. Lecturing. Arguing about whether the kid really said what he said. Asking "why are you doing this" in the middle of it, because an escalated kid usually cannot answer that. Threatening a consequence you cannot deliver. And the big one: making it a public win or lose. If there is an audience, the kid will choose looking strong in front of peers over doing what you asked. Every time.

So what to definitely say: short, calm, specific. "I'm going to give you a minute." "Your choice is here or there." "We'll talk when you're ready." What to definitely not say: "Don't you dare talk to me like that." "Do you want to go to the office?" said as a dare. Or anything sarcastic. Sarcasm is gasoline on stage two.

Now let me connect this to something you told me you want to work on. You said you tend to make spontaneous moves in chess without stopping to ask whether there is a better one, and that you do the same thing in life. An escalating kid is the most high pressure version of that. The urge is to react to the last thing he said. The better move is a two second pause where you ask yourself one question: what stage is he in? If you can name the stage, you know the response. Supportive, directive, safety, or reconnect. That one question is the "is there a better move" check, built into the moment.

Section two in a line. In the moment, name the stage and match the response, and remember most blowups are prevented at stage one or two, not stopped at stage three.

Halfway recap

Let's pause and recap, because we are about halfway.

Idea one. In Ontario, you cannot suspend, but you own most of the progressive discipline ladder. And in a Care and Treatment room, suspension is often the weakest tool anyway, legally and in the research.

Idea two. In the moment, name the stage. Anxiety, defensive, risk, tension reduction. Then match it. Supportive, directive, safety, reconnect. Avoid the power struggle, especially with an audience.

Two more ideas to go. The paper trail, and what works before anything even starts.

Section three: documenting and getting admin involved

Here is the third big idea. Writing it down is not paperwork for its own sake. In Ontario, writing it down is the thing that legally pulls the rest of the system in. It is one of the strongest levers you have.

There are two different reporting systems, and it helps to keep them separate.

The first one is under the Education Act. Section three hundred point two, if you ever want to look it up. If a student does something that a principal would have to consider suspension or expulsion for, any board employee who sees it has to report it to the principal. Things like threats, physical assault, being under the influence, uttering a threat to seriously hurt someone. The rule says you report as soon as reasonably possible, and no later than the end of the school day. If things are urgent, you can tell the principal verbally first, but a written report has to follow. That written report is the Safe Schools Incident Reporting Form, Part One.

Here is the part most teachers do not know. The principal then has to investigate. And the principal has to communicate the results back to you, the reporting teacher. The principal also has to give you a written acknowledgement using Part Two of the form. So the form is not a black hole. The law says it comes back to you.

The second reporting system is the Occupational Health and Safety Act, the workplace safety law. This one is about you as a worker. If a student hurts you or tries to, that is workplace violence under the law, and your board has its own reporting process for it, usually an online form. This is separate from the Safe Schools form. Sometimes one incident needs both.

And this law gives you one more thing that matters a lot. Under the Occupational Health and Safety Act, section thirty two point zero point five, the board has to tell workers about a risk of violence from a person with a history of violent behaviour, if you can expect to run into that person and the risk could lead to physical injury. In schools, the main tool for that is the student safety plan. The plan should tell you what the triggers are, what the early signs look like, what to do, and who to call. There is even a shorter version, a truncated safety plan, meant for occasional and itinerant staff.

So now let's put that together for your room. If you have a student who has gone physical or come close, it is reasonable to ask your principal, or Crane, whether there is a safety plan, and if there is, to read it. If there is not and there should be, that is a fair thing to raise. You are not being difficult. You are asking for something the law expects.

Now, the honest part. Documentation does not make the kid stop today. It is slow. But here is why it matters. Without a record, every incident looks like the first one. With a record, a pattern shows up. Patterns are what get a student more support, a changed schedule, a youth worker plan, a referral, a safety plan, or in the rare case, a different placement. Admin cannot act on a pattern they cannot see.

Here is what tends to work when you write it up. Stick to what you saw and heard. Times. Words actually said, in quotes if you remember them. What happened just before. What you did. What happened after. Use initials. Here is what tends not to work. Opinions about the kid's character. "He is just a bad kid." Guesses about motives. Long emotional write ups at the end of a bad day. Those read as venting and get taken less seriously.

And one more thing on getting admin involved. Bring them in early, before the crisis. A short heads up email that says "Here is a pattern I'm seeing with one student, here is what I've tried, here is what I'm wondering about" lands very differently from a call in the middle of a meltdown. It makes you look like the steady one. And it gives your principal the chance to help you shape the plan instead of cleaning up after it.

Section three in a line. In Ontario, writing it down is what legally pulls the principal and the safety system in, so document facts, use the forms, and ask for the safety plan.

Section four: what works in small, high needs rooms

Here is the fourth and last big idea. In a small room like yours, the most powerful thing you can do about kids going off happens before anyone goes off. It is structure plus relationship.

Let me give you three pieces, each with research behind it.

The first piece is the greeting at the door. There is a study by Clayton Cook and his team, published in 2018, called Positive Greetings at the Door. They randomly assigned teachers either to greet students at the door each day or to a comparison group. The greeting was simple. Say the kid's name, make eye contact, a handshake or a fist bump or a quick word, and a reminder of what to start on. The classrooms with the greetings saw more time on task and less disruptive behaviour. It cost nothing. Took maybe a minute.

Why would that work? Because the greeting is a stage one check. You see every kid's face before they sit down. You catch the one who came in hot. You get to do your supportive move at the door instead of at minute ten when the chair moves. In a room where kids arrive by taxi at different times, that door moment is gold.

The second piece is predictability. Kids from hard places often live with a lot of chaos. When a room is predictable, the brain does not have to stay on high alert. Same start every day. A desk sheet on the desk. The plan on the board. Warnings before transitions. Your morning desk sheets are already doing this. They are not just math practice. They are a predictable on ramp that tells a stressed kid, "You know what to do here."

The third piece is a mindset from Ross Greene, the psychologist behind Collaborative and Proactive Solutions. His line is "kids do well if they can." The idea is that most challenging behaviour comes from a lagging skill, like frustration tolerance or flexibility, meeting a specific expectation the kid cannot meet yet. So instead of asking "how do I make him stop," you ask "what expectation keeps setting this off, and what skill is he missing?" Then, at a calm time, not in the moment, you sit with the kid and solve that one problem together. "I notice when we do the reading part, things get hard. What's up?" Then you listen. Then you come up with a fix you can both live with.

Your restorative practices training fits right into this. The restorative conversation after an incident is the place where the reconnect stage from CPI and the problem solving from Greene meet. Stage four is not just calming down. It is where the next blowup gets prevented.

Now, an honest caution. None of these are magic. A greeting at the door will not stop a kid who walked in after a terrible night from having a terrible day. Predictability helps a lot of kids most days, not all kids every day. And Greene's approach takes time and repetition before you see change. These are not "do this and it will go well" tools. They are "do this, and over weeks, the number of blowups and their size tends to come down." That is a real win in a Care and Treatment room. Smaller and fewer, not zero.

And a small room has advantages a big room does not. You have another adult. You have a youth worker partner from John Howard. You know every kid's patterns. That means you can split roles. One adult stays with the class and keeps things normal, the other one goes to the kid. Agree on that ahead of time with your partner, so in the moment no one has to decide.

Section four in a line. In a small room, the biggest wins come before the blowup: greet at the door, keep it predictable, and solve the trigger with the kid at a calm time.

How it lands in your week

Let's bring this into your actual week.

In your classroom: you already have most of the pieces. The desk sheets are your predictability. Your restorative training is your progressive discipline. Your partner is your second adult. The piece that might be missing is a shared plan for the moment it starts. Who goes to the kid. Who stays with the class. What the cue word is. Where the kid can go to cool down. When you call the office. If that is not written down somewhere, it is worth fifteen minutes with your partner.

With admin: when you have a student whose pattern is getting worse, send a short, factual heads up early, and ask whether there is a safety plan you should see. That is also a good moment for one of those thank you cards you want to write more often. When the principal or a support worker actually helps you through a hard stretch with a student, a short handwritten card goes a long way. It also builds the kind of relationship that makes the next ask easier.

In your faith: there is a real overlap here. The stage two rule, avoid the power struggle, is close to something Proverbs says plainly. A soft answer turns away wrath. Not a weak answer. A soft one. Holding a firm limit in a gentle voice is harder than yelling. It takes more self control, not less.

One thing to try

Here is the one thing to try. Only one.

Next week, greet every student at the door by name, every morning, for five school days. Eye contact, their name, one friendly word, and a pointer to the desk sheet. While you do it, ask yourself one silent question for each kid: what stage is this kid in right now? If one comes in at stage one, do your supportive move right there at the door, before he sits down.

At the end of the week, notice whether the mornings felt any different. Do not expect a miracle. Just notice.

Closing recap

Three sentences to close.

It is not true that teachers can do nothing: you cannot suspend, but you own most of the progressive discipline ladder, and in your room suspension is often the weakest tool anyway. In the moment, name the stage and match it, and avoid the public power struggle. Over time, document the facts so the system can see the pattern, ask for the safety plan, and put most of your energy into the greeting, the predictable start, and solving triggers with kids when they are calm.

I'll leave you with a line from Alistair Begg, Truth For Life, from a devotional called Words That Help. Here it is. "It takes far more self-control to respond in gentleness than to give way to unbridled passion and anger."
