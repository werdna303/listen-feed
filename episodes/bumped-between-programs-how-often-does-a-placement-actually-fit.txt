Bumped Between Programs: How Often Does a Placement Actually Fit?

Cold open

Picture a student walking into your room for the first time. He has a file that is thicker than it should be for someone his age. Three schools. Maybe four. A section program, then a regular classroom, then a stretch at home, then you. Everyone who signed off on the move meant well. And everyone, if you are honest, was also a little relieved to see him go.

You give him a clean start. You give him a lot of grace. For a week or two it works. Then he starts pushing. He takes the grace and he leans on it. And somewhere in the back of your head a quiet voice says, here we go again. This is the part where the grace runs out, and he gets bumped to the next place.

That is the feeling you named. Principals and service administrators bumping difficult kids from program to program because it is just so hard. Grace that seems to expire. And the question underneath it: how rare is it that a kid actually finds a great fit?

Here is the promise for this episode. By the end, you will know roughly how often these moves work, what the research says predicts the ones that do, why the grace seems to expire, and one thing you can do so that your students are the ones who land.

Section one: How often does a move actually work?

Here's the first idea. The honest answer is that most moves do not stick, and the research on this is thinner than it should be.

Let me start with the best data I could find. In England they have a formal version of what you are describing. It is called a managed move. A student who is close to being expelled gets moved to another school on a trial basis instead. If it works, he joins the new school for good. If it does not, he goes back.

The Education Policy Institute, a research group in London, looked at this a few years ago. They asked local authorities for their numbers. Only a minority could even give them, which tells you something already. Nobody was really keeping track. But among the areas that did report, roughly three in five managed moves ended with the student going back to the school he came from. So only about two in five stuck. Two out of five.

And when the move failed, what happened next was not good. Areas reported more moves, permanent exclusions, students being pulled out to home education, and students sent to alternative provision. In other words, the failed move was often the first step down a staircase.

The same report found something even sharper. Students who had at least one unexplained move between schools were about ten times as likely to finish secondary school in an alternative setting, and about six times as likely to be permanently excluded, compared with students who never had that kind of move. Ten times. Six times.

Now, a careful word here. That does not prove the move caused the bad ending. The students who get moved were already struggling. But it does tell you this: being moved is a strong signal that a student is on a hard road, and the move by itself is not reliably fixing it.

There is older research from the United States on school changes in general. Researchers followed about a thousand low income children in Chicago from kindergarten to grade seven. Students who changed schools often ended up about a year behind their classmates in reading and math. About half of that gap was already there before they started moving. But the other half, around six months, was linked to the moving itself. The authors' conclusion was that frequent moves, not a single move, put kids most at risk.

So let me say the first idea again in one line. Most moves do not stick, maybe two in five do, and every extra move costs a kid something.

That matches your gut. You said it feels rare to find a great fit. The data says it is not rare in the sense of one in a hundred. But it is less likely than not. And it gets harder each time.

Section two: What predicts a good fit?

Here's the second idea. When a placement does work, it is almost never because the kid suddenly changed. It is because a few specific things were in place on the receiving end.

Two researchers in England, Tamzin Messeter and Anita Soni, did a systematic review of the research on managed moves. They gathered what students, parents and staff said about moves that worked and moves that did not. They were honest that there are not many studies. But three things kept coming up.

The first was relationships. Not one relationship. New relationships with staff and with other students, so the kid felt he belonged in the new place. Belonging was the word that kept appearing.

The second was communication. The sending school, the receiving school, the family and the student all talking to each other. When people talked, the move had a chance. When the file just got passed along, it usually did not.

The third was a personal plan for that specific child. Not the general school rules applied to him. A plan built around what he needs, with someone checking on it.

The Education Policy Institute report I mentioned also listed what other studies pointed to. Strong relationships. Home and school talking. Schools talking to each other. Flexible behaviour policies. Peer support. And here is the surprising part. When they tested the written rules that local areas had for managed moves, things like whether parents could appeal or whether success was monitored, none of those written rules made a measurable difference. The paperwork did not predict the outcome.

Let that sink in for a second. The policy on paper did not seem to matter much. What seemed to matter was what the people in the building actually did.

So far we've got two things. One, most moves do not stick. Two, the ones that stick have belonging, people talking, and a plan for that one kid. Notice that all three of those are things that happen in a room like yours, not in an office.

Let me make this concrete. Think of one of your students who has been bumped around. When he arrived, did anyone from his last placement actually talk to you? Not send a file. Talk. If not, that is one of the three supports missing from day one. And it is not your fault. But it is something you can partly fix, which we will get to.

Section three: Why does grace seem to expire?

Here's the third idea, and it is the one I think you most need. Grace does not really expire. What changes is how we read the second mistake.

Let me tell you about a study from Stanford. Two psychologists, Jason Okonofua and Jennifer Eberhardt, called it Two Strikes. They gave real teachers school records describing a student who misbehaved. Then they showed a second record of a second time the same student misbehaved. The researchers changed only the name on the file, to suggest a different race.

After the first incident, teachers reacted about the same no matter which name was on the file. After the second incident, things shifted. Teachers became more troubled. They were more likely to see the student as a troublemaker. They were more likely to see the behaviour as a pattern. And they were more likely to imagine suspending the student in the future. In that study the shift was much stronger for students who seemed to be Black, which is its own important finding about bias. But here is the piece I want you to hold on to for this episode. The jump happened at strike two.

That is what grace expiring feels like from the inside. The first time, you see a kid having a hard day. The second time, a story starts to form. This is who he is. This is a pattern. And once that story forms, every small thing after it gets read through the story.

Now let's look at the same moment from the kid's side. There is a writer in the youth care field named Thom Garfat. He wrote about what staff call the honeymoon period, when a young person arrives at a new placement and things go well at first, and then they fall apart. Garfat says the honeymoon may be more myth than reality. He suggests calling it an exploration period instead.

His point is that the calm at the start is partly mutual caution. The staff are tiptoeing. The kid is still learning the rules, who is safe, and what the risks are. Then, once things feel a little bit safe, someone pushes past the surface to see if the relationship will hold. That is the testing stage. And for a kid who has been hurt before, testing is not manipulation. It is a question. Will you still be here when I show you the real me?

Put those two pieces together and you see the trap. The kid's testing usually shows up right around the second or third incident. And that is exactly when the adult's story flips from hard day to this is who he is. The kid asks, will you stay? And the adult's brain, right on schedule, starts answering no.

So grace does not expire because the kid took advantage. It often looks like it expires because the moment the kid starts testing is the same moment our minds start labeling. That is the collision.

Let me say the third idea again in one line. Grace seems to expire at strike two, because that is when the kid starts testing and when the adult starts labeling.

There is something freeing in that. It means the kid pushing back after a good start is not proof the placement failed. It may be proof the placement is starting to matter to him.

Halfway recap

Let's stop and gather what we have so far.

First, most moves do not stick. Maybe two in five. And each move costs the kid something, both in learning and in the story the next school reads about him.

Second, the moves that work have three things on the receiving end. Belonging, people actually talking, and a plan for that one kid. The written policy matters much less than what the adults in the building do.

Third, grace seems to expire at strike two. That is when a kid starts testing whether this place is safe, and it is also when adults start reading behaviour as a pattern.

Now for the last idea, which is the hopeful one.

Section four: The teacher's mindset changes the outcome

Here's the fourth idea. The research says that how the teacher thinks about discipline can change how many kids get pushed out, and it does not take a huge program to do it.

The same researcher from the Two Strikes study, Jason Okonofua, went on to run an experiment with two colleagues, David Paunesku and Greg Walton. They worked with middle school teachers in five schools. Some teachers got a short online exercise that encouraged what they called an empathic mindset about discipline. The idea was that students' misbehaviour usually has reasons, that relationships with teachers matter a great deal to students, and that a good teacher keeps the relationship even while correcting behaviour. The other teachers got a control exercise about something else.

The result was striking. Over the school year, the suspension rate for students of the teachers who did the empathy exercise was about half that of the other group. Roughly ten percent dropped to roughly five percent. Cut in half, from one short exercise about how to think about discipline.

Notice what that exercise did not do. It did not tell teachers to lower their standards. It did not tell them to stop correcting behaviour. It changed the story in the teacher's head about why the kid was acting that way. And when the story changed, fewer kids got pushed out.

There is a second study that fits with this. It is from a group led by David Yeager. Teachers marked student essays, and then one group of students got a short note clipped to the feedback. The note said, in essence, I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them. Researchers call this wise feedback. It pairs high standards with belief in the student.

Among Black students in that study, about two in three who got the wise note chose to revise their essay, compared with about one in four who got a plain note. And among the students who already distrusted school the most, the effect was even bigger. Most of them revised with the wise note, and essentially none of them did without it.

That is a kid who expects to be written off, hearing in one sentence that he is not being written off. And he responds.

One more. A team at the University of Virginia led by Anne Gregory coached high school teachers over two years to improve how they connected with students and how they ran their classes. Teachers in the coaching program ended up with no real gap between Black students and their classmates in discipline referrals, while the other teachers still had one. And the piece that seemed to drive it was the teachers getting better at engaging students in real thinking. Kids who were challenged and engaged got sent out less.

So the fourth idea in one line. The teacher's story about the kid, plus high standards with real belief, changes how many kids get pushed out.

So far we've got four things. Most moves fail. The ones that work have belonging, talking and a plan. Grace seems to expire at strike two. And the teacher's mindset can cut the push out rate in half.

How this lands in your week

Let's bring it home to your actual week.

In your classroom, you have students who have already been bumped. Some of them have been bumped more than once. Every one of them walked in carrying a story that someone else wrote about them. And every one of them is, at some point, going to test you.

So here is what that looks like on a normal Tuesday. A student has had a good couple of weeks. Then he blows up over something small. He swears, or walks out, or refuses work. That is strike two, or three. And you will feel the shift in yourself. You will feel the story starting to form. Here we go. This is who he is.

That moment, right there, is the whole episode. The research says that moment is where placements are won or lost. Not in the intake meeting. Not in the paperwork. In the teacher's head on the day of strike two.

Here is what would work in that moment. Correcting the behaviour clearly and calmly, and in the same breath making it obvious the relationship is not on the line. Something like, that is not okay, and you are still welcome here tomorrow. Then, later, a short private conversation where you are curious about why, not just about what.

Here is what seems like it would work but probably would not. Giving even more grace, as in letting it slide. That can feel kind, but the kid may read it as you being afraid of him, or not caring enough to hold the line. Testing kids are often checking whether there is a floor. Letting it slide removes the floor. Grace is not the same as no consequence. Grace is the consequence plus the welcome back.

And here is what definitely would not work. Treating strike two as proof. Saying, even to yourself, I knew this would happen. Or, I gave you a chance and you blew it. That sentence is exactly the one the kid has heard at every placement before yours. If you say it, you become one more stop on his staircase.

What to definitely say: you are still welcome here. I expect more from you because I think you can do it.

What to definitely not say: I gave you a chance. Or anything that sounds like the clock has run out.

Now, on the bigger system piece. You are right that administrators sometimes move kids along because it is hard. The research backs you up that those moves often fail. But here is the thing you can influence. The two in five that work have people talking. When a student is coming to you, or leaving you, you can be the person who picks up the phone. You can ask the last teacher what worked, not just what went wrong. And when one of your students moves on, you can call the next teacher and tell them what worked with you. That is one of the three things the research says predicts a good fit, and it costs you a ten minute call.

There is also a faith piece here, and you said you wanted that brought in. The whole idea of grace that expires is foreign to how God treats us. We tested him far more than strike two. And he did not write the story of who we are based on our worst week. That does not mean we never correct. Scripture is full of correction. But it means the welcome back is never what is on the line. That is the thing you can offer a kid who has never had it.

One of your operating principles fits here, the one about pausing before a reaction rather than making the spontaneous move. In chess terms, strike two is the moment your opponent makes a move that looks aggressive, and the instinct is to react right away. The better move is to stop and ask, is there a better response than the obvious one? Here, the obvious move is to label. The better move is to correct and welcome back.

One thing to try

Here is the one thing to try. Only one.

Pick one student in your room who has been moved around the most. Write a short note, one or two sentences, and give it to him this week. Not after a bad day. On a normal day. Something close to the wise feedback note. I have high expectations for you here, and I know you can meet them. You belong in this room.

Then, the next time he has a strike two moment, and he will, remember you wrote that note. Correct the behaviour. And then say some version of the note again out loud. You are still welcome here.

That is it. One note now, so that the welcome is already on record before the testing starts.

It also fits that habit you want to build of writing more notes and cards. This is just the student version of it.

Closing recap

Here it is in three sentences.

Most moves between programs do not stick, maybe two in five do, and the ones that work have belonging, people talking, and a plan for that one kid.

Grace seems to expire at strike two because that is when a kid starts testing whether he is safe, and when adults start labeling him as a pattern.

A teacher who corrects clearly while keeping the welcome, and who pairs high standards with real belief, can cut the number of kids pushed out roughly in half, and that is how your students become the ones who land.
