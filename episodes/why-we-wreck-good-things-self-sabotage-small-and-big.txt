Why We Wreck Good Things: Self-Sabotage, Small and Big

Part one. The question.

A few days ago you said something out loud. You said, "Why do I like self sabotage so bad." And then you asked the bigger version. Why do people with a good life throw it away with one stupid decision?

You know both versions. The small one looks like this. You have a free hour after work. You know the one thing that would actually move your life forward. And instead you tidy a drawer, check your phone, answer a text that could wait, and then the hour is gone. Nothing bad happened. Nothing good happened either.

The big one you've seen in the news, and maybe closer to home. A pastor with a healthy church and a family who loves him. A man with a steady job, a good marriage, kids who look up to him. And then one decision, and it all comes apart. People ask, "What was he thinking?"

That's the question for today. What is actually going on in someone's mind?

Here's the promise for this episode. Self-sabotage is not one mystery. It's a few ordinary forces in the mind, and they can be spotted early, while the move is still small.

We'll take four ideas, one at a time. First, protecting yourself from a verdict. Second, the pull of right now. Third, why a good life can feel unsafe. Fourth, what the Bible says about desire. Then we'll land it in your week, and I'll give you one thing to try.

Part two. The first idea. Protecting yourself from a verdict.

Let's start with a strange study from the late 1970s. Two psychologists, Steven Berglas and Edward Jones, brought college students into a lab. The students did a test of problem solving. Here's the trick. For some of the students, the problems were mostly unsolvable. They had to guess. But afterward, the researchers told them they did great.

So picture that student. They've just been told they're brilliant, and they know, deep down, they don't know why. They got lucky and they can feel it.

Then the researchers said there would be a second test. And before it, the students could choose a drug. One drug, they were told, would help their performance. The other drug, they were told, would hurt it. The drugs weren't real in the way the students thought. That wasn't the point. The point was which one they'd pick.

Here's what happened. The students who had been praised for success they didn't understand were more likely to choose the drug that would hurt them. Especially the men in the study.

Why would anyone do that? Think about it from the inside. If I take the drug that hurts me, and I do badly, I have an excuse. It wasn't me. It was the drug. And if I somehow do well anyway, I look even more impressive. Either way, my picture of myself is safe.

The researchers called this self-handicapping. It means putting an obstacle in your own way before a performance, so that if you fail, the failure doesn't count against you.

And once you know the name, you see it everywhere. The student who doesn't study the night before the test, and tells everyone he didn't study. The person who stays up too late before a big interview. The teacher who doesn't quite finish preparing the lesson that matters most, so if it flops, well, it was thrown together.

Here's the heart of it. The thing you're protecting is not your result. It's your verdict on yourself. If I try my hardest and fail, that says something about me. If I half try and fail, it only says something about my effort. And effort feels like something I can always fix later.

This is why the most important tasks get avoided the most. Low-stakes things are easy. You'll mow a lawn. You'll seal a deck. But the email that could change your career, or the hard conversation with someone you care about, or the project that would show what you're really capable of, those carry a verdict. And part of you would rather not hear the verdict.

So that's the first idea, in one line. Sometimes we sabotage the important thing because a clean failure would hurt more than a messy one.

Part three. The second idea. The pull of right now.

Here's the second idea. It's simpler, and it explains a lot of the small daily stuff.

Economists and psychologists have a term called present bias. It means we give extra weight to what we feel right now, compared to what we'll feel later. Not a little extra weight. A lot. The reward that's here, in this minute, looks huge. The reward that's a week away looks small and blurry.

A researcher named Hal Hershfield has done some fascinating work on this. He put people in a brain scanner and asked them to think about themselves now, about themselves ten years from now, and about a stranger. For many people, thinking about their future self looked more like thinking about a stranger than thinking about themselves.

Sit with that for a second. When you put something off, you're often handing the problem to someone your brain treats like a stranger. Of course it feels fine. It's not you who has to deal with it. It's that other guy.

In another set of studies, Hershfield showed people a picture of their own face, aged to look much older. People who saw their older face put more money aside for retirement. The stranger became a person they cared about.

Now add one more piece. Two researchers who study procrastination, Fuschia Sirois and Tim Pychyl, made an argument that changed how a lot of people think about it. They said procrastination isn't mainly a time management problem. It's a mood problem. When a task makes us feel anxious, bored, or unsure, avoiding it makes that bad feeling go away. Right now. The relief is instant. The cost shows up later, and later is that stranger's problem.

So the small sabotage often goes like this. A hard task shows up. It brings a little cloud of bad feeling. You do something else, and the cloud lifts. Your brain learns, "That worked." And next time, the cloud lifts even faster when you dodge.

That's why "just try harder" doesn't work very well. You're not lazy. You're being very effective at feeling better in the next five minutes.

So, the second idea in one line. We trade the future for the present because the present feels real and the future feels like someone else.

Part four. Halfway recap.

Let's stop and take stock. So far we've got two things.

One. Self-handicapping. We put obstacles in our own way so that if we fail, it doesn't say anything about who we are. The important task gets avoided because it carries a verdict.

Two. Present bias. The feeling right now outweighs the payoff later, and avoiding a hard task is a quick way to feel better. Your future self feels like a stranger, so you hand him the bill.

Those two explain most of the small, daily sabotage. The drawer gets tidied. The real task waits.

But they don't fully explain the big one. The man with the good life who throws it away. For that we need two more ideas. One from psychology, and one from Scripture.

Part five. The third idea. Why a good life can feel unsafe.

Here's the third idea. And it's the one that surprises people most.

Most of us assume everyone wants to be seen in a good light. And mostly that's true. But a psychologist named William Swann spent his career studying something underneath that. He called it self-verification.

The idea is this. People want to be known as they see themselves. Not just liked. Known. Because if the world matches your picture of yourself, life feels predictable. You know where you stand. And predictable feels safe, even when the picture is a bad one.

Swann's research found something striking. People who held poor views of themselves often preferred feedback that confirmed those poor views. In some studies, people with negative self-views were more drawn to partners and roommates who saw them negatively too. Not because they enjoyed it. Because it made sense to them. It fit.

Now think about what that means for a good life.

Picture a man who, deep down, believes he's a fraud. Maybe he doesn't say it. Maybe he doesn't even know he believes it. But underneath, there's a voice that says, "This isn't really you. You don't deserve this. Sooner or later they'll find out."

And then his life goes well. Good marriage. Good job. People respect him. On the outside, that's a gift. On the inside, it creates a strange tension. The life doesn't match the picture. And the tension is uncomfortable. He's waiting for the floor to drop.

Sometimes, without ever deciding to, a person relieves that tension by dropping the floor himself. The big stupid decision isn't random. In a twisted way it resolves the story. "See? I knew it. This is who I really am." There's an awful kind of relief in it. The waiting is over.

Researchers who study happiness across cultures have even found a thing called fear of happiness. Some people genuinely believe that when things are going too well, something bad must be coming. So joy feels risky. And people who feel joy is risky will sometimes pull back from it, or quietly undermine it, before it can be taken from them.

There's an older line of research too. Roy Baumeister, one of the big names in this field, reviewed the studies on self-defeating behavior back in the late 1980s. One of his conclusions matters for us. In normal people, real self-destruction, where someone actually wants to be hurt, is rare. What's common is something else. People make a trade. They grab a short-term benefit, like relief, or escape, or a feeling of control, and they don't look hard at the long-term cost. Or they use a strategy that they think will help them, and it backfires.

So put those together. Almost nobody wakes up and says, "Today I'll ruin my life." What they say is, "I just need some relief." Or, "I need to feel in control of something." Or, "I need to feel wanted." The decision feels like a solution. It's only later that it looks like a bomb.

And there's one more pattern that makes the big ones bigger. Psychologists who study dieting found it decades ago, and people now call it the "what the hell" effect. If someone on a strict diet slips and eats something they shouldn't, they often don't stop there. They think, "Well, the day is ruined anyway," and eat far more. One small break turns into a landslide.

That's how a lot of life-wrecking choices actually unfold. Not one giant leap. A small step over a line. Then the thought, "Well, I've already crossed it." And then a much bigger step.

So, the third idea in one line. A good life can feel unsafe when it doesn't match who you secretly believe you are, and wrecking it can feel like relief.

Part six. The fourth idea. What Scripture says about desire.

Here's the fourth idea. Psychology can describe the machinery. But it can't fully explain the heart. For that we go to Scripture.

The very first story of self-sabotage is in the garden. Adam and Eve had a good life. Not a good-ish life. A perfect one. Every tree in the garden was theirs, except one. And the serpent's whole pitch was this: God is holding out on you. What you have isn't enough. The one thing you don't have is the thing you need.

Notice that. The first sin was a person with a good life throwing it away with one decision. That's not a modern problem. That's the oldest human problem there is.

Then there's King David. Second Samuel, chapter eleven. It opens in spring, the time when kings go out to battle. And David stays home. He's not where he's supposed to be. He's restless on a rooftop in the evening. And from that small drift comes adultery, a cover-up, and a murder. A man after God's own heart, a king with everything, and it starts with simply not being where he was meant to be.

That's the "what the hell" effect in the Bible. Each step is a little bigger than the last, because the line has already been crossed.

James describes the process almost like a psychologist would. James chapter one. Each person is tempted when they are dragged away by their own evil desire and enticed. Then, after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin. And sin, when it is full grown, gives birth to death.

Look at the stages. Desire. Enticement. Conception. Birth. Full grown. It's a growth process. It doesn't start as death. It starts small. Which is good news, because it means there's an early stage where you can catch it.

Paul knows the inside of this too. Romans chapter seven. "I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do I do not do, but what I hate I do." That's your capture from a few days ago. "Why do I like self sabotage so bad." Paul asked the same thing, and he was an apostle.

And Augustine, in his Confessions, tells a story from when he was a teenager. He and some friends stole pears from a neighbour's tree. They weren't hungry. The pears weren't even good. They threw most of them to the pigs. And Augustine spends pages on it, because he can't let it go. Why did I do that? His answer was uncomfortable. Part of him loved the wrongness itself. He wanted to be his own law, just for a moment.

So here's what Scripture adds to the psychology. The psychology says we're protecting ourselves, chasing relief, and resolving a story that doesn't fit. Scripture says yes, and underneath that, the human heart has a bent. It wants to be its own god. It believes God is holding out. And it reaches for what it wants, even when what it has is good.

But Scripture also says something psychology can't. That bad picture of yourself, the voice that says "This isn't really you, you don't deserve this"? The gospel actually agrees with half of it. You don't deserve it. None of us do. That's what grace means.

And then it removes the fear completely. Because a good life given by grace isn't something you have to keep earning, so it isn't something you have to wait to lose. The floor isn't going to drop because you're found out. You've already been found out, at the cross, and you were loved anyway. When your identity rests there, a good life stops feeling like a setup. It becomes a gift you can receive without bracing.

So the fourth idea in one line. Self-sabotage grows from desire in stages, and grace takes away the fear that makes a good life feel unsafe.

Part seven. How this lands in your week.

Let's bring it home. Where does this actually show up for you?

First, the after-work hours. You know the pattern. Kids are gone, the room is quiet, and you've got a window. The thing that matters most usually has some kind of verdict attached. A message to someone in leadership. A step toward the permanent contract. A hard part of a project. And the thing that's easiest to do is something with no verdict at all. That's self-handicapping and present bias working together. It's not a character flaw that you can't fix. It's a pattern you can see coming.

Second, the classroom. Some of the students you work with are masters of self-handicapping. They won't try the worksheet. They put their head down. They say, "This is stupid." Now you have a new way to see it. For a kid who believes he's not smart, a real attempt is dangerous. If he tries and fails, it proves the story. If he refuses, the story stays a maybe. That's the Berglas and Jones study, sitting at a desk. What usually helps is making the stakes small. A first question he can't fail. Effort praised before results. What usually doesn't help is telling him how capable he is, if he doesn't believe it. Remember the study. Praise that doesn't fit someone's picture can make them more likely to sabotage, not less.

Third, home and marriage. The big wrecks almost always start with a small drift, like David on the rooftop. Being where you shouldn't be. A conversation that runs a little too long. A habit you'd rather your wife didn't see. None of these feel like a big decision. That's the danger. The big decision is made in the small ones.

Fourth, money and buying. Present bias loves a purchase. The relief of buying the thing is immediate. The cost is the stranger's problem. And sometimes a purchase is just a way to feel better about a task you're avoiding.

And fifth, your faith. Your own sense that you can't be trusted with a good thing. If you notice that feeling, you can bring it to God by name. "I feel like this is too good, and I'm waiting for it to fall apart." That's a prayer the Psalms would recognize.

Part eight. One thing to try.

Here's the one thing. Only one.

You've said before that in chess you sometimes make a move quickly without asking whether there's a better one. And you've noticed you do the same in life. That's exactly where self-sabotage lives. In the fast move.

Good chess players have a habit called a blunder check. Before they let go of the piece, they pause for a moment and ask, "What does this move allow? Is there a better one?" It takes a few seconds. It catches most disasters.

So try a life blunder check. And make it specific, because research by Peter Gollwitzer shows that plans written as "When this happens, I will do that" work much better than general good intentions. He calls them implementation intentions.

Here's yours. "When I notice I'm about to switch to something easier, I'll stop and ask: what am I protecting myself from, and is there a better move?"

That's it. Two questions. What am I protecting myself from? Is there a better move?

The first question catches the self-handicapping. The answer is usually a verdict. "I'm protecting myself from finding out this email won't work." Just naming it takes away a lot of its power.

The second question catches the present bias. It puts your future self back in the room. And it's the same question you're already trying to build at the chessboard, so you're training one habit in two places.

You won't catch it every time. That's fine. James says sin grows in stages. If you catch it at the desire stage even once a day, you're catching it before it conceives. And the more you catch the small ones, the less likely the big one ever has room to grow.

Part nine. Closing recap.

Let's finish in three sentences.

We sabotage the important things to protect ourselves from a verdict, and we trade our future for relief right now because our future self feels like a stranger.

The big life-wrecking choices usually grow in stages from small ones, and they can feel like relief when a good life doesn't match who we secretly think we are.

Grace removes that fear, and a simple blunder check, "What am I protecting myself from, and is there a better move?", catches the move while it's still small.

As Alistair Begg put it in a Truth For Life devotional on James chapter one: "Every sin is an inside job."
