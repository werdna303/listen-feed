Self-Sabotage, Part 4: Walking Back Into What Failed Before

Cold open.

Picture a moment you know well. You are standing at the edge of something you have tried before. Maybe it is a way of talking to a student that blew up last month. Maybe it is a plan for the evening that has never once worked on a weeknight. Maybe it is a chess opening that keeps getting you into the same lost position by move twelve. And a small voice says, this time it might go differently. So you walk in. And it goes the way it went last time.

Afterward, there is a second voice. It says something like, what is wrong with me? Is this just a dumber version of me showing up?

Here is the promise of this episode in one sentence. Walking back into what failed before is not a low intelligence problem; it is a predictable pattern that very smart people fall into, it has names and research behind it, and there are two simple tools that catch it before you make the move.

We will go through four ideas. First, false hope syndrome. Second, sunk cost and escalation of commitment. Third, the old Freudian idea of repetition compulsion, and what the research actually supports. Fourth, why smart people do this, and why that question about IQ is the wrong question. Then we will land it in your week, give you one thing to try, and close with a word about a kind of hope that is not false.

Settle into your pace. Let's go.

Section one: false hope syndrome.

Here is the first idea. Sometimes we repeat a failed attempt because the act of trying again feels good, even when the result never comes.

Two researchers at the University of Toronto, Janet Polivy and Peter Herman, spent years studying people who try to change themselves. Mostly they studied dieting, but the pattern reaches far past food. They gave it a name: false hope syndrome. Their main paper on it came out around the year 2002, with the title "If at first you don't succeed: false hopes of self-change."

Here is what they noticed. People set out to change something. They tend to expect too much, too fast, too easily. They expect the change to fix more of life than it can. And here is the key part. The moment of deciding to change feels wonderful. You feel hopeful. You feel in control. You feel like a new person, before you have done a single thing differently.

Then reality shows up. Progress is slower than expected. The old pattern comes back. And the person has to explain the failure to themselves. The study found people usually explain it in a way that keeps the hope alive. They say, I just did not try hard enough. Or, I picked the wrong method. Or, the timing was bad. Notice what all of those have in common. None of them says, my expectations were off. None of them says, this approach does not work for me. So the person is free to try again. Same goal. Same size of expectation. Often the same method. And the cycle turns.

Polivy and Herman were careful about one thing, and it matters for you. Trying again is not the problem. Persistence is good. The problem is trying again without learning anything from the last attempt. They called the hope false not because change is impossible, but because the plan never got adjusted. The hope was resting on a rerun.

Let me give you a picture from your own world. Think about a student who has had a hard morning. You have a move you reach for. Maybe you go over, crouch down, and try to reason it out in the moment. It did not work last Tuesday. It did not work the week before. But today, you think, today he might be in a better place. You walk over. And here is the subtle part. Part of why you walk over is that walking over feels like doing something. It feels like care. It feels like action. That feeling is real, and it is not bad. But the feeling of trying can quietly replace the question of whether this is the right try.

So here is the first idea in one line. False hope is the good feeling of starting again, without the hard work of changing the plan.

Section two: sunk cost and escalation of commitment.

Here is the second idea. Sometimes we walk back into what failed because we have already put so much into it.

You probably know the phrase sunk cost. It means money, time or effort that is already spent and cannot be gotten back. The rational move is to ignore it. What matters is what happens next, not what you already paid. But people do not ignore it. We feel the weight of what we already spent, and it pushes us forward.

A well known study from 1985, by Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer, used a simple story. Imagine you bought a ski trip to one place for about a hundred dollars. Then you bought a cheaper ski trip, about fifty dollars, to a different place, and you think you will enjoy that one more. Then you find out the two trips are on the same weekend, and you cannot get your money back on either. Which do you go on? The rational answer is obvious. Both are paid for. Go on the one you will enjoy more. But in the study, more than half the people picked the more expensive trip. The one they expected to enjoy less. Because it cost more, and walking away from it felt like wasting it.

Now here is the stronger version. It is called escalation of commitment, and the person most tied to it is Barry Staw. In the 1970s he ran a study where business students played the role of a company executive. They decided where to put research money, and then they found out their choice had gone badly. Then they got a second chance to spend money. The students who had made the first bad call themselves put more money into that failing area than students who were handed the same situation from someone else.

Read that again in your head. The people who were personally responsible for the bad decision doubled down harder. Why? Because pulling out would mean admitting the first decision was wrong. Staying in lets you keep hoping it was right all along. You are not just protecting money. You are protecting your story about yourself.

This shows up everywhere in ordinary life. A home project you have already sunk three Saturdays into, using a method that keeps not working. A plan for a class routine you built and printed and announced, that is not landing. A way of doing your evenings that you have defended to yourself for months. The more you have put in, and the more the original idea was yours, the harder it is to say, this is not working, I am changing course.

So here is the second idea in one line. The more you have invested, and the more it was your idea, the stronger the pull to keep going, even when the evidence says stop.

Halfway recap.

Let's stop and gather what we have so far. Two ideas.

One. False hope syndrome. Starting again feels good, and that good feeling can stand in for actually changing the plan. We explain failures in ways that keep the hope alive, like I did not try hard enough, instead of facing that the approach does not work.

Two. Sunk cost and escalation of commitment. What we have already spent pulls us forward. And when the first choice was ours, we double down to protect our story about being right.

Neither of those has anything to do with being slow or dim. Hold onto that. We are about to see why.

Section three: repetition compulsion, and what the research actually supports.

Here is the third idea. There is an old, dramatic explanation for this pattern, and there is a newer, plainer one that the evidence supports better.

The dramatic one comes from Sigmund Freud. In a book from 1920 called Beyond the Pleasure Principle, Freud described what he called repetition compulsion. He noticed patients who kept recreating painful situations. Someone who keeps picking the same kind of hurtful relationship. Someone who keeps ending up betrayed by friends. Freud wondered if there was a deep drive in people to repeat painful experiences, maybe to master them, maybe for darker reasons.

It is a striking idea. It shows up in a lot of pop psychology. You hear people say, you are just repeating your childhood. And there is something true in the observation. People really do repeat patterns. That part is real.

But here is the honest part. The idea of a hidden drive pushing you toward pain has never been well supported by careful research. It is very hard to test. It explains everything after the fact and predicts very little ahead of time. Most researchers today do not treat it as an established finding.

So what does the research support instead? Mostly two plain things.

The first is habit. Wendy Wood, a psychologist who has studied habits for a long time, has shown that a large share of what we do each day happens in the same places, at the same times, in response to the same cues. When the same situation shows up, the same response fires, often before you have consciously decided anything. That is not a dark drive. It is your brain saving effort. It is efficient most of the time. It just does not check whether the response worked last time.

The second is familiarity. People tend to like and trust what is familiar. A known approach, even one that failed, feels safer than an unknown one. The unknown one carries a risk of looking foolish, or of a new kind of failure you cannot predict. The known failure at least comes with a map. So we pick the familiar move, and we tell ourselves maybe it will work this time.

Here is where this ties to something you already know about yourself. You have said you tend to make quick moves in chess without asking whether there is a better one, and that you do the same in life. That is exactly this. It is not a compulsion toward failure. It is a fast, familiar response firing before the slower question gets a turn. The slower question is simple. Is there a better move here? And especially, have I played this move before, and how did it go?

So here is the third idea in one line. You are not driven to repeat failure by some dark force; you are running a familiar habit before your slower mind gets to ask if it worked last time.

Section four: why smart people do this.

Here is the fourth idea, and it answers your question directly. Is this just a lower IQ version of me? The answer is no, and there is research on exactly this.

Keith Stanovich and Richard West have spent years studying how intelligence relates to thinking errors. In one well known paper from 2008, they tested a range of classic thinking biases, and they looked at whether people with higher cognitive ability avoided them more. For a lot of the biases, including sunk cost style choices, the answer was barely, or not at all. Smart people fell into them about as much as anyone.

Stanovich has a word for this. He calls it dysrationalia. It means being intelligent and still not thinking well in a particular moment. His point is that intelligence and rational thinking are not the same skill. Intelligence is how much mental horsepower you have. Rational thinking is whether you stop and use it. A powerful engine does not help if you never take your foot off the gas long enough to steer.

In fact, there are ways being smart can make this worse. A smart person is better at building a convincing reason why this time is different. You are better at the explanation that keeps the hope alive. I did not try hard enough. The timing was off. The student was having an unusual day. Those can all be true. A sharp mind can produce them fast and make them sound good.

So here is the reframe. When you walk back into what failed, it is not a sign that a dumber version of you took over. It is a sign that the fast system ran and the slow system did not get a turn. That is a skill gap, not an intelligence gap. Skills can be trained. And the two tools that train it are surprisingly simple.

Here is the fourth idea in one line. This pattern is about whether you pause, not about how smart you are, and smart minds are often better at talking themselves back in.

How to catch it before the move.

Now let's get practical. Two tools. Both have real research behind them. Both take less than a minute.

The first tool is the pre-mortem. Gary Klein, a psychologist who studies how experts make decisions under pressure, popularized it. You know what a post-mortem is: after something fails, you figure out why. A pre-mortem flips it. Before you act, you imagine that it is already over, and it failed. Then you ask, what went wrong?

Why does that work better than just asking what could go wrong? An older study from 1989, by Deborah Mitchell, Jay Russo and Nancy Pennington, found that when people imagined an event had already happened, they came up with more reasons for it than when they only imagined it might happen. Klein often cites it as about thirty percent more reasons. The research calls it prospective hindsight. Your mind is much better at explaining a past event than predicting a future one. So you trick it. You pretend the failure is already in the past, and suddenly the reasons come.

For your exact pattern, the pre-mortem has a built-in shortcut. You often already have a real post-mortem. It already failed last time. So the pre-mortem question becomes, it is an hour from now and this went exactly like last time. What happened? You will usually know the answer in three seconds. That three seconds is the slow system getting its turn.

The second tool is the if-then plan. Peter Gollwitzer, a psychologist in Germany and New York, calls these implementation intentions. Instead of a vague goal like, I will stop repeating failed moves, you write a specific link. If this situation happens, then I will do this. A large review of the research, by Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran in 2006, pulled together close to a hundred studies, and if-then plans helped people reach their goals noticeably better than goals alone. It is one of the more solid findings in behaviour change.

Why does it work? Because it uses the same machinery that causes the problem. Habits fire on cues. An if-then plan builds a new cue. You are installing a different fast response, so you are not relying on willpower in the hot moment.

Here is what one might sound like for you. If I notice I am about to do something I have tried before, then I will ask, how did it go last time? That is it. Short. Linked to a cue. The cue is the feeling of familiarity itself. The flicker of, oh, I know this move.

Now, what would not work. A few things seem like they would help but usually do not. Telling yourself, I need to be smarter about this. That aims at the wrong thing, since intelligence is not the gap. Trying harder at the same approach. That is the false hope cycle itself. And beating yourself up afterward. Shame tends to make people avoid looking at the failure closely, which means they learn less from it, which sets up the next rerun. What works is calm, specific, and before the move.

How it lands in your week.

Let's bring this into your actual week.

In the classroom. You have moves you reach for with students when things go sideways. Some of them work. Some keep not working with a particular student. Before you walk over, the question is, how did this go last time with this student? If the answer is badly, then the move is not to try it harder. It is to try something different, even something small. Step back instead of stepping in. Give space instead of words. Talk to a colleague before talking to the student. The bar is not a brilliant new plan. The bar is just not the rerun.

At home. Think of a recurring evening pattern that keeps not working. A plan to get a big project done on a weeknight after a full teaching day. You have tried it. It does not happen. False hope says, tonight I will push through. A pre-mortem says, it is nine thirty and I did not do it, why? You already know. Tired, hungry, and the task was too big. So you shrink it, or you move it to a Saturday morning, or you drop it. Each of those is a real change. Trying again unchanged is not.

With money and projects. When something has eaten your time or money and is not paying off, notice the sunk cost voice. I have already put so much into this. The useful question is the one Staw's research points to. If I were starting fresh today, knowing what I know now, would I start this? If the answer is no, the past investment is not a reason to keep going.

And in your faith. This pattern has a spiritual cousin. Many of us make the same resolution before God again and again. This time I will be more patient. This time I will pray every morning. And the hope rests on a better version of ourselves showing up. That is false hope in its purest form, because it rests on us.

The gospel angle: hope that is not false.

Let's stay there for a moment, because this is where the whole episode turns.

False hope syndrome is hope that rests on the self. I will be different this time. I will try harder. I will finally get it right. The research says that kind of hope tends to fail on repeat, because it never changes what it rests on.

The apostle Paul knew this cycle from the inside. In Romans chapter seven he writes, more or less, I do not do the good I want, and the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing. That is a man walking back into what failed before. Paul does not answer it by saying, try harder. He says, who will deliver me? And then, thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord.

The Christian answer to the repeat cycle is not a stronger resolution. It is a different place for hope to stand. Romans chapter five says hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts. That is a hope that does not depend on whether you got it right this time.

And this matters practically, not just spiritually. Remember what makes people repeat failures. Part of it is protecting a story about being right. Part of it is shame, which keeps us from looking closely at what went wrong. The gospel takes the pressure off both. You do not need to defend your record, because your standing does not rest on your record. So you are free to look honestly at the last attempt and say, that did not work, I was wrong, I will change course. Repentance, in that sense, is the opposite of false hope. False hope says, I will try again the same way. Repentance says, I was wrong, and I am turning.

Think of Peter. He boasted that he would never deny Jesus. Then he did, three times. After the resurrection, Jesus does not hand him a better plan to try harder. He asks him three times, do you love me? And then gives him work to do. Peter's hope after that is not in Peter. It is in the one who restored him.

That is the hope that is not false. It is not, this time I will be different. It is, he is faithful, so I am free to change.

One thing to try.

Here is the one thing. Only one.

Write this if-then plan on a card or in your notes, and say it out loud once a day this week. If I notice I am about to do something I have tried before, then I will ask, how did it go last time?

That is the whole thing. You are not trying to fix every pattern. You are installing one pause, tied to one cue: the flicker of familiarity. When the answer to the question is, it went badly, you do not have to know the perfect alternative. You just have to not do the rerun. Even a small change counts.

Closing recap.

Three sentences to take with you.

Walking back into what failed before is driven by the good feeling of starting again, by what you have already invested, and by fast familiar habits, not by low intelligence. Smart people do it about as much as anyone and are often better at talking themselves into it. The fix is a pause before the move, a quick pre-mortem or an if-then plan, resting on a hope that does not depend on you getting it right this time.

Alistair Begg put it this way, in a Truth For Life devotional on a living hope: "Our hope no longer hinges on ourselves or our circumstances."
