Part one. The default is the decision.

A default is simply what happens if you do nothing. If you don't fill out the form. If you don't click the button. If you don't make the call. Every system has one. The question is only whether you picked it, or someone else did.

Here's the research that made economists sit up. In the late nineteen nineties, a large American company changed one thing about its workplace retirement plan. In the United States, that plan is called a four oh one k. It's a lot like a group R R S P through work. You put in part of each paycheque, it grows for retirement, and the company often adds a bit on top.

Before the change, new employees had to sign up. If they did nothing, they weren't saving. After the change, new employees were signed up automatically. If they did nothing, they were saving. They could leave any time with one form. Same plan. Same choices. Same people, more or less. The only thing that changed was which way the door swung.

Two economists, Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea, studied what happened. They published it in two thousand and one, under a great title. The Power of Suggestion.

What did they find? Among newer workers, participation jumped from under four in ten to more than eight in ten. Let that sink in. Same people. Same money. Same plan. And roughly twice as many of them were saving, just because saving became the thing that happened when you did nothing.

But the study found a second thing, and it's almost more interesting. The company had to pick a default contribution rate for the people it signed up automatically. It picked a low one, three percent of pay. It also had to pick a default fund for the money to sit in. And most of the people who were enrolled automatically just stayed there. At the default rate. In the default fund. Even when they could have chosen more. Even when the company would have added more if they did.

The researchers called this inertia. People didn't stay put because they had studied the options and decided three percent was perfect. They stayed because it was the setting already on the dial. Some may even have taken it as advice, as if the company was saying, this is the right amount.

So the default worked in two directions at once. It got far more people saving. And it also held many of them at a lower rate than they might have picked on their own. The default didn't just open a door. It set the furniture in the room.

Now let's bring that to you.

You already live inside one of the most powerful savings defaults in the country. The Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan. You didn't apply for it the way you'd apply for a credit card. When you started teaching and your board began taking pension contributions from your pay, you became a member automatically. And the government and employers match what members put in. That's the default doing heavy lifting on your behalf, every two weeks, whether you're paying attention or not.

Think about how different that is from the T F S A in your banking app. With the pension, the default is saving. With the T F S A, the default is not saving. You have to make a fresh decision every single month, usually at the exact moment there are other good things to spend the money on.

That's the whole first idea. Willpower asks you to win the same fight over and over. A default lets you win it once.

So here's part one in a line. The default is the decision, so you might as well be the one who makes it.

Part two. Why we stick.

Now you might be thinking, fine, but surely smart people override a bad default. Surely if the default is wrong, a thoughtful person changes it.

Here's the uncomfortable answer. Mostly, they don't.

Back in nineteen eighty eight, two economists, William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser, gave this habit a name. They called it status quo bias. It's our strong tendency to stick with the current state of things, simply because it is the current state of things.

They ran experiments where people made the same choice, but with different starting points. When an option was framed as the way things already were, people chose it far more often. Then they looked at real decisions. One of the most telling was at Harvard, where faculty were picking health plans and retirement options. When a new health plan came along, people who were brand new tended to pick it. But people already enrolled in an older plan, even ones of the same age and situation, mostly just stayed where they were. Same people, same options. The only difference was where they were standing when the choice arrived.

Why does this happen? The study pointed to a few reasons, and I'll give you three.

First, losses feel bigger than gains. Switching means giving up something you have, and that stings more than the thing you'd gain feels good.

Second, regret. If you change something and it goes badly, it feels like your fault. If you leave it alone and it goes badly, well, that's just how it was.

Third, plain effort. Changing a setting means a form, a login, a phone call, a decision. Doing nothing costs nothing today.

Here's the most vivid example of all of this, and it's not about money. It's about organ donation.

In two thousand and three, Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared countries in Europe. In some countries, you're not an organ donor unless you sign up. That's opt in. In other countries, you are a donor unless you say no. That's opt out. People in these countries are not wildly different in their values about generosity or medicine or death. Some of them are next door neighbours with similar cultures.

But the rates of people agreeing to be donors were wildly different. In the opt out countries, nearly everyone was counted as willing. In the opt in countries, far fewer were. The gap was at least sixty percentage points.

One fair caution here. That gap is about how many people are on the list as consenting. It's not the same as the number of transplants that actually happen, which depends on a lot of other things in a health system. But as a picture of how defaults steer people, it's hard to beat. One of the weightiest choices a person can make about their own body, and the biggest factor was which box was already ticked.

The researchers also ran a simple online experiment. They asked people to imagine moving to a new state and choosing whether to be a donor. Same question, different starting box. When people had to opt out, they agreed far more often than when they had to opt in.

So if a default can steer something as serious as organ donation, it can certainly steer whether your three hundred dollars goes into savings or into Saturday.

And here's the thing I want you to take from this. Status quo bias isn't a character flaw. It's not laziness, exactly. It's how a busy mind protects its limited energy. You make hundreds of decisions a day. You've got students who need you, a wife you love, a house to run. Your brain is right to save its strength. The trick is not to fight the bias. The trick is to put it on your side.

Part three. Save more tomorrow.

Here's a problem with automatic saving. Even if you set it up, you might set it too low. Just like those workers at three percent. Because saving more today feels like taking a pay cut today. And nobody likes a pay cut.

Two behavioural economists, Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi, came up with an answer. They called it Save More Tomorrow. They published the results in two thousand and four.

The idea is simple, and a little bit sneaky in a good way. You don't ask people to save more now. You ask them to agree, today, to save more later. Specifically, every time they get a raise, a slice of that raise goes into savings automatically.

Think about why that works. It handles three human problems at once.

First, it's easier to promise something for the future than to do it now. Future you is generous. Present you is tired and has a car payment. So the study asked people to commit before the pain arrived.

Second, it never feels like a loss. Your take home pay doesn't go down. It just goes up a bit less than it would have. You never see the money you don't miss.

Third, and this is the beautiful part, it uses status quo bias as a tool. Once you've said yes, the increases keep happening automatically. To stop them, you'd have to take action. And as we just learned, people mostly don't. Inertia, the very thing that held workers at three percent, now carries them upward.

What happened? In the first company that tried it, most of the people offered the plan joined. Close to eight in ten. And among those who joined, their average saving rate went from about three and a half percent of pay to more than thirteen percent, in a little over three years. That's close to four times as much. Not because they became more disciplined people. Because the default kept climbing for them.

A fair caution again. That big jump is for the people who chose to join. The point isn't that every workplace sees those exact numbers. The point is the design. Commit future raises, not present pay. Let inertia climb the ladder for you.

Now, how does this fit you? Your teacher pension already takes its share of your pay. That's handled. But think about your own personal saving outside the pension. The T F S A. Maybe an R R S P. Maybe one day an R E S P, a registered education savings plan, if children come along.

You know roughly when your pay changes. You move up the salary grid with experience. There are negotiated increases. There's the year you finish an extra qualification. Each one of those is a raise you can see coming.

So here's a Save More Tomorrow move for your own life. Decide now, before the next increase lands, that some portion of it goes straight to savings. Not all of it. Some. Half, maybe. When the raise arrives, you bump your automatic transfer up by that amount the same week. Your take home still goes up. You still feel the raise. But your saving climbs with it, every time, without a fresh act of willpower.

And think about what usually happens to raises. You don't even notice them, honestly. Your spending quietly rises to fill the space. A slightly nicer this, a slightly more frequent that. That's a default too. It's just one nobody chose. Save More Tomorrow is simply choosing a better one before the money ever arrives.

Part three in a line. Promise your future raises to saving before they arrive, and let inertia do the climbing.

Part four. The defaults working against you.

Now the flip side. Because if defaults are this powerful, somebody else has noticed too.

Every company that sells you a subscription understands status quo bias perfectly. The streaming service. The app with the free trial. The gym membership. The cloud storage. The magazine you read once. The software that renews every year on a date you'll never remember.

Here's how it works. The default is that it continues. You have to act to stop it. And because stopping takes a login, a menu buried three screens deep, maybe a phone call where someone offers you a discount to stay, you put it off. Next month. After the weekend. And it renews again.

That free trial that turns into a paid plan? That's a default. The insurance policy that auto renews at a higher price? A default. The phone plan you signed up for years ago that now costs more than the new customer price? A default. None of these companies are forcing you. They're just letting your inertia do their selling.

And notice, it's the same psychology we've been talking about. Losses feel bigger than gains, so cancelling feels like losing something. Regret, because what if you need it next month? And effort, because who wants to fight a cancellation page at nine at night after report cards?

The amounts are usually small. That's the design. Ten dollars here. Fifteen dollars there. Small enough that no single one is worth the trouble to cancel. But add up several of them over a year and you can easily be looking at hundreds of dollars. Money flowing out automatically, every month, to things you'd never choose again if someone asked you fresh today.

So here's a way to think about it. Every automatic payment in your life is a vote. It's a little standing instruction about where your money goes when you're not paying attention. Some of those votes are wise. Your pension. Your mortgage. A regular gift to your church. Some of those votes you cast years ago and forgot. They're still being counted.

Here's the good news. You can use the same tool on both sides. Make good things automatic. Make wasteful things deliberate. If something is worth keeping, let it renew. If you're not sure, make it something you have to choose again. Some people put a reminder in their calendar a few days before any free trial ends or any annual renewal comes up. That way the default stops being invisible. You get a moment to decide on purpose.

Start with home money. The phrase people use for all this is pay yourself first. It's an old idea, but now you know why it works. It's not a slogan about discipline. It's a slogan about defaults.

Paying yourself first means the saving happens first, automatically, on payday. Before the groceries. Before the takeout. Before the birthday gift. Your bank lets you set up a pre authorized transfer. You pick an amount and a date. Ideally the same day your pay lands, or the day after. The money moves from chequing into your tax free savings account, or your R R S P, or whatever savings account you've chosen, before you've had a chance to need it for anything else.

Then you live on what's left. And here's the surprise most people find. You adjust. Fairly quickly. The same way you've adjusted to never seeing your pension contribution. You don't miss money you never had in your hand.

I won't tell you what to buy inside those accounts. That's a different conversation, and you've got your portfolio check episodes for thinking about investments. This episode is just about the behaviour. Get the money moving on its own. What it's invested in is the next question, not this one.

Next, your marriage. Defaults matter even more when two people share money. Because every month, without a system, someone has to bring it up. Should we save this month? How much? Is now a good time? And money conversations, even in a good marriage, can carry tension. One person feels like the nag. The other feels like the spender.

A default takes the pressure off. You and your wife sit down once, maybe over coffee on a quiet Saturday, and decide together. How much goes to giving. How much goes to saving. Which day it moves. Then you set it up and let the system carry it. You're not having the same conversation every payday. You're having one good conversation, and then honouring it together.

That also fits beautifully with planning for kids someday. If children are in your future, there are going to be new costs and probably some months with less income. A savings habit that already runs on its own is a gift to that future family. You're not trying to start a discipline in the middle of sleepless nights. It's already running. You set it up in a quieter season so it would carry you through a busier one.

And there's a faith piece here that's worth naming. Stewardship isn't only about how much we give or save. It's about being faithful in the ordinary, day after day. A good default is a way of making faithfulness the easy path instead of the heroic one. You decide once, with care and with prayer, what wisdom and generosity look like for your household. Then you let that decision quietly carry out, week after week, even on the weeks when you're tired. Scripture talks about setting something aside regularly. A pre authorized transfer is a very modern way of keeping a very old pattern.
