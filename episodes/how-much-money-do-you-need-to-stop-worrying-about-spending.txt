Part one. More income doesn't automatically mean less worry.

Let's start with the big question people have studied for decades. Does more money make you happier?

In two thousand ten, two very famous researchers, Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton, published a study on this using a huge survey of Americans. They found that people's day to day emotional well being rose with income, but only up to a point. Around seventy five thousand American dollars a year, at the time, it seemed to level off. Above that, more money didn't make people's everyday moods much better. That number got quoted everywhere.

Then in two thousand twenty one, a younger researcher named Matthew Killingsworth published a study that seemed to contradict it. He had people report how they felt at random moments through the day, using an app. And he found that well being kept rising with income, well past that old number. No plateau.

So what did these researchers do? Something rare and wonderful in science. Kahneman and Killingsworth teamed up, with a third researcher, Barbara Mellers, as a referee, to figure out who was right. They published the result in two thousand twenty three. And the answer turned out to be, both, sort of.

For most people, happiness keeps going up as income goes up. But for the least happy group of people, it does level off. Past a certain income, more money stopped helping them. Their unhappiness was coming from somewhere money couldn't reach. Grief, loneliness, illness, conflict, worry.

Now, why does this matter for you and the winter tires? Because it tells you something important. Money helps. It's not nothing. But some kinds of unhappiness, and I'd include some kinds of money worry, don't go away just because the number gets bigger. You probably know people who earn far more than a teacher and still feel anxious every time they buy something. And you probably know people who earn less and feel relaxed.

So the honest answer to the question, how much money do you need to stop worrying, is that there probably isn't a single income number that does it. If the worry has roots somewhere else, it'll follow you up the pay scale.

There's a practical reason for that, too, and you'll recognize it from the open loops series. Remember Parkinson's law? Work expands to fill the time available. Money has its own version. Spending tends to expand to fill the income available. When people get a raise, the truck gets a little nicer, the groceries get a little fancier, the subscriptions creep in. Within a year or two, the new income feels just as tight as the old one did. Economists talk about people adapting to a new level of comfort, so it stops feeling like extra and starts feeling like normal. So if your worry is tied to how tight things feel, a raise often buys you only a short break before the tightness comes back. The gap between what comes in and what goes out matters more than the size of what comes in.

But that's not the end of the story. Because there are specific kinds of money, and specific habits, that research suggests really do calm people down. Let's look at them.

One line for part one. More income tends to help, but past a point, the worry often comes from somewhere a bigger paycheque won't reach.

Part two. The cushion matters more than the paycheque.

Here's the first thing that seems to help. Not income. Cash. Money sitting in the bank, ready to use.

In two thousand sixteen, three researchers, Peter Ruberton, Joe Gladstone and Sonja Lyubomirsky, got something unusual. Real bank data. They worked with a bank in the United Kingdom and matched hundreds of customers' actual account balances with surveys about how satisfied they were with their lives.

And what did the study find? People with more money sitting in their chequing and savings accounts felt better about their finances, and that in turn went with higher life satisfaction. And that held up even after the researchers accounted for income, investments, debt and spending. In other words, it wasn't just being rich. It was having money you could get at.

And here's a detail I find really encouraging. The benefit levelled off. One of the authors put it simply in an interview. The first thousand dollars mattered more than the next nine thousand. That's not a precise law. But it fits what you'd expect. Going from nothing to something in the bank makes a big difference to how you feel. Going from a lot to a lot more makes less.

Now an honest note. That study shows a connection, not proof of cause and effect. It could be that people who are calmer about money are also better at saving it. But the pattern fits with other research about what financial stress does to us.

Here's one more study that explains why a cushion matters so much. In two thousand thirteen, a team including Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir published a study in the journal Science about what money worries do to your thinking. In one experiment, they asked shoppers at a mall to think about a car repair. For some, the repair was a small bill. For others, it was a big one, over a thousand dollars. Then everybody took tests of reasoning and problem solving.

For the small repair, people with lower and higher incomes did about the same. But for the big repair, people with lower incomes did noticeably worse on the tests. Just thinking about a bill they couldn't easily cover seemed to tie up their mental bandwidth. Some researchers have questioned how large that effect really is. But the idea is easy to recognize. A worry about money doesn't stay in its box. It leaks into everything else.

Now connect that to your Saturday. Part of what's going on when you hover over the tires is that your brain is running the big repair scenario. What if the transmission goes next month? What if something happens? Without a clear cushion, every purchase feels like it might be taking money from a future emergency. So every purchase gets a little jolt of fear.

That's the first number you need. A cushion. Money set aside, in a separate place, for emergencies. Canadian financial agencies commonly suggest working toward about three to six months of your regular expenses. With a steady job like teaching, you might lean toward the lower end. With kids, a mortgage and an old truck, maybe more. You and your wife can decide. But having a target, and knowing when you've hit it, changes how every other purchase feels. Because now the emergency money isn't the same money as the tires money.

And if three months sounds impossibly far away, remember the bank study. The first thousand mattered more than the next nine. So don't wait until you can do it all. Start with a first goal you can see. One thousand dollars, sitting in its own account, not touched. Set up a small automatic transfer every payday, even fifty dollars. Throw in Marketplace money when you sell something. When you hit the first thousand, notice how it feels. Then set the next target. You're building the cushion in pieces, the same way you'd build anything in your shop.

One line for part two. A cash cushion seems to calm people more than a bigger paycheque, so the first number is your emergency fund, kept separate.

Part three. Decide once, and the rest is free.

Now here's the thing that I think matters most for someone like you. The worry over the tires isn't really about whether you have four hundred dollars. It's that you haven't decided whether you're allowed to spend it. And so you have to decide it fresh, right there, with your coffee, every single time.

Remember the open loops series? Part three was about deciding once, while you're fresh. In the moment, the question stays open, and an open question is a nagging question. The same thing happens with money. If you don't have a plan, every purchase is a little open loop. Should I? Can I? Is this okay? And because you're a careful, responsible person, the answer is always a nervous, maybe.

So the fix isn't more money. It's a decision made ahead of time. Here's the simplest version, and lots of financial writers describe something like it. Each payday, money goes out in a set order. First, giving. Second, the fixed bills, like the mortgage and the phone. Third, saving, including the cushion and anything else you've agreed on. And then whatever is left, you've already decided, is free to spend. Not, free to spend if you feel okay about it. Free. Guilt free. Already approved.

Now, you might say, isn't that just a budget? Sort of. But most budgets are built to make you spend less. This is built to let you spend without worry. Because once the important things are taken care of, first, automatically, the money that's left really is fine to spend. You don't have to check the balance on the tires. You check one thing. Is it in the free money? If yes, buy them. If no, it waits for next payday.

It helps to be clear about what the free money is for, so you're not arguing with yourself about categories. Free money is for the wants and the small stuff. A tool you'd like. A meal out. A gift. The used chess book. Truck tires might belong here, or might belong in a truck fund, which we'll get to. What it isn't for is the bills or the cushion. Those are already handled. And for anything really big, above an amount you and your wife agree on, a simple rule helps. Wait a day, and talk about it. Not because big purchases are bad. Because a big decision deserves a fresh head, not a Saturday morning impulse with a coffee in your hand.

There's a name in economics for something like this. Richard Thaler, who won a Nobel prize for his work on how people actually behave with money, described what he called mental accounting. People mentally sort money into different jars, the rent jar, the fun jar, the savings jar, even though money is all the same in theory. Economists usually describe that as a mistake. But you can use it on purpose. Put the free money in its own actual account if you can, so the jar is real. Then the balance in that account is your answer. You don't have to do math with the mortgage in your head on a Saturday morning.

You've probably met a version of this already, in the episode on defaults and paying yourself first. That was about saving automatically. This is the other side of it. When the saving happens first and automatically, the spending can happen without fear.

One line for part three. The worry comes from deciding every purchase fresh, so decide once, take care of giving, bills and saving first, and let what's left be truly free.

Part four. Enough.

Here's a strange thing about money worry. It's often not really about money. It's about security. About control. About the fear that something bad will happen and you won't be able to handle it. And no amount of money fully fixes that fear, because there's always a bigger thing that could go wrong.

The Bible is surprisingly practical here. There's a prayer in Proverbs chapter thirty that I think is one of the wisest things ever said about money. The writer asks God for two things. First, keep falsehood and lies far from me. And second, give me neither poverty nor riches, but feed me with the food that is needful for me. And then he explains why. Lest I be full and deny you and say, who is the Lord? Or lest I be poor and steal and profane the name of my God.

Do you hear that? Too little money has a danger. Too much money has a danger too. The prayer is for enough. Not the most. Enough.

And it's worth naming one thing you have that many people don't. As an Ontario teacher, you're part of a defined benefit pension plan. That's a big piece of future security that most workers today simply don't have. It doesn't mean you can ignore saving. But when the late night worry says, what about retirement, what if it all falls apart, it's fair to remind yourself that a lot of your long term cushion is already being built, every paycheque, whether you think about it or not. Some of the fear is about a cliff that isn't really there.

Paul says something similar in Philippians chapter four. I have learned, in whatever situation I am, to be content. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. Notice the word learned. Contentment isn't a personality type. It's something you learn, with practice.

Think about fuel on a long run. Too little, and you hit the wall. But carrying way more than you need doesn't make you faster. It just weighs you down and sloshes around. Experienced runners learn what enough looks like for them, and they carry that, and then they stop thinking about it and run. Money's a lot like that. The goal is to know your enough, carry it, and then get on with the run. And Paul says the secret is Christ, who strengthens him.

So what's the opposite of money worry, from a Christian point of view? It's not a bigger number. It's trust, expressed in wise habits. You plan carefully, you save a cushion, you give generously, and then you hold the rest with an open hand, trusting that God knows what you need.

And giving is part of how that works, practically. There's interesting research here too.
