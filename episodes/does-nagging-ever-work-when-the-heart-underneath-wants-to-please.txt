Let me give you the verdict up front, so you can listen for it. You are partly right. Persistent asking can produce real change. But the persistence is usually not what does the work. The relationship and the heart underneath do the work. Nagging often succeeds in spite of itself, not because of itself. And there's a cost that you may not be counting.

Let's walk through it in four ideas.

Section one. The case against nagging, stated fairly.

Here's the first idea. There is real research behind the saying that nagging doesn't work, and it's worth knowing exactly what it says, so you're arguing with the real thing and not a slogan.

Start with something called psychological reactance. A psychologist named Jack Brehm described it back in the 1960s. The idea is simple. People value their freedom to choose. When they feel that freedom is being threatened, they push to get it back. Sometimes they do the exact opposite of what was asked, just to prove they still can.

You've seen this a hundred times. You tell a student, you need to start this now. And the student, who was about to start, now doesn't. Not because they hate the work. Because now starting would feel like losing.

Later researchers tested the language itself. In health messages, for example, studies found that forceful, controlling wording, things like you must, you should, you need to, tends to raise people's resistance compared to softer, choice-giving wording. Same request. Different words. Different amount of pushback.

So that's the first piece of the case against nagging: pressure can trigger a push for freedom.

The second piece comes from marriage research. John Gottman studied couples in his lab for decades. One of his best known findings is about what he calls harsh startup. That's when a conversation about a problem begins with criticism, blame or sarcasm. Gottman reports that you can predict how a conversation will end, about ninety six percent of the time, from the first three minutes. Start harsh, and it almost always ends badly. Ninety six percent, from three minutes.

Now notice. Nagging, by the time it's the fourth or fifth ask, very often starts harsh. The tone has an edge. There's a sigh before it. "Again, can you please..." That edge is the startup, and it sets the course.

The third piece is what researchers call the demand withdraw pattern. Andrew Christensen, a psychologist at UCLA, identified it in the 1980s. One partner presses an issue. The other pulls back, avoids, goes quiet, or changes the subject. And here's the trap: the more one presses, the more the other withdraws, and the more the other withdraws, the more the first one presses. It's a loop. And the research is clear that the more often couples get stuck in that loop, the lower their satisfaction with the relationship.

So let me say the case against nagging in one line. Pressure can spark a fight for freedom, a harsh start predicts a bad ending, and pressing invites withdrawing, which invites more pressing.

That's real. That's not a slogan. Hold that.

Section two. Where you're right.

Here's the second idea. You noticed something true, and there's research that backs you up.

The research framework I want to bring in is called self determination theory. It comes from two psychologists, Edward Deci and Richard Ryan. It's one of the most tested theories of motivation in education. You'll have heard pieces of it: autonomy, competence, relatedness.

The part that matters for us is a process they call internalization. Internalization is how a rule that starts outside a person, someone else's expectation, slowly becomes their own. The theory says this is a normal, healthy process. Kids are not born valuing clean rooms or loaded dishwashers or finished math. They take those values in from people they're connected to.

And here's the key. The theory says internalization runs on relatedness. People take in the values of people they feel connected to, people they respect, people whose approval matters to them. It's not mainly about the rule. It's about the relationship carrying the rule.

That is exactly what you described. You said, I genuinely did want to please my mom. That's relatedness. You respected your parents. That's the soil the values grew in. So when you say nagging worked on me because underneath I wanted to please, the research would say, yes, the wanting to please is the engine. You've named the right thing.

And it's the same with your wife. You said, in her heart she is happy to do something, she actually wants to, she just pushes back. That's a person who values you and values the home you share. The request lands in soil that's ready for it. The pushback is on the surface. The willingness is underneath.

And here's another place you're right. The research on reactance shows that resistance is often a reaction to how something is asked, not a verdict on what is asked. So when someone pushes back hard and then comes around, it doesn't mean the pushing back was fake. It means the reaction to pressure and the real value can sit in the same person at the same time. Your fifteen year old self fought tooth and nail and also respected your mom. Both were true.

There's even a word you used that deserves credit. You said, an understanding of sin in their heart. In plain terms, you're saying people resist good things not only because they're being pushed badly, but because something in us resists being told, period. Scripture would agree with you. So the resistance isn't always proof the asking was wrong. Sometimes it's just the human heart.

So that's what you've got right, in one line. When the person already values you and values the thing, persistent asking can lead to real change, because the relationship carries the value inside, and some resistance is just the heart, not proof of bad asking.

Section three. Where you might be wrong.

Here's the third idea, and this is the honest part. There are two places I think your argument has a gap.

The first gap is about what exactly did the work.

Here's a question to think about on your run. When your mom's values finally took hold in you, was it the fourth reminder that did it? Or was it years of her love, her example, and your respect, slowly winning? I'd guess the second. The nagging was present when the change happened. That doesn't mean the nagging caused the change.

It's like a kid who finishes his worksheet after you've said start working five times. Did the fifth reminder do it? Or did the work get done because he likes you, the room feels safe, and he was going to get there once he'd saved face? If it's the second, the reminders weren't the engine. They were noise on top of the engine. And possibly they slowed the engine down.

So here's my fair verdict on gap one. Your examples show that change can happen while nagging is going on. They don't show nagging was the cause. The relationship is a better explanation.

The second gap is about the kind of change.

Self determination theory doesn't just say people internalize or don't. It says there are levels. Let me give you two of them, because the difference matters.

The first level is called introjected. That's when a person does the thing because they'd feel guilty or anxious or ashamed if they didn't. The rule is inside them, but it feels like a voice pushing on them. It's I have to, or I'll feel bad.

The deeper level is called identified. That's when a person does the thing because they actually see why it matters and agree with it. It's I want to, because this is who I am.

Here's why that matters. Research on parenting, including work by Avi Assor, Guy Roth and Edward Deci, looked at what happens when parents use their love and approval as leverage: more warmth when you comply, less when you don't. They found it can get compliance. Kids do the thing. But the kind of motivation it builds tends to be the guilt and pressure kind, the introjected kind. And it came with more resentment toward the parents and less wellbeing, compared with kids whose parents supported their choices.

So here's a question worth asking honestly. When you got in line with your parents' program, which kind was it? Some of it, I'd guess, is deeply yours now. You chose it. Some of it might still feel like a pressure voice. Most of us carry both.

And the same question for your wife and your students. A loaded dishwasher done because she gets why it matters to you, and she loves you, is one thing. A loaded dishwasher done to avoid the next reminder is another. Both look the same from the kitchen doorway. They are not the same inside the marriage.

So that's where you might be wrong, in one line. Change during nagging isn't proof nagging caused it, and the change nagging gets is often the guilt kind, not the I agree kind.

Section four. How to ask a heart that wants to please.

Here's the fourth idea. If the person underneath wants to please, your job is to make it easy for that heart to say yes without losing face.

There's a well known experiment from 1994 by Deci and colleagues. People were given a dull task, the kind of thing nobody would do for fun. Some got three things along with the instruction. First, a meaningful reason why the task mattered. Second, an acknowledgement that, yes, this is boring, I get that. Third, language that gave a sense of choice, rather than pressure words like should and must. People who got those three things took the value of the task in more deeply. They didn't just do it while someone watched. They kept caring about it.

So let's make that the method. Three moves. Give a reason. Name their feeling. Leave room for choice.

Let me say why each one works with a heart that wants to please.

Give a reason. A heart that wants to please needs to know what pleases you and why. "Load it the other way" is a command. "When the bowls go on the top rack they come out clean, and then I don't have to rewash them at night" is information. A willing person can act on information without feeling ordered.

Name their feeling. This takes the wind out of reactance. If you say, I know this is annoying, and I know I've mentioned it before, the person doesn't have to fight to show you it's annoying. You already said it. There's nothing left to defend.

Leave room for choice. Not fake choice. Real room. "Would you be up for trying it that way this week?" Or with a student, "Do you want to start with question one or question three?" The thing still gets done. But they get to be the one who chose it. And remember, the heart that wants to please wants to give, not be taken from. Let them give.

Now, a fourth thing, from Gottman. He calls it soft startup. Start the conversation gently. Talk about yourself and what you need, not about what's wrong with them. Something like, I feel a bit stressed when the counter is full at night, could we try something? Not, you always leave the dishes. One describes you. The other accuses them. Soft startup, remember, because the first three minutes predict the ending.

And one more thing that matters in a marriage. Gottman found that marriages do better when husbands accept influence from their wives. That means being persuadable yourself. If you want your wife to take your requests seriously, the fastest way is to visibly take hers seriously, and change because of them. It makes the house a place where asks go both ways. That's not a trick. That's just what a good marriage looks like.

So that's the fourth idea, in one line. Give a reason, name their feeling, leave room for choice, and start soft.

What not to do, and what seems like it would work but won't.

You asked me to include what doesn't work too, and the things that seem like they'd work but don't. So here are three.

First. Raising the volume each time. It seems logical. They didn't hear the soft version, so say it louder. But each time the edge gets sharper, you're feeding the demand withdraw loop. You press, they pull back, you press harder. Louder is the loop speeding up.

Second. The guilt line. Things like, after everything I do around here. Or, with students, I thought you were better than this. It seems like it would work, because it often does get compliance in the moment. But that's the introjected kind. It gets the dishwasher loaded and costs you warmth. And it teaches the person that pleasing you is about avoiding your disappointment, not sharing your values.

Third. Over-explaining. This one surprises people. A reason helps. Five reasons, delivered as a speech, start to sound like a lecture. And a lecture is pressure in a nicer coat. Give one good reason, then stop.

And what definitely to say? Short, warm, reasoned. "Hey, can I ask a small thing? When the bowls go up top they come out clean. Would you mind trying it?" And then, the hard part: say nothing else. Let it sit.
