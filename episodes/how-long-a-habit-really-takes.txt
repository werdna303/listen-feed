Part one. The myth of twenty one days.

You have heard it. Maybe you have even said it to a student. It takes twenty one days to form a habit. Do something for three weeks and it becomes automatic. It is on posters. It is in apps. It is in a lot of well-meaning sermons and staff meetings.

So where did it come from? It came from a plastic surgeon. His name was Maxwell Maltz. In 1960 he wrote a book called Psycho-Cybernetics. In that book, Maltz noticed something about his patients. After surgery on their faces, it took them a while to get used to their new appearance. He said it took a minimum of about twenty one days for an old mental image to dissolve and a new one to settle in.

Notice what he was talking about. He was not talking about habits. He was talking about self-image. He was talking about a person looking in the mirror and getting used to a new nose. He also noticed something similar with people who had lost a limb, how long the strange feeling of the missing limb would hang around.

And notice one more word. Minimum. He said a minimum of about twenty one days. Not twenty one days and you are done. At least twenty one days, and maybe more.

Over the years, that careful little observation got passed from book to book and speaker to speaker. Each time, it got a bit shorter and a bit neater. The word minimum fell off. The subject changed from self-image to habits. And by the end, it became a rule. Twenty one days to a new habit.

Why does this matter to you? Because the myth does real damage. Picture one of your students. He decides he will do his push-ups every morning, or show up on time, or stop vaping at lunch. He pushes hard for three weeks. And on day twenty two, it still feels like work. It is not automatic. So what does he conclude? Something is wrong with me. I failed. I am not a habit person.

But nothing was wrong with him. He was just measuring himself against a number that was never true. He was running a race with the finish line painted in the wrong place.

The same thing can happen to you. You start a new routine. Maybe it is a shop clean-up system in your construction class. Maybe it is a new prayer list. Three weeks in, it still takes effort, and you wonder if it is ever going to settle. The myth makes you think you are behind. You are not behind. You are right on schedule. You just had the wrong schedule.

So here is part one in a single line. Twenty one days was about getting used to a new face, it was a minimum, and it was never a rule for habits.

Part two. What the study actually found.

So if not twenty one days, then what? For a long time, nobody had really measured it. Then a health psychologist in London named Phillippa Lally and her colleagues decided to find out. Their study was published around 2009.

Here is what they did. They recruited about a hundred volunteers. Each person picked one new, simple, healthy behaviour. Something to eat, something to drink, or some kind of physical activity. And they picked a cue for it. A specific time or place in their day. Something small, like a glass of water with lunch, or a bit of exercise at a set point in the evening.

Then each day, for about twelve weeks, they did the behaviour. And each day they answered a few questions about how automatic it felt. Did I do it without thinking? Would it feel strange not to do it? Did I have to remind myself?

That is the key. The study was not asking, did you do it? It was asking, how automatic does it feel? Because that is what a habit really is. Not just something you do often. Something you do without having to decide.

So what did they find? Three things, and I want you to hold all three.

The first thing is the number. For the people whose data was clear enough to measure, the middle point was about sixty six days. Not three weeks. More like two months. Closer to nine or ten weeks.

The second thing is even more important than the number. The range was huge. Some people got there in under three weeks, around eighteen days. Others were still climbing at the end of the study, and the researchers estimated it could take some of them the better part of a year, around two hundred and fifty days. Simple behaviours tended to settle faster. Harder ones, like exercise, tended to take longer.

So sixty six is not a new magic number. If you replace twenty one with sixty six and treat it like a rule, you have only moved the painted line. The honest answer is this. It usually takes a couple of months, and it can take a lot longer, depending on the person and the behaviour.

The third thing is the one I think matters most for you and your students. Missing one day did not wreck it. In the study, when someone missed a single opportunity, it did not materially change their progress. They picked up the next day and kept climbing. What did seem to hurt was being very inconsistent over and over. A miss here and there was fine. A pattern of missing was not.

Let me also give you the shape of it, because the shape is encouraging. The climb was not a straight line. It was steep at the start and then it flattened out. Think of your heart rate training. When you first started running slow, at that low aerobic heart rate, the early gains came fairly quickly. Your pace at the same heart rate improved. Then it kept improving, but more slowly, and eventually it levelled off into a new normal.

Habits look a lot like that. The biggest jumps in automaticity come in the early days. Every repetition in the first few weeks adds a lot. Later repetitions add a little. And then, at some point, you reach a plateau. That plateau is the habit. It is where the behaviour just runs.

So, what does this mean in practice? It means that week three is not the finish line. Week three is the steep part of the hill. It means that if you are a month in and it still takes effort, you are not failing. You are climbing. And it means that if you miss a day, the best move is simply to show up the next day. Not to restart the count. Not to beat yourself up. Just to do the next one.

Think about how you would coach a student on this. One of your guys says he will bring his gym clothes every day for your phys-ed class. He forgets on a Wednesday. In the old way of thinking, he has broken the streak. He might as well give up. But the research says something kinder and truer. One miss barely moves the curve. What moves the curve is coming back on Thursday.

Here is part two in a single line. A habit usually takes around two months, sometimes much longer, it climbs fastest at the start, and one missed day does not undo it.

Halfway recap.

So far we have covered two ideas. First, the twenty one day rule is a myth. It came from a surgeon talking about how long it took patients to get used to their new faces, and even he called it a minimum. Second, the real study found it usually takes around two months for a simple daily behaviour to feel automatic, with a huge range from a few weeks to most of a year. The climb is steep early and flat later. And a single missed day does not undo the progress.

Now here is the question that leads us into the second half. If it takes that long, what actually drives the process? What is it that turns a repeated action into an automatic one? The answer is not willpower. The answer is the cue. Let's go there.

Part three. Habits live in places, not in willpower.

Go back to the study we just talked about. Remember that each person picked a behaviour and also a cue. A time or place. That was not a small detail. That was the engine.

A psychologist named Wendy Wood has spent much of her career studying this. In one of her early studies, she and her colleagues gave college students a watch that buzzed every hour. Each time it buzzed, the students wrote down what they were doing, where they were, and what they were thinking about.

What they found was striking. Roughly two in five of the things people did in a day, a bit over forty percent, were done almost every day in the same setting. Often while their mind was somewhere else. They were thinking about one thing while their hands did another. That is a habit at work.

Wood's big idea is this. A habit is not mainly a decision. It is a link between a context and an action. The context can be a place, a time of day, an object, the people around you, or the thing you just finished doing. Over time, through repetition, your brain stitches that context to the action. And then the context starts the action for you. You do not have to decide. The cue decides.

Think about your morning again. You do not wake up at five fifteen and think, should I have devotions today? Let me weigh the costs and benefits. No. You come down the stairs, the chair is there, the lamp is there, the Bible is there, the coffee is going. The context fires the routine. That is why it feels easy. It is not that you are more disciplined at five thirty than at any other time. It is that you have built a very strong link between that place, that hour, and that action.

Here is the flip side, and it is just as useful. When the context changes, habits break. Wood points out that when people move house, or start a new job, or their routine gets shaken up, old habits often stop firing. The cues are gone, so the automatic behaviour is gone too.

You have probably felt this. Think about a family vacation, or the first week of summer break. Your devotions can wobble. Not because you love God less in July. Because the chair is different, the hour is different, the kids are up at different times. The cues moved, and so the habit had to be carried by effort again.

Or think about your runs. When you run the same route, from the same door, at the same hour, you hardly think about it. Lacing up is just what happens after a certain point in the morning. But when you travel, or the weather pushes you onto a treadmill, or you try to run in the evening for a week, suddenly it is harder. Same body. Same goal. Different cues.

This is good news, not bad news. Because it means you do not have to try harder. You have to design better. If habits live in context, then the lever is the context.

Let me say that again, because it changes how you approach almost everything. The lever is not how much you want it. The lever is where and when it happens.

This also explains something about your students. Many of the kids in your program have lives with very few stable cues. Mornings look different every day. Who is home changes. When they eat changes. Sometimes where they sleep changes. So if they struggle to build habits, it is not always a character problem. Sometimes there just is not a stable enough context for a habit to attach to.

Which means your classroom might be one of the most stable contexts in their week. Same room. Same door. Same time. Same teacher. That is a real gift. You can use it.

Here is part three in a single line. Habits are links between a cue and an action, so the place and the moment matter more than the effort.

Part four. The if-then plan.

So if habits form by tying an action to a cue, how do you make the tie on purpose, right from day one? That is where a researcher named Peter Gollwitzer comes in. He studied something he called implementation intentions. That is a mouthful. The simple name for it is an if-then plan.

Here is how it works. Most of us set goals in a general way. I want to pray more. I want to read more. I want to run more. Those are good intentions, but they leave the hardest part undecided. When? Where? What exactly?

An if-then plan fills that in. It sounds like this. If it is this situation, then I will do this action. When I sit down at my desk at lunch, I will do three chess puzzles. When I finish my coffee, I will put on my running shoes. When a student walks through the door in the morning, I will hand them the desk sheet.

Notice what that does. It takes the cue, the context that Wendy Wood talks about, and it ties your action to it in advance. You are not waiting for the link to form slowly by accident. You are building it deliberately.

Gollwitzer and a colleague named Paschal Sheeran gathered together close to a hundred studies on this. They found that people who made if-then plans were noticeably more likely to follow through on their goals than people who only stated the goal. The effect was solid. They called it medium to large, which in this kind of research is a big deal for something that takes about ten seconds to do.

Why does it work? Here is the simple version. When you form an if-then plan, you are telling your brain what to watch for. The situation becomes a kind of tripwire. When it shows up, the action is already loaded. You do not have to stop and decide in the moment, which is exactly when you are tired, distracted, or tempted to skip it.

Let me give you an example from your own life. Suppose you want to add a short time of prayer for your students into your week. A general goal sounds like this. I should pray for my students more. It is sincere. And it will probably happen now and then.

An if-then plan sounds like this. When I unlock my classroom door in the morning, I will pray for the students who will sit in those desks today. Now there is a cue. The key in the lock. The click of the door. Every school day, that cue shows up. And every day it shows up, the prayer is waiting for it. After a couple of months of that, it may become one of those things you do without deciding.

One more important point. If-then plans also work for obstacles, not just starts. You can plan for the moment you usually slip. If it is raining in the morning, then I will run the indoor route. If I miss my devotions at five thirty because a child is sick, then I will read at lunch. That second kind of plan protects you on the days that would otherwise become two missed days, then three, then a broken pattern.

And remember the study from part two. One missed day does not hurt much. A string of them does. So a backup if-then plan is the thing that turns a miss into a single miss.

Here is part four in a single line. An if-then plan ties your action to a specific cue in advance, so the moment does the deciding instead of you.

Part five. How this lands in your week.

Let's bring all of this into your actual week. Three places. Your classroom. Your own mornings and running. And your home.

Start with the classroom.

You teach a group of students who are, by design, the ones the regular system was not working for. Many of them have been told, in words or without words, that they cannot stick to things. So here is something you can quietly teach them by how you run your room. Habits are not about being a certain kind of person. They are about cues and repetition and time.

Take the morning desk sheet. If you want it to become automatic, make the cue the same every day. Same spot on the desk, or same stack by the door, or you handing it to them as they walk in. Do not change the system every two weeks. Each change resets the cue. Hold the routine steady and then give it time. Not three weeks. More like two months. If it still feels like pushing in October, that is normal. You are on the steep part of the hill. By November, it may just be what the room does.

And when a student misses, which they will, do not make it a crisis. One miss does not undo the curve. Just get them back on it the next day. You might even tell them that. You might say, in your own words, missing one day does not wreck a habit, but coming back tomorrow matters a lot. For a kid who has a long history of feeling like one mistake ruins everything, that sentence can be freeing.

In your phys-ed course, use the same idea for warm-ups. Same start signal, same location, same first movement. Over weeks, the bell or the whistle becomes the cue, and bodies start moving without you having to sell it every day.

In your construction course, think about shop clean-up. Clean-up is a classic place where routines fall apart, because it happens at the end, when everyone is tired and thinking about leaving. So tie it to a clear cue. When the timer goes at ten minutes to the bell, then tools go back on the wall. Same timer, same sound, every day. The sound becomes the cue. And in time, the clean-up starts before you say a word.

You could also teach the if-then plan directly in Civics and Careers. When your students talk about goals for work, or for getting a licence, or for showing up to a co-op placement, have them write an if-then sentence. Not, I will be on time. But, when my alarm goes off, I will put my feet on the floor before I touch my phone. It is a simple, concrete life skill. It is also backed by real research, which some of your students may find surprisingly convincing.

Now your own mornings and running.

Your five thirty devotions are a working example of everything we have talked about. Same chair, same hour, same order. It is a strong habit because the context is strong. So protect the context. If something about the space changes, like a new chair, a moved lamp, or a phone that starts sitting next to your Bible, notice that it may weaken the cue. Small changes in context can make a strong habit wobble.

And when you want to add something new to that time, attach it to what is already there. Wood's research suggests that the thing you just finished doing can serve as a cue. So you might say, when I close my Bible, I will write one line of prayer for the day. The old habit becomes the cue for the new one.

Your running works the same way. Your heart rate training asks you to run slower than feels natural, and that takes discipline. But the habit of going out the door does not have to take discipline, if the cue is clear. Shoes in the same place. Watch charged in the same spot. Headphones by the door. Same route on most days. You have probably already set a lot of that up without naming it. Now you know why it works.

And your chess puzzles are a nice small test case. Puzzles are a simple behaviour, so they may settle into automaticity faster than something like running. If you want them to be a daily thing, give them a home. One fixed moment. Maybe after lunch, or after you cool down. Not whenever I have a minute. A specific when.

Now home.

You are married, and much of what makes a home work is shared habits. Who does what after dinner. When you talk. When you pray together, if you do. Here is the gentle point. If there is a shared routine you and your wife want, think about the cue, not just the intention. We should talk more is a good intention. When the dishes are done, we sit on the couch for ten minutes with no phones is a plan. It has a cue. It can become a habit.

And have patience with each other. If you start something new together, expect it to take a couple of months before it feels natural. If one of you forgets, that is not a sign it has failed. It is one missed day. Pick it up again tomorrow.

Here is part five in a single line. In your classroom, your mornings, and your home, the same rule holds: pick a steady cue, repeat it, give it a couple of months, and treat a miss as just a miss.

Part six. One thing to try.

I said only one, and I meant it. Here it is.

Pick one routine you want to build this term. It can be for you, or for your class. Then write one if-then sentence for it, using a cue that already happens every day.

That is it. Not three routines. Not a full system. One routine. One sentence. One cue that already exists.

For example. When I unlock my classroom door, I will pray for today's students. Or, when the timer goes at ten minutes to the bell, tools go back on the wall. Or, when I close my Bible, I will write one line of prayer for the day.

Then do three more things, which are really just ways of keeping that one thing going. Keep the cue the same every day. Expect it to take around two months, not three weeks. And if you miss a day, do it again the next day without making a big deal of it.

If you want to mark the timeline, put a small note on your calendar about nine or ten weeks from today. When that date comes, ask yourself one question. Does this happen now without me deciding? If yes, you have a new habit. If not yet, you are still climbing, and that is fine. Keep going.

Closing recap.

Here it is in three sentences. The twenty one day rule is a myth, and the real research says a habit usually takes around two months, sometimes much longer, and one missed day does not undo it. Habits live in cues and context, not in willpower, so the place and the moment do most of the work. And the simplest way to build that link on purpose is an if-then plan tied to something that already happens every day.

I will leave you with one short line from Alistair Begg, Truth For Life. He was writing about Ananias, an ordinary believer in Damascus, and he said this.

"Ananias's life challenges us to be faithful in seemingly small ways on apparently ordinary days."

That is what a habit is, in the end. Small things, done on ordinary days, again and again, until they become part of who you are.
