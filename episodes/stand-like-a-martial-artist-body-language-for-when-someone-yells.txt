Stand Like a Martial Artist, Body Language for Staying Steady When Someone Yells

COLD OPEN

Picture this. You are in the grocery store on a Saturday. Your wife is two aisles over. You reach for something on a shelf and a man behind you, a total stranger, decides you were in his way. He starts yelling. His face is red. He is closer than you would like. People are looking.

Now picture a different room. It is a Tuesday in your classroom. One of your students is upset. Maybe it is about the work, maybe it is about something that happened at home last night. Their voice is going up. They are watching you. Not your words. You. Your face, your shoulders, your hands.

In both of those rooms, before you say a single word, your body has already answered.

So here is the question you asked. Are there body language tips from martial arts that are powerful enough to just build into your life? And is there a book on this?

The short answer is yes, and yes. There is not one perfect book, but there are three good ones that fit together, and I will give them to you near the end.

THE PROMISE

Here is the promise of this episode in one sentence. By the end of this run, you will have four simple things, stance, spacing, hands and breath, that you can practise in calm moments so that they show up on their own when someone is yelling at you.

Four things. Stance, spacing, hands, breath. Let's go one at a time.

SECTION ONE: YOUR BODY ANSWERS FIRST

Here is the first big idea. In a tense moment, the other person reads your body before they hear your words. And your body tends to answer before you have decided anything.

Let me start with a man named Joe Navarro. He spent about twenty five years as an FBI agent, much of it in counterintelligence, which means his job was reading people who did not want to be read. He wrote a book called What Every BODY Is Saying. The word body in the title is in capital letters, which is the joke.

Navarro's main idea is simple. When we feel threatened, the oldest part of our brain takes over, and it has three moves. Freeze. Flight. Fight. In that order.

Freeze is the first one. Think of a deer in headlights. In people, it looks like going stiff, holding your breath, pulling your arms in, getting very still.

Flight is the second. In a classroom or a grocery store, you usually can't run, so flight shows up small. Leaning back. Turning your feet toward the door. Looking away. Putting something between you and the other person, like a cart, or a desk, or crossed arms.

Fight is the third. In daily life, it rarely means throwing a punch. It shows up as puffing up, squaring your chest, stepping in, pointing, raising your voice to match theirs.

Here is why this matters for you. Navarro says these moves are mostly automatic. They happen before your thinking brain catches up. So when an angry person sees you freeze, they read fear. When they see you puff up and square off, they read a challenge. Either way, you have given them something to push against.

You told me once that you think one of your students watches your body language right after insulting you, and that you can't help reacting differently. That is exactly this. The student is not reading your words. They are reading whether they landed a hit. And your body is telling them, before you can stop it.

Now here is where martial arts come in.

Martial artists train for this exact problem. In a fight, the old brain wants to freeze or flail. So they practise a calm, balanced way of standing and breathing, over and over, in easy moments, until it becomes the thing their body does when it gets scared. They are not trying to feel no fear. They are trying to give their fear a better default.

There is a Japanese word for the state they aim for. Zanshin. It roughly means a relaxed, alert awareness. Not tense. Not checked out. Awake, settled, and ready.

That is what you want in that grocery aisle and in your classroom. Not tough. Not soft. Settled.

So here is the first idea again in one line. Your body answers first, so train it in calm moments to give a settled answer.

SECTION TWO: STANCE AND SPACING

Here is the second idea. Where you put your feet and how far you stand from someone changes the whole conversation.

Let's start with stance.

Almost every martial art has some version of a ready stance. The details differ, but the bones are the same. Feet about shoulder width apart. One foot a little ahead of the other. Knees soft, not locked. Weight spread evenly, so nobody could tip you over with a light push. Shoulders down and loose.

Try to picture the opposite. Feet together, knees locked, weight on your heels. That is a body that can be knocked over. And people feel that, even if they can't name it.

A balanced stance does two things. First, it actually steadies you. When your body is stable, it sends a message back up to your brain that you are okay. Second, it tells the other person you are not going anywhere, and you are not coming at them either.

Now, a quick honest note about posture, because you have probably heard of power posing. A few years ago, a famous study said that standing like Superman for two minutes changed your hormones and made you take more risks. That got a lot of attention. Then bigger studies tried to repeat it, and the hormone part did not hold up. One large repeat, by a researcher named Eva Ranehill and her team, had about two hundred people and found no change in hormones or behaviour.

But here is the part people missed. That same study did find that people felt a bit more powerful after open postures. And later reviews suggest the real effect may be on the other side. Closing up, slumping, curling in on yourself, seems to make people feel worse. So the honest takeaway is not that you need a superhero pose. It is that you should avoid collapsing. Stand open and balanced, and you will not be making things harder on yourself.

Now, spacing.

In Japanese martial arts there is a word for the distance between two people. Ma ai. It is the gap that decides what can happen. Too close, and you can be grabbed or hit before you can react. Too far, and nothing can happen at all. Martial artists spend years learning to feel that distance without thinking.

In everyday life, you want a gap that says I am here with you, but I am not crowding you.

There is a training program used in a lot of schools and hospitals called Nonviolent Crisis Intervention, from a group called CPI. You may have had it, or something like it, through the board. They teach something they call the Supportive Stance. Two parts. First, give the person space, at least one and a half to three feet. Second, stand at a slight angle to them instead of head on.

Why the angle? Because standing square to someone, chest to chest, toes pointing at them, is the stance of a fight. It is how two people square up. When you turn a little to the side, you are still facing them, still listening, but your body is saying I am not here to fight you. And as a bonus, it is a more stable stance, the same one many martial arts use.

So in the grocery store, here is what that looks like. The man is yelling. You do not step toward him. You do not plant your feet square and stare. You take one small step back, or to the side, to open a bit more space. You turn slightly. You keep your feet balanced. And you make sure there is a clear path out, both for you and for him. An angry person who feels trapped gets more dangerous, not less.

In your classroom, it looks a little different. If a student is upset at their desk, standing over them is the head on, crowding version, even if you don't mean it that way. You are taller. You are the adult. Coming in from the side, a bit back, and sometimes getting down to their eye level, takes a lot of heat out of it. You are not looming. You are beside.

Here is what does not work, even though it feels like it should. Stepping in close to show you're not scared. Closing the distance to make your point land. Blocking a doorway so a kid can't walk out mid conversation. All of those feel like being firm. All of them make a frightened or angry person feel cornered.

So here is the second idea again in one line. Stand balanced and a little to the side, and give people room.

HALFWAY RECAP

Let's take a breath. We're about halfway.

So far we've got two things.

One. Your body answers before your words do. Under threat, the old brain freezes, flees or fights, and the other person reads that instantly. Martial artists train calm so it becomes their default.

Two. Stance and spacing. Feet balanced, knees soft, a slight angle instead of head on, and a gap of a few feet with a clear way out for both of you.

Two more to go. Hands, and breath.

SECTION THREE: OPEN HANDS, AND THE FENCE

Here is the third idea. Your hands are the loudest part of your body language, and in a tense moment they should be open, visible and calm.

Think about what hands say. A pointing finger says blame. Hands on hips says I'm in charge here. Crossed arms say I've already decided. Fists say danger. Hands in pockets can say you don't care, or it can make the other person nervous because they can't see what you're holding. And hands behind your back, which is a common teacher habit, can look either stern or hidden.

Navarro makes a related point. When people are stressed, they do small self soothing things with their hands. Rubbing the neck. Touching the face. Wringing their fingers. He calls them pacifiers. They are normal, and everyone does them. But in a standoff, they are a little flag that says I'm rattled.

So what should your hands do?

This is where a man named Geoff Thompson comes in. Thompson is British. He worked for years as a nightclub door man, which in Britain means a bouncer, and he is a high ranking black belt in karate and also holds a black belt in judo. He has stood in front of a lot of angry, drunk people. He wrote a whole book on one idea, called The Fence.

The fence is simple. You raise your hands, open, palms facing the other person, somewhere around chest height, between you and them. Relaxed, not stiff. Not pushing. Just there.

It looks like the most natural thing in the world. It's the same gesture you make when you say whoa, hang on, or easy, easy. To a bystander, it looks like you are calming someone down. To the angry person, it looks like you are not a threat. And quietly, without anyone noticing, your hands are now in a place where they can protect you if something goes wrong. That is the martial arts part. It's a guard that doesn't look like a guard.

Now, let's be clear. In your classroom, you are not going to stand in front of a student with your hands up like you're about to get hit. That would be strange, and frankly a little insulting to the kid. But the principle still works at a smaller size. Hands visible. Open. Still. Resting loosely in front of you, maybe one hand lightly holding the other, or resting on a desk. Palms more open than closed.

Here is a way to think about it. In a classroom with a frustrated student, your hands should look like they are offering something. In a grocery store with a stranger who is losing it, your hands can come up a bit higher into that soft fence, the whoa, easy gesture, while you step back.

A few things that do not work. Reaching out to touch an upset person's arm or shoulder. It feels kind, but to someone who is flooded with anger, a touch can feel like an attack. Pointing at the door and saying out. Waving your hands around a lot while you talk, because big fast hand movements can look like aggression. And any gesture that mocks, like a slow clap or an eye roll with a shrug. Those turn a heated moment into a personal one.

Now there's a verbal side to this too, and it comes from the second book I want to tell you about. It's called Verbal Judo, by George Thompson. No relation to Geoff, just a coincidence. George Thompson was an English professor, with a doctorate, who became a police officer. He noticed that some officers could talk almost anyone down, and others started fights just by opening their mouths. So he studied what the good ones did.

The name tells you the idea. In judo, you don't meet force head on. You redirect it. Verbal Judo does that with words.

Here are three things from it that fit right alongside your open hands.

First, don't say calm down. Thompson points out that calm down sounds like criticism. It tells the person they are wrong to feel what they feel, and it usually makes things worse. Something like I can see you're upset, or I hear you, does the job better.

Second, let insults go. An insult only has force if it gets a reaction. If you don't pick it up, it falls on the floor. That fits with something you wrote down yourself. If a student fights with you and you reply with logic, you're giving them a fight to win. It's the same idea as the rope on the ground. You don't have to pick up your end.

Third, respond, don't react. Thompson draws a line between the two. A reaction is your old brain grabbing the wheel. A response is you, choosing. And the way you buy yourself the half second to choose is the fourth thing on our list. Breath.

So here is the third idea again in one line. Keep your hands open, visible and still, and let the insult fall on the floor.

SECTION FOUR: BREATH AND VOICE

Here is the fourth idea. Your breath is the one part of your stress response you can grab with your hands, and when you slow your breath, your voice slows down with it.

Every martial art I know of cares about breathing. Karate students breathe out sharply when they strike. Judo players learn to breathe low and slow so they don't gas out. Boxers learn to breathe through a punch instead of holding their breath. The common thread is this. Under stress, people either hold their breath or breathe fast and high in the chest. Both make you tense, and both make your voice tight and high.

Here's the good news. There is decent research on a simple breathing pattern that helps.

In 2023, a team at Stanford, led by Melis Yilmaz Balban, with the well known neuroscientist Andrew Huberman as the senior author, ran a study. They had about a hundred adults do five minutes of breathing a day for about a month. Some did a mindfulness meditation. Others did different breathing patterns.

One pattern was called cyclic sighing. Here is how it goes. You breathe in through your nose. Then, at the top, you take a second short sip of air in through your nose, to fill your lungs all the way. Then you breathe out slowly and fully, through your mouth, longer than you breathed in.

Two in, one long out.

The study found that the cyclic sighing group had a bigger boost in positive mood than the meditation group, and their resting breathing rate slowed down more. The effect grew over the month. Now, honest caveats. It was a fairly small study, done remotely, and the authors themselves called it a first step that needs bigger follow ups. It did not find big differences in anxiety between the groups. So I would not oversell it. But it is a simple, cheap thing, and the long slow out breath is something martial arts, police training and calm teachers all seem to land on.

Here's the part I really want you to hold onto. You don't need five minutes in a heated moment. One of those double inhale, long exhale breaths, done quietly, while the other person is still talking, buys you that half second. It turns a reaction into a response.

And here's the bonus. When your breathing slows, your voice follows. You told me in October that one thing you wanted to try was slowing down your voice, your pace and your tone when students are aggravated, instead of ramping up with them. Breath is the handle for that. It is very hard to talk fast and high while breathing out slowly. Your body won't let you.

There is research on something called emotional contagion. The idea is that people tend to catch each other's emotions, partly by unconsciously copying each other's faces, voices and postures. That is why yelling spreads. But it works the other way too. If you can stay slow and low, some of that can spread back. Not always. Not with everybody. But you are at least not adding fuel.

What works with your voice in these moments. Lower. Slower. Fewer words. Short sentences. Leaving a pause after they finish before you answer.

What doesn't work. Matching their volume to be heard. Talking fast to get your point in. Long explanations. Sarcasm. And, this one is sneaky, an overly sweet, sing song voice. Angry people often hear that as fake or condescending, which is the opposite of what you want.

So here is the fourth idea again in one line. Breathe in twice, breathe out long, and let your voice slow down with your breath.

HOW IT LANDS IN YOUR WEEK

So let's bring these four things home. Stance, spacing, hands, breath. Where do they actually show up in your life?

Start with your classroom.

You already found something big on your own. In October, you noticed that your calm, controlled presence gave the kids a really good day. You wrote that you want to be deadly predictable, even in where you sit and when. Body language is the most predictable thing you can offer. If every time a student escalates, they see the same thing from you, balanced, a bit to the side, hands open, voice low and slow, then after a few weeks your body stops being something to test. It becomes something to lean on.

Here's a picture of how that might go. A student is upset and their voice is rising. You notice your own body wanting to square up or freeze. You take one cyclic sigh. Two in through the nose, one long out. You move so you're beside them, not over them, at an angle, a few feet back. Your hands are loose and in view. You say something short. I can see this is frustrating. Then you stop talking. You let them go first. That's another thing you've written down, that sometimes the best thing you can do is shut up and let the kids talk first. Body language is how you do that without looking like you've checked out.

Then, later, when things are calm, that's when your three question debrief comes in. What happened, what would it cost, what would work better. The body language gets you through the heat. The debrief does the teaching.

I want to be careful here and not oversell this. None of this is a magic trick. Some days a kid is going to escalate no matter what you do with your feet. The point isn't that it always works. The point is that your body stops adding fuel, and you stay yourself.

Now, the grocery store, or the parking lot, or anywhere with a stranger.

With a stranger, the goal is different. With a student, you have a relationship to protect and something to teach. With a stranger who is screaming at you, your only goals are to stay safe, keep it from getting worse, and leave. You don't have to win. You don't have to explain yourself. You don't have to be right.

So the version for strangers is simple. Step back and a bit to the side. Turn slightly. Hands come up into that soft whoa, easy fence, palms toward them. One long breath out. Short, calm words. Something like, Sorry, my mistake, or, okay, I hear you. Then you leave. Walk to where your wife is. Find a staff person if you need to. Walking away is not losing. In every martial art worth learning, the best fight is the one you never have.

Here's what not to do with a stranger. Don't try to correct them, even if they're wrong. Don't film them. Don't stare them down. Don't get drawn into a back and forth about who was in whose way. And don't touch them, even gently.

Now home. These same four things matter with your wife, in a different key. When a conversation gets tense at home, a lot of the same old brain moves show up. Crossed arms. Turning away. Talking faster. Squaring up across the kitchen island. You don't need a fence with your wife. But turning toward her a little instead of squaring off, uncrossing your arms, sitting down instead of standing over, and taking one long breath out before you answer, are all the same skills at a gentler size.

And faith. James writes, let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger. Read that again with your body in mind. Quick to hear is an open stance and open hands. Slow to speak is the breath before you answer. Slow to anger is the half second you buy so that you respond instead of react. Martial artists train their bodies to be steady under pressure. Christians are called to something deeper, a steadiness that comes from being secure in God, not in winning the moment. But the body is part of that. You can practise slow to anger with your feet and your lungs.

ONE THING TO TRY

Here's the one thing to try. Only one.

Pick a calm moment every school day, and run a ten second ready check. I'd suggest the ten twenty five break, because it happens at the same time every day and your students are out of the room.

Here's the check. Three parts.

Feet. Shoulder width, one a little ahead, knees soft.

Hands. Open, loose, in front of you.

Breath. One cyclic sigh. Two in through the nose, one long out through the mouth.

That's it. Ten seconds. Do it once a day when nothing is happening.

Why in a calm moment? Because that's how martial artists train. They don't learn a stance in the middle of a fight. They do it a thousand times when it's easy, so that it's there when it's hard. If you only ever try this when a kid is yelling, your old brain will win. If you practise it every day at ten twenty five, then in a few weeks, when a voice rises, your body will already know where to go.

Feet, hands, breath. Every day at break.

BOOKS AND SOURCES TO LOOK UP LATER

You asked if there's a book on this. There isn't one book that does martial arts body language for everyday life all in one place, but these three together cover it well.

First, What Every BODY Is Saying, by Joe Navarro. That's the former FBI agent. It's the best one for understanding the freeze, flight, fight reactions and the self soothing hand moves, in other people and in yourself. Easy to read, lots of pictures. Start here.

Second, Verbal Judo, The Gentle Art of Persuasion, by George Thompson. The English professor turned police officer. It's mostly about words, but it pairs perfectly with body language, and a lot of it maps straight onto a classroom. There is a second edition from 2013 that's easier to find.

Third, The Fence, The Art of Protection, by Geoff Thompson. The British bouncer and karate black belt. This is the most martial arts of the three, and the one to read for the stranger in the grocery store. A heads up, it comes out of nightclub door work, so some of it is about real fights and the language is rough in places. Read it for the hands and spacing ideas, not the fighting.
