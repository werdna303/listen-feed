Part one. Framing.

Framing is the simplest idea in this whole episode. The way you phrase a request shapes the answer you get. Not because the words are magic. Because the words tell the student what's normal.

When you say, "Are you going to do this today?" you've framed doing the work as an open question. You've put "no" on the table as a perfectly reasonable answer. When you say, "Do you want to start with the materials list or the measurements?" you've framed doing the work as settled. The only open question is where to begin. That's called choice framing, and it's a close cousin of what classroom people call assumptive language. You speak as if the good thing is going to happen.

Doug Lemov's book, Teach Like a Champion, has a few techniques that live right in this neighbourhood. One is called What to Do. The idea is simple. Tell students what to do, not what not to do. And make the direction specific, concrete, in order, and something you can actually see. So not "stop messing around." Instead, "Tape measure down, eyes on the board, pencil in your hand." Lemov's point, which he's made in interviews, is that a lot of what looks like defiance is really confusion. A kid who doesn't know exactly what you want will fill the gap with something you don't want. Precise directions teach the kids who honestly didn't know.

Another technique in the book is called Positive Framing. You correct by describing the solution, not the problem. You assume the best about why a student is off task, at least at first. Maybe he didn't hear. Maybe he didn't understand. And you narrate what's going right in the room. "I see three groups already measuring. Good." But here's a detail I love. Lemov's own guidance warns against narrating mediocrity, and against overdoing the assume-the-best move. You only narrate the positive when there's a real positive. You don't praise a room that's half asleep as if it's buzzing.

That warning is the whole ethical line, hiding in plain sight.

So let's run the test on choice framing. Would it still work if Tyler knew exactly what you were doing? Picture yourself saying, "Tyler, I'm giving you a choice of where to start because I want you to start, and I'd rather you pick the door than have me pick it for you." Does that kill it? Not really. Most kids would roll their eyes and pick one anyway. Because the choice is real. Both options are acceptable to you. And it's in his interest, because a kid who starts is a kid who gets the shed built.

Now here's where it crosses the line. It crosses when the choice is fake. "Do you want to do the worksheet now, or do it at lunch while everyone else is in the gym?" That's not a choice. That's a threat in a choice costume. Or it crosses when you use the same trick every single time, so the kid figures out that every question you ask him is a trap with two doors and you're standing behind both. Teens pick up on that pattern fast. And once they do, they stop trusting your questions. Which means you've spent the tool, and you've spent some trust along with it.

There's also a quieter way it crosses. Sometimes a kid has a real reason he can't do either option today. He didn't sleep. Something happened at home last night. If you lock him into "this or that" without ever leaving room for the honest third answer, you're not framing anymore. You're steamrolling, just politely.

So what do you use instead when you notice you're crossing? Two things. First, go back to a plain What to Do direction. Honest, clear, no dressing. "Tyler, flip the sheet over and write the first three materials. I'll be back in two minutes." That's not manipulative at all. It's just clear. Second, when you sense the third answer, ask for it straight. "Something's off today. Is this a can't, or a won't?" Then listen. That's not soft. It's respect, and teens can tell the difference.

So here's part one in a line. Frame the request so doing the work is normal, keep every choice real, and when the choice stops being real, just say plainly what you want.

Part two. Anchoring.

In nineteen seventy four, two psychologists named Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a famous paper on how people make judgments when they're unsure. One of the effects they described is called anchoring. Here's the classic demonstration. They spun a wheel of fortune in front of people, numbered from zero to a hundred. The wheel was rigged to stop on either ten or sixty five. Then they asked people what percent of the countries in the United Nations were African.

Now think about that. The wheel had nothing to do with Africa. Everybody watching knew it was a random spin. And still, the people who saw ten guessed somewhere around twenty five percent. The people who saw sixty five guessed around forty five percent. A meaningless number, seen first, pulled their answers by about twenty points.

That's anchoring. The first number, or the first example, becomes the starting point. People adjust from it, but not enough. The study and many after it found that this happens even when people know the anchor is arbitrary.

You already use this in your life. Every time you list something on Facebook Marketplace, you're setting an anchor. If you list the old tool chest at a hundred and fifty dollars, the buyer's counteroffer orbits a hundred and fifty. If you list it at eighty, the conversation orbits eighty.

So where does it show up in your classroom? Two big places. The bar for quality, and negotiations with students.

Start with the bar for quality. Whatever a student sees first becomes his picture of what good enough looks like. If the first thing he sees in your Civics and Careers class is a resume with three lines and a spelling mistake, that's his anchor. If the first thing he sees is a clean, one page resume from a former student, with real job experience written up properly, that's his anchor instead. Same kid, same ability. Different starting point.

In your construction class, this is even more obvious. Hold up a joint that's tight and square before anyone picks up a saw. Let them run their fingers over it. That joint is now the anchor. When a kid brings you something loose and gappy, you don't need to lecture him. You just put his next to the anchor. He can see it himself.

Won't a perfect example discourage struggling kids? It can, if you leave it there. But hold that thought, because the research on wise feedback, which we'll get to in part three, says high standards don't discourage kids. Standards without belief discourage kids. Standards with belief are rocket fuel.

Now the second place. Negotiations. Your students negotiate with you constantly. About due dates, about how many questions, about whether they can go to the washroom for the third time. Anchoring tells you something useful. Whoever names the first number usually shapes the deal.

So when a kid says, "Sir, can I hand it in next week?" you can let his anchor sit there, next week, and negotiate down from it. Or you can name your own first. "I need it Thursday. What do you need from me to make Thursday happen?" Now the conversation is about Thursday. Maybe it ends up Friday morning. That's fine. You moved a little, and he feels heard. But the conversation started where you put it.

Let's run the test. Would it still work if the student knew exactly what you were doing? If you said, "I'm showing you the best example first because I want you aiming at it," would that wreck the effect? No. You could even say it out loud. Most good coaches do. "This is what we're aiming for." The anchor works partly because it's true. That really is the standard. Is it in his interest? Yes. A kid who aims higher usually lands higher.

Now here's where anchoring crosses the line. It crosses when the anchor is fake. Picture this. You actually only want ten questions done. But you announce twenty, so that when the kids groan, you can graciously cut it to ten and look like the reasonable one. Robert Cialdini, whose work we'll get to in part three, describes a version of this. Start with a big request you expect to be refused, then retreat to the smaller one you wanted all along. It works on people. It really does.

But run the test. Would it still work if they knew? Absolutely not. If Tyler knew you planned the retreat from the start, the whole thing collapses, and worse, he feels played. Is the anchor true? No. Twenty was never real. And here's the practical problem with your kids in particular. They've been worked by adults before. Some of them have been worked by adults their whole lives. They are very good at noticing when the first offer was theatre. The first time they catch you inflating an anchor, every number you say afterward gets discounted. You've taught them that your first number is never your real number.

The same goes for due dates. If you name Thursday as your anchor, Thursday should be a real date you have a real reason for. Not a fake hard line you were always going to fold on. If you fold every time, the kids learn that your first date is fiction.

So what do you do instead, when you feel the pull to fake an anchor? Name your real number, and give your real reason. "I want ten done today because tomorrow we start building, and if you don't know these, you'll cut the wrong length." Then let the negotiation happen honestly around the real number. If you're flexible, be flexible on purpose, out loud. "Thursday is my target. If something's going on, come tell me before Thursday, not after." That keeps the anchor real and the trust intact.

So here's part two in a line. Whatever comes first becomes the standard, so make the first example excellent and the first number real.

Let's take a quick breath and recap, because we're about halfway.

You've got one ethical test with three questions. Would it still work if they knew? Is it in their interest? Is the thing I'm leaning on actually true? You've got framing. Word requests so doing the work is normal, and keep the choices real. And you've got anchoring. Show the best example first, name your real number first, and never inflate an anchor just to look generous when you back off it.

Now the big one. Authority.

Part three. Authority, and its two cousins.

Let me get the famous study out of the way quickly, because you've probably heard of it. In the early nineteen sixties, Stanley Milgram ran experiments at Yale where ordinary people were told by a man in a lab coat to give what they believed were painful electric shocks to another person. The shocks weren't real, but the participants didn't know that. About two thirds of them went all the way to the highest level on the machine, mostly because an authority figure calmly told them to continue.

I mention it for one reason only. It shows how powerful the signals of authority are. A lab coat, a calm voice, a setting that looks official. People defer to those signals. That's exactly why authority is a tool worth thinking hard about. It's strong, and strong tools can be misused.

Now let's bring in Robert Cialdini. He's a psychologist who spent decades studying why people say yes. His book, called Influence, describes a handful of principles that reliably move people. He originally named six, and later added a seventh. The ones that matter most for you today are authority, consistency, and liking. I'll take authority first, then the two cousins more briefly.

Here's the most useful thing Cialdini says about authority. There's a difference between being an authority and having the trappings of authority. Titles, uniforms, a raised voice, the big desk at the front. Those are trappings. People respond to trappings, but trappings are also easy to fake. Real authority is expertise plus trustworthiness. You actually know the thing, and you're actually honest about it.

Now think about your students. They have spent years watching adults lean on trappings. And David Yeager, a psychologist at the University of Texas, has done a lot of work showing what this does to teenagers. In a paper he wrote with Ronald Dahl and Carol Dweck in two thousand eighteen, the argument is this. Adolescents have a stronger hunger for status and respect than younger kids do. And a lot of programs aimed at teens fail because they feel condescending. They treat teens like children who need to be told. When an intervention respects a teen's wish for status, it works better and it lasts longer.

Put that together with Cialdini. Leaning on the trappings of authority, with a teenager, is pushing on the exact bruise that makes him resist. "I'm the teacher, so you'll do it" is a status attack. He hears, "You're beneath me." And your students, more than most, have been made to feel beneath people. So positional authority, used alone, is one of the weakest tools you have with them. It might win the moment. It costs you the room.

But real authority, the expertise kind, is one of the strongest. And you've got a lot of it. You fix your own truck. You've built things. In your construction class, when you pick up a framing square and show them a trick you actually use, you aren't saying "respect me." You're showing them why they might. In your phys-ed class, when you run the drill with them and you know the pacing because you're out running at dawn, that's real. Kids notice real.

Cialdini has another sharp point here. One of the fastest ways to become credible is to admit a weakness first. When a person says, here's what I'm not good at, then says, here's what I am good at, people believe the second part more. For you, that might sound like, "I'm not great at the drywall finishing part, so I brought in a video from someone better than me. But I can show you framing all day." That kind of honesty doesn't shrink your authority. It makes the rest of what you say believable.

Now the test. Would authority still work if the student knew what you were doing? If you said, "I'm showing you this because I know it and I want you to trust me on the next part," does it still work? Yes, because the expertise is real. Is it in his interest? Yes, he learns something true. Is it true? That's the big question for authority. Cialdini puts the whole ethics of influence on exactly that. He describes the difference between what he calls a detective of influence and a smuggler of influence. The detective looks for the principles that are genuinely present in a situation and brings them to the surface. You really are an expert, so you let them see it. The smuggler drags a principle in where it doesn't belong. He fakes expertise, invents a deadline, flatters someone he doesn't like. Cialdini's line is that you want to be the detective, never the smuggler.

So authority crosses the line when you bluff. When a kid asks you a question about the building code and you make up an answer rather than say "I don't know, let's look it up." When you claim the principal "insists" on something to dodge being the bad guy. When you hint at consequences you don't have the power to deliver. Each one is smuggling. And your students will find out, because they always find out. When they do, your real authority takes the hit too.

What do you use instead? Say "I don't know" when you don't know. Own your own rules. "This is my rule, and here's why." And let your real expertise do the work, rather than your title.

Now the two cousins, briefly.

The first cousin is consistency, which Cialdini sometimes calls commitment and consistency. People want to act in line with what they've already said and done. Once someone commits to something, especially out loud or in writing, they feel pressure to follow through. Classroom version. At the start of your Civics and Careers block, ask each kid to write one thing they'll finish today, on a sticky note, on their desk. Small, specific, their own words. Later you just point at it. "That's yours, not mine." That works because it's their commitment.

Here's where it crosses. When you trick a small yes so you can leverage it into a big one. "You said you'd try, right? So you'll stay at lunch." The kid didn't agree to lunch. You smuggled it. Test it. Would it work if he knew? No. Is it his commitment? No, it's yours, dressed in his clothes. Instead, keep the commitment theirs and keep it small. If you need something bigger, ask for the bigger thing directly.

The second cousin is liking. People say yes more to people they like. Cialdini points to things like similarity, real compliments, and working together toward the same goal. Classroom version. You notice a kid's truck in the parking lot and ask him about it. You both know what it's like when a starter goes. That's real similarity, and he'll work a little harder for you. It's not a trick. It's a relationship.

Where it crosses. When you fake it. Pretending to care about a band you've never heard of. Laying on praise to grease a request. Teens can smell fake liking from across the gym. And fake liking is worse than none, because it tells the kid you think he's easy to fool. That's a status insult again. Instead, find something you actually share, or something you actually admire, and say only that. If you can't find anything today, be honest and kind, and keep looking.

So here's part three in a line. Real authority is expertise plus honesty, and the moment you fake any of it, you trade a short win for a long loss.

Now let's go back to one study, because it connects all three tools.

Part four. Wise feedback, and why teens test you.

In two thousand fourteen, David Yeager, Geoffrey Cohen and colleagues published a study on what they called wise feedback. It was with grade seven students, kids earning around Bs and Cs. The students wrote essays. Teachers marked them up with critical comments, the normal kind. Then, in half the cases, a small note was attached. In the comparison group, the note was bland. In the wise feedback group, the note said this: I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them.

That's it. One sentence. And the kids who got that note were much more likely to revise their essays. Reports of the exact numbers vary across the different studies in the paper, but in rough terms, revision rates went from somewhere under half to somewhere around double that. And the effect was biggest for the students who trusted their teachers the least. For those students, the note made the most difference of all.

Why does that note work? Because it answers the question every struggling kid is silently asking about criticism. Is this teacher on my side, or is this teacher against me? Critical feedback, by itself, is ambiguous. A kid who's been burned assumes the worst. The note removes the ambiguity. It says, the standard is high, and you belong to the group that can meet it.

Now look at how this one sentence passes our test perfectly. Would it still work if the kid knew what you were doing? Yes. In fact, the whole point is that you're saying it out loud. Is it in his interest? Completely. Is it true? Here's the catch. It has to be. If you say it and don't mean it, if you write it on every paper like a stamp while treating some kids as lost causes, they'll feel it. Your students especially. The power of the note comes from the fact that it's an honest statement of two real things. High standards. Real belief.

And notice how it combines the three tools. It's framing, because it frames criticism as respect. It's anchoring, because it sets the bar high, right up front. It's authority, the real kind, because it comes from someone who knows what good work looks like and is honest enough to say so.

Here's the deeper point for you. Teens test adults. Your students more than most. When a kid pushes back, slumps, hands in junk, he's often running an experiment. Is this adult real? Will he still hold the line? Does he actually think I can do this? Manipulation fails those experiments eventually, because a trick only works until it's spotted. Honesty passes them, because the more the kid looks, the more true it turns out to be. That's the real answer to your question about commanding respect. You don't command it with techniques. You earn it by being the adult whose techniques still work after the kid sees through them.

So here's part four in a line. High standards plus honest belief, said out loud, beats any trick, and it works best on the kids who trust you least.

Now let's put this into your actual week.

Monday morning, the kids roll in for the desk sheet. A few have their heads down. Instead of "Are you going to do the desk sheet?" try a real choice. "Start with the top half or the bottom half, your pick." Keep it real. Both halves get done, but they choose the door.

In your Civics and Careers class, when you start the resume unit or any written task, put the excellent example up first. Ideally something a real student from your program made. That's your anchor. Then use the wise feedback sentence, or your own honest version of it, when you hand back the first drafts. Write it on the drafts of the kids who trust you least.

In your construction class, let your expertise carry your authority. When you correct a cut, show them the reason, not your title. When you don't know something, say so and look it up together. That will build more respect than any amount of "I'm in charge here."

In phys-ed, use the consistency tool lightly. Before the run or the drill, ask each kid what their own target is today. One more lap. Beating last week's time. Then, at the end, ask if they hit it. Their goal, not yours.

And when a student wants to negotiate a due date, name your real date first, give your real reason, and flex only on purpose, out loud. No fake twenty to get to ten.

This isn't about being calmer or firmer. It's about the words and the order. Small things that add up across a semester.

Now, one thing to try. Just one.

This week, when you catch yourself using one of these tools, run the first question of the test out loud. Literally say the technique to the student. "I'm giving you two choices because I want you starting, and I want you to pick." Or, "I'm showing you the best one first because that's what we're aiming for." Or, "I'm telling you this because I have high expectations and I know you can reach them."

If the tool survives being said out loud, keep it. It's honest influence, and you'll find it often works even better when the kid knows. If you can't bring yourself to say it out loud, because saying it would ruin it, that's your signal. You've drifted into smuggling. Drop the trick and say plainly what you want instead.

You're a systems guy. Think of this as a filter you install once and run on every move. One question, said out loud. Would this still work if he knew?

Before we wrap, a short line that fits. In a Truth For Life devotional on the ninth commandment, Alistair Begg lists some of the quieter ways we fail to tell the truth, and one of them is this. Giving false impressions. Alistair Begg, Truth For Life. That's really what a smuggled technique is. Not a lie in words, exactly. A false impression. Which is a good reason to stay the detective.

Here's the closing recap in three sentences. Framing, anchoring and authority are all fine tools when the choice is real, the anchor is true, and the expertise is yours. They become manipulation the moment they'd stop working if the student saw what you were doing, and teens, especially your teens, always eventually see. When you hit that line, trade the trick for a plain honest statement of what you want and why, and add high expectations with real belief.
