Rest in Hebrews, the rest that remains

Part one. A scene.

Picture a Tuesday morning. You are up before it is light. You have read your Bible, and you have opened the planning chat, and you are texting a friend while the first answer is written. Everything on the list is reasonable. Every item is good. And somewhere in the middle of it, a small voice says, if I do all of this well, then I will finally be able to relax.

Now picture the end of that same day. The list is mostly done. And you are not relaxed. A new list has quietly taken its place.

That is the scene I want to start with, because it is the exact scene the letter to the Hebrews walks into. Not your Tuesday, of course. A group of tired Jewish Christians who were tempted to go back to the old system, because the old system had something they could do. It had rules, and offerings, and days, and a checklist. Faith felt like standing still. And the writer says to them, in the middle of the letter, one of the strangest and most beautiful promises in the whole Bible. There remains a rest for the people of God.

Here is the promise of this episode, in one sentence. The rest in Hebrews is not a nap and not a day off. It is God's own rest, it is finished work you receive by believing, and you already taste it today while you are still running toward it.

We will take that in four steps. First, where the word rest comes from, and why unbelief is the one thing that blocks it. Second, why Canaan was never the real rest, and why that matters. Third, what it means to cease from your own works, which is the heart of it. And fourth, how a rest you have already entered can also be a rest you are told to strive for. Then we will land it in your actual week, and I will give you one thing to try.

Part two. Where the word rest comes from.

The writer of Hebrews does not make up the idea of rest. He borrows it from a Psalm. Psalm ninety five. It is a short song that begins with praise and ends with a warning. Come, let us sing to the Lord. And then, near the end, God speaks. Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness. And then a terrible line. God says of that generation, they shall not enter my rest.

Hold that picture. A whole people had been rescued from Egypt. They had seen the sea open. They had eaten bread that fell from the sky. And when they came to the edge of the land that God had promised, they did not believe him. So they wandered for forty years, and that generation died in the wilderness. The rest God had promised them, they did not enter.

Hebrews three and four takes that old story and says, this is not just history. This is about you. The first thing it says is simple. Why did they not enter? Hebrews three nineteen says it plainly. They were unable to enter because of unbelief.

Now listen to how John Calvin handles that. Calvin is commenting on Hebrews four, verse three, where it says, we who have believed enter that rest. And Calvin says, and I quote, "Unbelief alone shuts us out; then faith alone opens an entrance."

Say that to yourself once. Unbelief alone shuts us out. Faith alone opens the door.

Notice what that does not say. It does not say that laziness shuts us out. It does not say that a low score on your list shuts us out. It does not say that being behind shuts us out. The only thing that blocks God's rest is refusing to believe what he has said. That is good news for anyone who is tired, because tired is not the problem. Unbelief is the problem.

And notice the second thing. The writer says in verse two that the good news came to us just as to them. The wilderness generation had the gospel, in shadow form, and the message did not benefit them, because it was not mixed with faith in those who heard it. So the question is never, did you hear the promise. The question is, did you trust it.

So that is our first idea, and here it is in one line. God's rest is entered by believing his promise, and only unbelief shuts it out.

Part three. Why Canaan was never the real rest.

Here is the second idea, and it is a clever piece of argument, so stay with me.

You might think, fine, the wilderness generation failed, but the next generation got in. Joshua led them across the Jordan. They took the land. The book of Joshua even says that the Lord gave them rest. So the rest was Canaan, and that is that.

The writer of Hebrews says no, and he gives two arguments. Let me give them to you slowly.

The first argument is about the seventh day. In verse four he quotes Genesis. God rested on the seventh day from all his works. Think about that. God's rest did not start at Canaan. It started at creation. God finished his work, and sat down, so to speak, and enjoyed what he had made. So when God says, they shall not enter my rest, he means a rest that was already there, ready, since the beginning of the world. The writer says in verse three that his works were finished from the foundation of the world. The rest was prepared. It was waiting. The only question was who would enter.

The second argument is about one little word. Today. Verse seven says that long after Joshua, God spoke through David and said, today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts. David wrote that Psalm hundreds of years after Joshua. If Joshua had given them the true rest, says verse eight, God would not have spoken of another day later on. The fact that God was still saying today, centuries later, proves that the final rest had not been reached yet.

Calvin explains this well. He says that the land of Canaan was real, and was a kind of rest, but that it was, and I quote, "an image and a symbol of the spiritual inheritance." The people who got into Canaan should not have settled down and said, we have arrived. They should have looked past the land to what the land was pointing at.

Now that is a very important idea for anybody who lives by plans and goals. Canaan was a real gift. God meant it. But it was a signpost. And the danger of a signpost is that you stop at it, and treat it as the destination.

Let me give you a modern version. A good job is a real gift. A finished house project is a real gift. A permanent contract would be a real gift. A calm classroom is a real gift. None of those is the rest of Hebrews four. If you wait for any of them to give you the rest, you will find that they are Canaan. Good. Real. And not the end.

Here is the second idea in one line. Every earthly rest is a signpost, and the true rest is the one God entered on the seventh day, and invites us into.

Part four. A recap, halfway.

Let me stop and put the two ideas together.

First, God's rest is his own rest, prepared since creation, and it is entered by believing his promise. Unbelief alone shuts a person out.

Second, Canaan, and Joshua, and the weekly Sabbath were all signposts. David's word today, hundreds of years after Joshua, proves that something better was still ahead.

Now we come to the sentence that all of this has been building to. Verse nine. So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God.

Here is a small detail worth knowing. All through chapters three and four, the writer uses one Greek word for rest. It is katapausis. It means a resting place, or a cessation. Then, in verse nine, he suddenly switches to a different word, sabbatismos. It is used only here in the entire New Testament. It means, specifically, a sabbath rest, a sabbath keeping. A teacher at Ligonier Ministries points out this exact switch, and says that Hebrews teaches us that eternal glory is a Sabbath rest. So the writer is saying, the thing God has prepared is not only the end of a journey. It is a kind of celebration. It is the joyful enjoyment of what God has finished.

Spurgeon preached on this verse in eighteen fifty seven, and he put the word like this. He said, "In my text it is, in the original, called the Sabbatism, not the Sabbath, but the rest of the Sabbath, not the outward ritual of the Sabbath, which was binding upon the Jew, but the inward spirit of the sabbath, which is the joy and delight of the Christian."

The inward spirit of the sabbath. Not the rules about the day, but the joy under the rules. The rest of Hebrews is the thing the sabbath day was always pointing to.

Part five. Ceasing from your own works.

Now the heart of it. Verse ten. For whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his.

This is the verse that should go off like an alarm for anyone who treats life as a project.

Let me explain what it is saying. God finished his work on the sixth day, and then he rested. The writer says that when you enter God's rest, you rest from your works in the same way. You stop working for your standing. You stop trying to earn the place that has already been prepared and finished.

Calvin takes a whole paragraph on this verse, and it is worth hearing his own words. He says this is a definition of what he calls the perpetual Sabbath, and then he says, and I quote, "The chief good of man is nothing else but union with God; this is attained when we are formed according to him as our exemplar."

That is his first step. The best thing in the universe for a human being is to be united to God.

Then he says this is reached when we rest from our works. And he asks a sharp question. "For what else is to cease from our works, but to mortify our flesh, when a man renounces himself that he may live to God?"

So for Calvin, resting from your works is not laziness. It is the opposite of self reliance. It is a kind of dying to the idea that I hold my life together.

And then comes the line I want you to carry with you today. Calvin says that we must begin there, with a man being in a manner dead to himself, so that he may "abstain from his own works, so as to give place to God to work." And then, "such is the opposition between God's government and our corrupt affections, that he cannot work in us until we rest."

Let me say that in my own words. God cannot do his work in you while you are busy doing his job. Resting from your works is not the absence of work. It is making room for God to be the one who works.

That is a hard thing to hear if you love getting things done. So let me be careful. This is not a rule against working. Nobody in the Bible tells you to quit your job and sit on the couch. Paul worked harder than all the other apostles, and he said it was the grace of God with him. The point is where your confidence rests. Are you working in order to get safe, or are you working because you are already safe?

A teacher works differently in those two states. Picture a morning in your classroom. A student is not settling. The room feels loud. You can be working from the first posture, where every bad ten minutes is a verdict on you, and every good ten minutes is a win you have to protect. Or you can be working from the second posture, where the verdict was already given, in Christ, a long time ago, and today is only today. The second teacher is not lazier. The second teacher is steadier. The kid who is not settling needs a steady adult more than a perfect plan.

And that, if I can say it kindly, is also where the thank you cards and the decision razors fit. Those are good practices. But practices are Canaan. They are real and useful signposts. They cannot be the rest itself.

Here is the third idea in one line. To enter God's rest is to stop working to earn your standing, and to give God room to work in you.

Part six. The already and the not yet.

Now the puzzle. And it is a real puzzle, so I want to be honest about it.

In verse three it says, we who have believed enter that rest. Present tense. We are entering it. In verse ten it says, whoever has entered has also rested. Past tense. But in verse eleven it says, let us therefore strive to enter that rest. Strive. Make every effort. And in verse nine it says a rest remains, so it is still ahead.

So is it a rest you have entered, or a rest you are still heading toward? Is it something to receive, or something to strive for?

The old Reformed answer is yes. Both. Already and not yet.

Spurgeon said it plainly in that same sermon. He said, "Now, this rest, I believe, is partly enjoyed on earth." Then he said, quoting the verse, "We that have believed do enter into rest, for we have ceased from our own works, as God did from his." But, he said, the full fruition of it remains in the future and eternal state.

Partly now. Fully later. Both are true.

A writer at Ligonier explains why. He says God's own rest has the same shape. God rested on the seventh day from the work of creation. That is his already rest. But he has not stopped working. He is still redeeming his people. And the writer says, and this is a short line, "He cannot rest from that work until all the elect have been glorified." So God's own not yet rest comes at the end, when everything he has set out to do is complete. And we share in that rest in two stages. A real taste now, and the whole thing later.

Calvin makes the same point about the striving. He says the completion of this rest cannot be reached in this life, but we should always strive for it. And then he gives a line I love, "Thus believers enter it but on this condition, that by running they may continually go forward."

By running. You will like that one.

Here is how the striving and the resting fit together. The striving in verse eleven is not a striving to earn. It is a striving to keep believing. Look at the very next verses. Verse twelve says that the word of God is living and active. Verse fourteen says that we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, and that we can draw near to the throne of grace with confidence, to receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need. So the striving is not climbing up a ladder. It is running toward a throne where someone already sits for you.

Think of a child running toward a parent with open arms. That child is exerting effort. Legs are pumping. But nobody would say the child is earning the hug. The effort is the shape that trust takes.

Kim Riddlebarger, writing for Ligonier, describes the Christian life as a pilgrimage and puts it in one sentence I will repeat. He says, "A life of toil and labor will be followed by an eternity of rest." He goes on to say that we receive what God promised through faith in Christ, and not through our own good works. So the pilgrimage is real, and the rest at the end is real, and the thing that carries you is not your list.

Here is the fourth idea in one line. You have entered the rest by believing, and you are running toward its fullness, and the running is trust and not earning.

Part seven. A recap of the four ideas.

Let me gather them. One, only unbelief blocks God's rest, and faith opens the door. Two, Canaan and the weekly Sabbath were signposts, and the true rest is God's own, prepared since creation. Three, to enter that rest is to cease from your works as a way to earn your standing, and to give God room to work in you. Four, the rest is already yours in part, and not yet complete, so we run toward it in trust.

Part eight. How this lands in your actual week.

Let me bring this down to the week you are living.

First, the morning. Your morning has a rhythm. Bible reading, then the planning chat, then breakfast for your wife around ten past six. Notice that the Bible reading comes before the planning. That is already the order of Hebrews. First, receive. Then, work. The danger is that the planning chat quietly takes over, and the reading becomes one more item you did. The rest of Hebrews four is the reverse. Before you do anything, you are already held.

Second, the classroom. When a morning goes sideways, the old reflex says, I failed. The rest of Hebrews says, your standing is not on the line this morning. You are free to be curious about what the student needs, instead of defensive about how it reflects on you. That is a real difference in what you do next. The steady response almost always works better than the anxious one.

Third, the permanent contract, and the side projects, and the lawn care, and the reselling. All of those are good. And all of them are Canaan. If a contract comes through, it will be a real gift, and still not the rest. If it does not come through, that was never the thing that could shut you out of the rest either. Only unbelief does that. So you can pursue the contract with energy and still hold it loosely.

Fourth, the evening. You have a bedtime you want to keep. Lights out at nine forty five. Here is a thought. Going to sleep is a small daily practice of Hebrews four. You stop. The world keeps running without you. God neither slumbers nor sleeps, so you can. You do not have to finish the list for the world to hold together overnight. Every night, you act out the verse. You rest from your works as God did from his, and you trust that he is working.

Fifth, Sunday. Sunday is the day you meet with others and sing and pray and hear the word. Riddlebarger says it directly. We observe the Lord's Day as a festive day of rest that foreshadows the glories of an eternal Sabbath yet to come. That is what Sunday is for. Not a day to fall behind, but a day to taste the end. A small preview of where the whole thing is going.

Part nine. One thing to try.

Only one.

Before you start the first task of the day, say one sentence out loud. Say it on the run, or at the sink, or in the car. The sentence is this. The work that matters most for me is already finished, and today I get to work from it, not for it.

That is it. One sentence, before the first move. If it feels too tidy, change the words. The point is to put the order right. Finished work first. Your work second.

And a small note on how to use it. On a hard day, say it again at the moment you feel most behind. Not as a magic phrase. As a reminder of where your weight rests.

Part ten. The closing recap.

Here is the whole episode in three sentences.

God has prepared a rest since the foundation of the world, and only unbelief keeps a person out of it. Every earthly rest, Canaan and the weekly Sabbath included, was a signpost, and the true rest is to cease from your works and let God be the one who works. You have already tasted that rest by faith, and you are running toward its fullness, and the running is trust, not earning.

And I will close with one line from Alistair Begg, from a Truth For Life devotional called God Rested. He says, "We experience now the rest of bringing our sin to Jesus to be dealt with and our cares to Jesus to find help for."

Your sin, and your cares. Both to Jesus. That is the rest, for today.
