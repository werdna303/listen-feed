Part one. Two kinds of thread.

Let's start with the one people love to mock. The mixed fabrics law. It shows up in Leviticus chapter nineteen and again in Deuteronomy chapter twenty two. And the Deuteronomy version is specific. Don't wear a garment of wool and linen woven together.

Now, people quote this online to make Christians look silly. You eat shrimp and wear polyester blends, so why care about any of the other laws? We'll come back to that question. But first, let's ask the real one. Why wool and linen?

Here's a clue that a lot of scholars point to. Go back to Exodus, where God gives the plans for the tabernacle and the high priest's clothing. The curtains of the tabernacle, and parts of the high priest's garments, were made of fine linen woven with blue, purple and scarlet yarn. And that coloured yarn, in that world, was most likely dyed wool. In other words, the very mixture that was forbidden to the ordinary Israelite was used in the holiest place, and on the holiest person.

So one strong reading is this. Wool and linen together was the fabric of the sanctuary. It belonged to holy space. An ordinary Israelite wearing it would be like a regular person walking around in a priest's vestments. Not because the cloth was evil, but because it was set apart. The law kept a line between ordinary life and the holy presence of God. Jewish scholars noticed this connection long ago, and so have Christian ones.

And there's a lovely exception that fits. In the book of Numbers, chapter fifteen, Israelites were told to put tassels on the corners of their garments, with a cord of blue in each one. Jewish tradition has long understood that blue cord to be wool, even on a linen garment. So every Israelite carried a tiny thread of the sanctuary on the edge of his clothes. And Numbers says what it's for. When you see it, you'll remember the commandments, and you won't just follow your own heart and your own eyes. A thread of holiness, as a reminder.

Now the honest part. Not everyone reads it this way. The medieval Jewish thinker Maimonides thought the rule was aimed at pagan priests who wore wool and linen together, so Israel was being kept apart from their practices. Others think it's simply about keeping God's created kinds distinct, the same way the seeds and the animals were not to be mixed. These ideas can overlap. But the sanctuary reading is the one with the most support right in the text.

And while we're here, the same verse in Leviticus nineteen has two cousins. Don't breed two kinds of animals together, and don't sow your field with two kinds of seed. Here's a detail you'll enjoy. Israel wasn't supposed to breed mules, since a mule is a cross between a horse and a donkey. But later in the Old Testament, King David rides a mule, and Solomon rides David's mule to his coronation. So using a mule wasn't the problem. The law was about Israelites deliberately mixing kinds. It seems to echo Genesis chapter one, where God makes everything according to its kind. Israel's fields and barns were to mirror the order of creation, the way the tabernacle mirrored heaven. Every time a farmer planted one crop in a field, he was quietly saying that the world has a shape, and God gave it that shape.

So what's the big idea? The mixed fabric law was a daily lesson in holiness. There's a difference between ordinary and holy. God is not common. And his people wore that lesson, literally, every day.

You already understand this. Think about your team's jersey when you were young, or the shirt you wear on a work site with the company logo. Clothes say who you belong to. Israel's clothes said it too. Not just in what they wore, but in what they didn't.

One line for part one. The mixed fabric law kept the holy fabric for the holy place, and turned every Israelite's wardrobe into a reminder that God is holy.

Part two. The menu.

Now the food laws. Leviticus chapter eleven has the long list. Animals that chew the cud and have a split hoof are clean, so cows and sheep and goats. Pigs, camels, rabbits, not. Fish need fins and scales, so no shellfish. A list of birds, mostly birds of prey and scavengers, are off limits. And so on.

Why? You've probably heard the health explanation. Pork carries parasites. Shellfish can make you sick. God was protecting them from food poisoning. It sounds sensible, and some of it may even be a side benefit. But most scholars don't think it's the main reason. A few problems. Plenty of the banned animals aren't especially unhealthy, and some allowed ones can be. Camels aren't more dangerous to eat than cows. And the Bible itself never gives health as the reason. And if the food laws were mainly about health, it would be very strange for the New Testament to set them aside, as we'll see in a minute.

In nineteen sixty six, an anthropologist named Mary Douglas wrote a famous book called Purity and Danger. She argued that the food laws were about categories, about wholeness. Clean animals were the ones that fit their category neatly. Land animals walk on hooves. Sea creatures have fins and scales. Animals that blurred the lines, like a creature that lives in water but has no fins, were unclean. Holiness, in her reading, meant completeness and order. It was hugely influential. And then, decades later, she herself said her first explanation had been a big mistake, and she offered a different one. So that tells you something. Very smart people have struggled with these laws, and changed their minds.

But here's where I want to land, because the Bible actually gives a reason. In Leviticus chapter twenty, God says something like this. I am the Lord your God, who has separated you from the peoples. So you shall separate the clean animal from the unclean. And you shall be holy to me, for I the Lord am holy, and have separated you from the peoples to be mine.

Do you hear it? The food line is tied to the people line. God set Israel apart from the nations, and the menu was the daily, three times a day reminder of that. Every meal said, you belong to the Lord. You're different. And practically, it also made it hard to sit at a pagan neighbour's table and slide into his way of life. The kitchen was a boundary marker.

Now watch what happens in the New Testament. In Mark chapter seven, Jesus tells the crowds that what goes into a person's mouth doesn't make him unclean. What comes out of the heart does. Evil thoughts, theft, greed, deceit. And Mark adds a short comment. In saying this, Jesus declared all foods clean.

And then in Acts chapter ten, Peter has a vision. A sheet comes down from heaven, full of animals, clean and unclean, and a voice says, kill and eat. Peter says, no way, I've never eaten anything unclean. And the voice says, what God has made clean, do not call common. Then, right after, Peter gets called to the house of a Roman soldier named Cornelius. A Gentile. And Peter says, God has shown me that I should not call any person common or unclean.

So the vision about food was really about people. The food laws had marked the line between Israel and the nations. And now that the gospel was going out to the nations, the line came down. The fence that had protected the nursery was opened, because the family was growing to include the whole world.

That's why you can eat a bacon sandwich in good conscience. Not because God changed his mind about pigs. Because the thing the menu pointed to has arrived. The separation now is not about the kitchen. It's about the heart.

One line for part two. The food laws were a daily boundary marker for a set apart people, and when the gospel went to the nations, the boundary moved from the menu to the heart.

Part three. The goat and its mother.

Here's the strangest of the lot. Three times in the Old Testament, in Exodus twice and Deuteronomy once, you find this. Do not boil a young goat in its mother's milk.

Of all the laws, this one sounds most random. And honestly, it's the one where scholars admit they're least sure. So let me give you the main ideas, and be clear about how solid each one is.

Idea one. It was aimed at a pagan ritual. Maimonides suggested this back in the twelve hundreds. He guessed that the Canaanites boiled a kid in milk as some kind of harvest rite, and Israel was told not to copy it. In the last century, people thought they'd found proof, in an ancient text from a city called Ugarit, in what's now Syria. But later scholars looked closely at that text and found the reading was shaky. It doesn't clearly mention a goat in its mother's milk at all. So that proof has mostly fallen apart. The idea could still be right, but it doesn't have the evidence people once thought.

Idea two. It's about compassion. And this one has a lot going for it, because it sits next to a family of similar laws. In Leviticus chapter twenty two, a newborn calf or lamb had to stay with its mother for a week before it could be offered. And you weren't to kill a cow and her calf on the same day. In Deuteronomy chapter twenty two, if you found a bird's nest, you could take the eggs or the young, but you had to let the mother go. Over and over, the law draws a line around the mother and her young.

Put that together and the goat law starts to make sense. Milk is what a mother gives to keep her young alive. To cook the young in the very milk that was meant to give it life is to take something life giving and make it part of a death. There's something twisted about it. Even when you eat meat, which God allowed, he put a small fence around tenderness. Not everything that's permitted is fitting.

Jewish tradition built a large set of rules on this law, keeping meat and dairy completely apart. That's a later development, and Christians don't follow it. But the core idea, that the law protects something tender in the created order, is worth holding onto.

So is this certain? No. But the compassion reading fits the pattern of the surrounding laws, and it fits what we know of God's character. He cares about sparrows. He cares about the mother bird. He cares about the goat.

One line for part three. The goat law is hard to pin down, but it fits a family of laws that protect the bond between mother and young, a fence around tenderness even in ordinary life.

Part four. Clean and unclean, life and death.

Leviticus has long chapters on what made a person unclean. A skin disease, often translated leprosy, though it was probably a range of skin conditions, not just what we call leprosy today. Mould in a house. Bodily discharges. Childbirth. Touching a dead body. A person who was unclean couldn't come to the tabernacle until they'd been cleansed, which usually meant waiting, washing, and sometimes an offering.

To modern ears, this sounds harsh, even insulting. A woman is unclean after childbirth? A man with a skin disease has to live outside the camp? What's going on?

Here's an idea that has helped a lot of readers. A Jewish scholar named Jacob Milgrom, who wrote one of the biggest commentaries ever on Leviticus, noticed something about the list. Blood. Semen. Skin disease. Death. What do they have in common? Milgrom argued that the common thread was death. Losing blood is losing life. A skin disease makes the living body look like it's wasting away, like a corpse. A dead body is death itself. Even the bodily fluids connected with creating life, when they were lost, pointed to life slipping away.

And the tabernacle was the place of the living God. The place of life. So anything that carried the shadow of death had to stay back from it, for a time.

Now notice what this means. Being unclean was not the same as being sinful. A mother who had just given birth hadn't done anything wrong. A man who had just buried his father had done something good. But they'd been close to the edge between life and death, and that edge had to be respected before they came near the source of life. Uncleanness was a state you were in, not a crime you'd committed. And for most kinds of uncleanness, it was temporary and could be cleansed.

That matters for one law in particular. People sometimes hear that a woman was unclean after childbirth and assume the Bible was ashamed of women or of babies. But think about it in Milgrom's terms. Childbirth involves a great loss of blood. It's the place where life and death come closest together, as any parent who's been in a delivery room knows. The law gave the new mother a period of separation, which in practice also meant a period of rest, away from the normal duties of worship and community. And it ended with an offering and a return. It wasn't a mark of shame. It was a recognition that she had just been standing at the very edge of life and death.

So the whole system taught a lesson, over and over, in the body. God is life. Death doesn't belong in his presence. And human beings, in a broken world, are always being brushed by death.

And now watch Jesus.

In Mark chapter one, a man with a skin disease comes to Jesus and kneels and says, if you are willing, you can make me clean. Under the law, you don't touch that man. If you do, you become unclean. But Jesus touches him. And instead of Jesus becoming unclean, the man becomes clean.

In Mark chapter five, a woman who has been bleeding for twelve years, unclean for twelve years, pushes through the crowd and touches the edge of Jesus' cloak. Under the law, she should have made him unclean. Instead, she's healed. Right after that, Jesus goes into a house where a little girl has died. He takes the dead girl by the hand. Under the law, touching a corpse makes you unclean. Instead, she gets up.

Do you see the pattern? Under the purity laws, uncleanness spread. Touch something unclean, and you became unclean. With Jesus, it's the other way around. Cleanness spreads from him. Life flows out of him into the places where death had a grip. The purity laws drew a careful line around death. Jesus walks right across the line and pushes death back.

That's why the purity laws matter, even though you don't keep them. They were teaching Israel to feel the problem. Death. Distance from God. And Jesus is the answer to the problem they were teaching.

How it lands in your actual week.

So what does any of this mean for a teacher in Ontario on a Tuesday?

Start with your devotions. Next time your reading plan lands you in Leviticus, don't skim with your eyes glazed. You don't need a verse by verse study. Ask three simple questions of any strange law. What line is this drawing? What is it protecting? And where does Jesus meet it? Those three questions will take you a long way. The fabrics law draws a line around holy things. The food laws protect Israel's identity. The goat law protects tenderness. The purity laws protect the place of life. And Jesus meets every one of them.

Now your classroom. Think about your Shifting Gears kids. A lot of them feel unclean, in a sense. Not by Leviticus. But they've been put outside the camp. Kicked out of classes. Labelled. Some of them have been told, in a hundred quiet ways, that good people keep their distance from them. And Jesus' whole direction was toward people like that. He didn't wait for the leper to clean himself up. He reached out first. You can't heal anyone. But you can move toward the kid other people move away from. A hand on the shoulder, a seat by the door, a word in the hallway that says, you're not outside the camp in here.

Next, your Civics and Careers class. These laws are a good reminder that rules carry meaning. Your students think rules are just adults being annoying. And sometimes they are. But often a rule packs real knowledge into a habit, like the flammables cabinet. When you explain the reason behind a rule in your shop, you're doing what we're doing with Leviticus. You're unpacking the knowledge inside the habit. Kids who understand the reason follow the rule better and resent it less.

And the online jab. Someone says you eat shrimp, so you can't take any of the Bible's laws seriously. Now you have an answer. The food laws were a boundary marker for Israel, and the New Testament itself says that boundary has moved. That's not Christians cherry picking. It's the Bible explaining its own story. The moral law, love God and love your neighbour, didn't expire. The fence around the nursery did, because the family grew.

Last, your table at home. You host people. You know that what you eat and who you eat with says something. Israel's menu said, we belong to God and we're separate. The church's table, after Acts chapter ten, says something new. Everyone is welcome here. Jew and Gentile, clean and unclean. Your dinner table can say that too. Who's the person nobody else would invite?
