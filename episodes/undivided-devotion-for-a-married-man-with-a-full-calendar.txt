Undivided Devotion: One Heart, A Full Calendar

Picture a Tuesday. You wake up early, you sit with your Bible, you pray with your wife. Then it's the classroom by nine fifteen, breakfast and the morning sheet, the first break at ten twenty five exactly. Then the drive, maybe IO, maybe a stop at the hardware store. Then home for an hour. Then MC. Then home again, late, and your wife is tired, and you're tired, and the day is done.

Every single thing on that list is good. Not one of them is sin. And yet you can get to the end of a day like that and feel like you were never fully anywhere. Your body was in the classroom while your mind was planning MC. Your body was at the dinner table while your mind was replaying a hard moment with a student. Your body was at MC while part of you was worried about whether your wife felt left behind.

That is the feeling Paul is talking about in First Corinthians seven. And the phrase you asked about, undivided devotion to the Lord, is his answer to it.

So here's the promise for this run. By the end, you'll see that undivided devotion is not about doing fewer good things. It's about doing all your good things with one heart pointed at one Lord. And you'll have one small practice to try this week.

We're going to take this in four ideas. First, what Paul actually means by divided. Second, the picture hidden in the Greek word for undivided. Third, why serving your wife is not a rival to devotion but part of it. And fourth, what research on attention can teach a busy man about doorways.

Let's start.

Here's the first idea. Paul is not scolding married people.

Let's hear the passage. First Corinthians seven, verses thirty two to thirty five. In the ESV it goes like this. "I want you to be free from anxieties. The unmarried man is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord. But the married man is anxious about worldly things, how to please his wife, and his interests are divided." Then he says the same about women. And then verse thirty five. "I say this for your own benefit, not to lay any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and to secure your undivided devotion to the Lord."

Now, when a married man reads that, it's easy to hear an accusation. It sounds like Paul is saying, you married guys are second class. Your heart is split. Too bad.

But listen to the tone again. "I want you to be free from anxieties." "I say this for your own benefit." "Not to lay any restraint upon you." That word restraint, in Greek, is a word for a noose or a halter, the rope you throw over an animal's neck. Paul is saying, I'm not trying to lasso you. I'm trying to help you.

And notice something else. Paul says the married man is concerned with how to please his wife. He doesn't call that sin. He calls it normal. It's what a married man should be doing. In chapter seven he's already told husbands and wives to give themselves to each other. In Ephesians he'll tell husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the church. So pleasing your wife is not the problem. It's an assignment.

The problem Paul names is the divided interest. The pull in two directions. And he's being honest that marriage adds real weight to that pull. Alistair Begg, preaching on this passage, put it in one short line, which we'll come back to at the end.

So what is divided, exactly? It's not your calendar. Your calendar is always going to have more than one thing on it. Paul himself was a tentmaker, a church planter, a letter writer, a traveller and a pastor to dozens of people. His calendar was full.

What's divided is the heart. A divided heart has two masters. It's pulled between pleasing God and pleasing someone or something else, and it feels the strain of that pull as anxiety.

That's why verse thirty two starts where it does. "I want you to be free from anxieties." The opposite of undivided devotion isn't being busy. It's being anxious. It's being torn.

Now, here's something that helps us read this passage more honestly. Paul himself was not free of this pull. In Second Corinthians eleven, after he lists shipwrecks and beatings and sleepless nights, he says this. "And, apart from other things, there is the daily pressure on me of my anxiety for all the churches."

Did you hear it? Anxiety. Same word family as First Corinthians seven. Paul was single, and he still felt the daily weight of caring for people. He wasn't floating above it all. He carried real people on his heart every day.

So undivided devotion doesn't mean you never feel the weight. Paul felt it. You'll feel it too. When a student is in crisis, you'll feel it. When your wife is hurting, you'll feel it. When someone in MC is drifting, you'll feel it. That's love. Love always carries weight.

The difference is where the weight goes. A divided heart carries all those weights in separate bags, pulling in separate directions. An undivided heart brings them all to one place. To one Lord. And lets him hold the bags.

There's a prayer in the Psalms that says this better than I can. Psalm eighty six, verse eleven. "Teach me your way, O Lord, that I may walk in your truth; unite my heart to fear your name."

Unite my heart. That's a prayer for undivided devotion, written about a thousand years before Paul. David knew his heart could split. So he didn't just try harder. He asked God to pull the pieces back together. That's a good prayer for a man with a full calendar. Unite my heart. Not shrink my life. Unite my heart.

So here's the first idea in one line. Paul isn't against your marriage or your full calendar. He's against your heart being pulled apart.

Here's the second idea. The word Paul uses for undivided has a story behind it.

The phrase in verse thirty five, undivided devotion, is really two Greek words. The first one is about sitting well beside something. Sitting near. Attending. Being close and settled. The second one means without distraction. Literally, not being dragged around.

And here's the part I want you to hear. That second word comes from a verb that means to be pulled in every direction. Dragged around. That verb shows up only one time in the whole New Testament. Do you know where?

Luke chapter ten, verse forty. "But Martha was distracted with much serving."

Martha. The woman with the full calendar. Jesus is in her house. There's a meal to make, people to host, a table to set. Every single thing Martha is doing is good. She's serving Jesus himself. And yet Luke says she was dragged around. Pulled in every direction.

And then there's her sister Mary, who is sitting at the Lord's feet, listening to his teaching.

So when Paul writes to the Corinthians and talks about undivided devotion, he's using language that almost paints Mary and Martha side by side. Sitting well beside the Lord, without being dragged around.

Now, here's what's important. Jesus doesn't tell Martha to stop serving. He doesn't say cooking is bad. He says, "Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her."

Did you catch the word anxious? It's the same family of word Paul uses in First Corinthians seven. Anxious about many things.

So the problem with Martha wasn't the serving. It was the anxiety. It was that the serving had become the center, and Jesus had moved to the edge. She was serving Jesus while not attending to Jesus.

That's the danger for a man like you. Not laziness. You're not lazy. The danger is Martha. Doing many good things for the Lord, with your heart dragged in every direction, while the Lord himself sits in the room waiting for you to look at him.

So here's the second idea in one line. Undivided devotion is Mary's posture, sitting near the Lord, carried into Martha's kitchen.

Let me pause and recap where we are, because we're about halfway.

So far we've got two things. One, Paul isn't against your marriage or your full calendar. He's against your heart being torn and anxious. Two, the word undivided paints a picture of Mary at Jesus' feet, not dragged around like Martha, even while there's real work to do.

Now let's get practical. Because you have a wife, and students, and a missional community. And you might be wondering, does undivided devotion to the Lord mean I care about them less?

Here's the third idea. Serving your wife is not a rival to devotion. It's one of the ways devotion shows up.

Here's a way to think about it. Imagine a wheel. In the center is the hub. Coming out from the hub are spokes. The spokes go in all different directions, but they're all connected to one center. That's what makes it a wheel instead of a pile of sticks.

A divided heart is a pile of sticks. There's your wife, and there's school, and there's MC, and there's God, and they're all competing for the same scarce space. God becomes one more stick in the pile. One more thing to fit in. Devotions at five thirty, check.

An undivided heart is a wheel. Christ is the hub. Your wife is a spoke. Your students are a spoke. MC is a spoke. They don't compete with him. They come out of him.

Paul says it this way in Colossians three. "Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men." Whatever you do. Not just the church stuff. The classroom stuff. The marriage stuff.

So what does that mean for your wife? It means that when you're loving your wife, you're not taking time away from the Lord. You're serving the Lord through her. Ephesians five says to love her as Christ loved the church. That's not a distraction from devotion. That is devotion, aimed at the person God put closest to you.

But here's the honest part. The same thing can be done with a divided heart or an undivided heart. You can serve your wife to please the Lord. Or you can serve your wife to manage her, to get the evening to go the way you want, to win, to be seen as a good husband. From the outside, those can look the same. Inside, one is a wheel and the other is a pile of sticks.

You told me something a while ago that I think is the clearest picture of this. You said your wife's favourite version of you is when you're with Darren. Because you're tender, gentle, understanding and soft with him.

Think about why that is. When you're with Darren, you're not managing him. You're not trying to fix him or optimize him. You're just with him. You're present, and you're gentle, and you're there for his good, not for your agenda. That's what undivided looks like with a person. It's not intensity. It's attention without an angle.

And your wife sees that and says, that's the man I want.

Here's a simple test you can use. When you're serving your wife, ask yourself, who is this for? If the honest answer is, it's for the Lord, and it's for her good, you're at the hub. If the honest answer is, it's so the evening goes my way, or so she'll finally see it my way, you've slipped into the pile of sticks. And the fix isn't guilt. It's turning back to the center. A quick, quiet, Lord, this is for you. Then a softer voice. Then a question instead of a correction.

There's one more thing worth saying here. Sometimes a man with a strong sense of devotion can use God as a weapon in his marriage without meaning to. Spiritual language becomes leverage. So the question for your marriage isn't, how do I love your wife less so I can love God more? The question is, how do I love your wife from the center, from the hub, the way I'm with Darren, instead of from the anxious pile of sticks?

Here's the fourth idea. And this one comes from research on attention. It's about doorways.

In 2009, a business school professor named Sophie Leroy published a study with a great title. "Why is it so hard to do my work?" She wanted to know what happens in our minds when we switch from one task to another.

Here's what she found. When you switch tasks, your attention doesn't fully switch with you. Part of your mind stays stuck on the thing you just left, especially if it's unfinished. She called this attention residue. Residue, like the film left in a pan after you cook. You've moved on, but something's still stuck.

And the study found that the more residue you carry, the worse you do at the next thing. You process information less carefully. You miss things.

Now think about your Tuesday. You leave the classroom with an unfinished worry about a student. That's residue. You walk in the door at home, and your wife is talking about her day, and you're nodding, but part of you is still at school. That's residue. You head to MC, and part of you is still processing that conversation with your wife that didn't quite land. Residue.

That's what divided feels like, from the inside. Not that you're doing too many things. It's that you're carrying all the previous things into the next thing.

But here's the hopeful part. Leroy did a later study with a colleague named Theresa Glomb. They tested something very simple. They called it a ready to resume plan. When people were interrupted, they took less than a minute to write down where they'd left off and what they'd do next when they came back.

That's it. A minute. And the study found that people who did this switched attention more fully, made better decisions and remembered more from the next task. Writing it down let their minds set it aside.

There was one more finding from this line of research that I think is really interesting. In one experiment, some people were told they'd have plenty of time to come back to their first task. Others were told they'd have very little time. The people under time pressure carried more residue, and did worse on the next task. The people who believed there was time to come back let go more easily, and did better.

Think about what that means. When you feel like there's never enough time, your mind clings harder. Scarcity makes you more divided, not less. But when you trust that there's a time and place to return to the unfinished thing, you can let it go and be present now.

For a Christian, that's not just a productivity trick. That's trust in the Lord of time. Your times are in his hands. Tomorrow belongs to him. The unfinished things are not lost. You can set them down.

Why does the writing part work? Because the mind holds on to unfinished things. It's afraid it'll forget. When you write it down, you're telling your mind, it's safe now. You can let go.

Now here's where I want to bring this back to faith. Because for a Christian, there's something even better than writing it down. It's handing it over.

First Peter five, verse seven. "Casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you." Same word family again. Anxieties. The very thing Paul wants you free from in First Corinthians seven.

That's not productivity. That's devotion. You're not just clearing your head. You're putting your work back in the hands of the one you did it for.

So here's the fourth idea in one line. Every doorway in your day is a chance to hand the last thing to God so you can be fully present for the next thing.
