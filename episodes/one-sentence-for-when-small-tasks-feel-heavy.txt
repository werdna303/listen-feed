Part one. Self talk is real, and it does something.

Let's start with whether this is even worth bothering about. Talking to yourself sounds a bit soft. Like a poster in a guidance office. Does it actually change anything?

First, a simple fact. Everybody talks to themselves. Not always out loud, but inside. Psychologists call it inner speech. And it starts early. If you've ever watched a little kid build something with blocks, you'll hear him narrate. Now this one goes here. No, not that one. A Russian psychologist from the early twentieth century, Lev Vygotsky, noticed this and argued that this out loud talk to oneself slowly goes underground as kids grow. It becomes the voice in your head. The kid who said, now this one goes here, becomes the man who thinks it.

So the question isn't whether you talk to yourself. You do. All day long. The question is whether the voice is helping. And on a heavy Thursday night, it usually isn't.

The strongest evidence comes from sport. Athletes and coaches have used self talk for a long time, so researchers have tested it a lot. In two thousand eleven, a team led by Antonis Hatzigeorgiadis, in Greece, pulled together the studies on self talk and sport performance. A little over thirty studies. Things like throwing, shooting, swimming, balance, endurance. And the study found a real, moderate benefit. People who used deliberate self talk tended to perform better than people who didn't. A later look at the research, more than a decade on, came to much the same conclusion.

But the interesting part is in the details. The study compared two kinds of self talk. Motivational self talk, which is the pep talk kind. Come on. You've got this. Let's go. And instructional self talk, which is the coaching kind. It tells you what to do. Elbow up. Eyes on the rim. Short steps.

Both helped. But for tasks that needed precision or were new to the person, the instructional kind helped more. The coaching beat the cheering. And self talk worked better when people practised it, rather than just being handed a phrase on the day.

You know this from your own running. On a hard hill, telling yourself, come on, you're tough, might help a little. But something like, short steps, drive the arms, works better, doesn't it? It gives your brain a job. It's not a feeling. It's an instruction.

And chess, if you think about it, is full of instructional self talk. Strong players train themselves to run a little script before they move. Checks, captures, threats. Three words. It's not motivational. Nobody says, believe in yourself, before playing a knight move. It's a coaching cue that tells your eyes where to look. And it catches blunders, because it gives the brain a job at exactly the moment it's most likely to get lazy. You've probably used something like it for years. That's self talk, and it works.

So the first ingredient of your sentence is this. It should tell you what to do, not just how to feel. Think about the best coaches you ever had. The ones who helped most in the moment probably didn't just yell, come on. They shouted something you could do. Eyes up. Stay low. Finish the play. Your sentence should sound like that coach, not like the crowd.

One line for part one. Self talk really does affect performance, and coaching words that tell you what to do tend to beat cheering words.

Part two. Talk to yourself like you'd talk to a friend.

Now here's the part that surprised me most when I first came across it. It's not just what you say to yourself. It's how you address yourself.

A psychologist named Ethan Kross, at the University of Michigan, has spent years studying this. In two thousand fourteen, he and his colleagues published a set of experiments with a plain title. Self talk as a regulatory mechanism. How you do it matters.

They put people in stressful situations. Like having to give a speech with very little time to prepare. Before the speech, some people were asked to think through their feelings using I. Why am I nervous? Others were asked to use their own name, or you. Why is Andrew nervous? Andrew, you can do this.

What did the study find? People who used their own name or you felt less anxious afterward. And when independent judges watched videos of their speeches, they rated them as better. A tiny change in grammar. A real change in how people handled the stress.

Kross calls this distanced self talk. When you use your own name, you step back a little. You see yourself the way you'd see someone else. And that matters, because most of us are much wiser about other people's problems than our own.

Kross and a colleague named Igor Grossmann tested exactly that, in a study they called Exploring Solomon's paradox. Named after King Solomon, famous for his wisdom, who still made a mess of his own life. In the study, people reasoned more wisely about a friend's relationship problem than about their own. They were more open minded, more humble, better at seeing other sides. But when people were asked to think about their own problem using their own name, or you, that gap closed. They got as wise about themselves as they were about their friend.

Now think about how you'd talk to a buddy who was standing in his garage on a Thursday night, staring at a pile of small jobs. Would you say, you're useless, why is everything piling up? Of course not. You'd say something like, hey, you're tired, it's been a long week, just do the washer fluid and call it a night. Kind. Practical. Specific.

That's the voice you want. And using your own name is one of the simplest ways to switch it on.

You see this with your students. A kid who's spiralling about a failed test can't hear himself. But if you ask him, what would you tell your little brother if he failed a test? he suddenly gets sensible. You've given him distance. And distance brings wisdom.

So the second ingredient of your sentence is this. Use your name, or you. Talk to yourself like a coach talking to a player, not like a player talking to himself.

And if using your own name feels strange, and for a lot of people it does at first, you works too. You've got this. You can do one more. The research used both, and the point is the same. Step outside your own head for a second, and talk to yourself from the sideline.

It's also useful in the moment, in a heated situation. A kid in your shop mouths off, and you feel the heat rise. Try a quick, silent, distanced question. Andrew, what does this kid need right now? It takes a second. And that second of distance can be the difference between a snap and a steady response. You're not just calming down. You're asking the wise friend version of yourself to take the wheel.

One line for part two. Using your own name or you creates a little distance, and that distance makes you calmer and wiser about your own problems.

Part three. Believable beats big.

Now, if self talk works, you might be tempted to go big. Stand in front of the mirror and say, I am a productive, powerful, unstoppable man of God! And here's where the research gives a warning.

In two thousand nine, a psychologist at the University of Waterloo, right there in Ontario, named Joanne Wood, ran a set of studies with two colleagues. They had people repeat a positive statement to themselves. I am a lovable person. And they looked at how people felt afterward.

For people who already felt good about themselves, the statement helped a little. But for people with low self esteem, it backfired. They ended up feeling worse than people who didn't repeat it at all.

Why? The best explanation is that a statement you don't believe triggers an argument in your head. You say, I am a lovable person, and some part of you answers, no I'm not, remember that thing I did? The statement doesn't drown out the doubt. It calls it to the microphone.

So big, glossy affirmations can work against you, especially on a night when you're already down on yourself. If you're standing in front of the laundry thinking you're useless, saying I am unstoppable will probably start a fight you'll lose.

There's another study I want to mention, because you might see it in productivity books, and I want you to know it's shaky. In two thousand ten, researchers reported that asking yourself a question, Will I do this?, worked better than telling yourself, I will do this. It got a lot of attention. But when other researchers tried to repeat it, the question form didn't beat the statement. Self talk helped, but the grammar trick didn't hold. So don't worry about finding magic words. There aren't any.

What you want is a sentence that's true. Something you can actually believe on a bad night. Not, I'm unstoppable. More like, you can do one small thing. That's believable. Even on your worst night, you know it's true.

So the third ingredient is this. Keep it believable. Smaller and true beats bigger and false.

There's another line of research that points the same way, from the other direction. If big affirmations don't help, surely being tough on yourself works? Plenty of people think the harsh voice is what keeps them going. If I'm not hard on myself, I'll get lazy.

Two researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, Juliana Breines and Serena Chen, tested something close to this in two thousand twelve. They looked at what happens after people fail. In one of their experiments, students did poorly on a hard test. Then some were guided to think about their failure with kindness and understanding, what researchers call self compassion. Others were guided to boost their self esteem, or given nothing. Then everyone got a chance to study for a second test. And the self compassion group studied longer. Not shorter. Being kind to themselves didn't make them lazy. It made them more willing to try again.

The reason seems to be this. When you're harsh with yourself, failure feels dangerous, so you avoid looking at it. When you're kind, failure is just information, and you can afford to go back to it. That's the same thing we said about grace in the ignored laws series. Security makes it safe to look at your mistakes and try again.

So your sentence should be kind as well as believable. Not a pep rally. Not a drill sergeant. A good friend.

One line for part three. Big affirmations can backfire when you don't believe them, so choose words that are small, true, and believable.
