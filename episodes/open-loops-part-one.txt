Part one. The waiter and the student.

Let's start with a story. It is the 1920s. A psychologist named Kurt Lewin is sitting in a café with his students. Lewin is a big name. He's one of the founders of what we now call social psychology. And the story goes that he noticed something about the waiter.

The waiter was taking big orders. Lots of people, lots of items, no notepad. And he remembered everything perfectly. Who had the coffee, who had the cake, who owed what. Impressive. But then, after the bill was paid, someone asked the waiter what the table had ordered. And he couldn't remember. It was gone. Wiped.

Now, I want to be straight with you. That café story is the kind of thing that gets told and retold, and some historians treat it as more legend than record. So hold it loosely. But it's a good picture of the idea, and it's the picture that got the research started.

Because one of Lewin's students was a young woman named Bluma Zeigarnik. And she turned the waiter's trick into an experiment. She gave people a bunch of small tasks. Puzzles, little bits of handwork, simple problems. Some tasks she let them finish. Others she interrupted partway through. Then, later, she asked them to recall the tasks they'd done.

What she found, in her paper published in 1927, was that people tended to remember the interrupted tasks better than the finished ones. The unfinished stuff stuck. The finished stuff faded. That became known as the Zeigarnik effect. Unfinished tasks stay more available in memory than finished ones.

Lewin had a way of explaining it. He talked about tension. When you set out to do something, you create a kind of tension in your mind, a pull toward the goal. When you finish, the tension releases. When you get interrupted, the tension stays. It's like a spring that's been wound up and never let go. And that leftover tension keeps the task close to the surface.

Now, you asked what's going on biologically. So let me give you the honest version. Lewin's tension was a metaphor. Nobody has found a little spring in the brain. But modern psychology has a fairly plain way of saying something similar. When you commit to a goal, your brain keeps that goal active in the background. It stays a little bit switched on, ready to grab your attention when something related shows up. Walk past the truck, and the goal lights up. Smell motor oil at the gas station, and it lights up. That's not a flaw. That's a feature.

Think about why a brain would work that way. For most of human history, the things you left unfinished were things that mattered. The fence that wasn't mended. The field that wasn't harvested. The tool that was half made. A brain that let those go the moment you got distracted would be a brain that let a lot of important things slip. So the brain keeps a quiet watch on unfinished business. It's like a background app on your phone that keeps checking for updates. Useful. But it uses battery.

And here's the part that connects to your anxiety. That background watch doesn't know the difference between a big loop and a small one. The truck and the Marketplace message both get a little slice of attention. One loop is fine. Five is fine. But thirty, forty, fifty open loops, each one with a little background process running, and you start to feel it. Not as thirty separate thoughts. As a general hum. A low sense that something is slipping. That hum is a lot of what people mean when they say they feel overwhelmed. It's not one huge problem. It's a crowd of little ones, each tapping you on the shoulder.

There's also a good reason the tapping gets louder at night. During the day, your attention is full. Students, lessons, the drive home. The background goals can't get a word in. But when you lie down and the room goes quiet, there's finally space. And all the little background processes say, now's my chance. That's why the Marketplace message shows up at ten at night and not during third period.

That's the classic story, plus the modern gloss. And it's a lovely story. It explains your truck. It explains the marking. It explains why, at ten at night, your brain suddenly remembers the Marketplace message.

So the big idea of part one is this. Unfinished goals keep pulling on your mind, because your mind was built to finish what it started.

Part two. What held up, and what didn't.

Now here's where I have to be the honest friend and not just the fun one.

The Zeigarnik effect is famous. It's in textbooks. It's in productivity blogs. It's in marketing, because TV shows end on cliffhangers for a reason. But when researchers tried to repeat Zeigarnik's experiment over the following decades, the results were mixed. Sometimes people remembered the unfinished tasks better. Sometimes there was no difference. Sometimes it even flipped, and people remembered the finished tasks better.

And recently, in 2025, two researchers at the University of Bern, Romain Ghibellini and Beat Meier, pulled together decades of these studies into one big meta-analysis. A meta-analysis is just a study of studies. You gather all the experiments you can find, and you ask, on average, what's really there.

Here's what the study found. The memory part, the idea that you remember unfinished tasks better, did not hold up as a general rule. Across the studies, people recalled finished and unfinished tasks about the same. So the famous version of the Zeigarnik effect, the memory advantage, is shaky. It shows up in some conditions, not in general.

But here's the part that matters for you. There's a cousin of the Zeigarnik effect, discovered by another of Lewin's students, Maria Ovsiankina. She looked not at memory, but at behaviour. She interrupted people in the middle of a task and then gave them free time. And what did they do? A lot of them went back and finished the task. Nobody told them to. They just did. That's the urge to resume.

And in that same 2025 meta-analysis, the urge to resume held up. People really do tend to go back to unfinished things. The pull is real. It's the memory claim that's weak.

So let me put that plainly. Your brain may not store your unfinished tasks any better than your finished ones. But your brain does seem to want to go back to them. The loop doesn't necessarily stick in memory. It tugs on your will.

That actually fits your experience better, if you think about it. It's not that you remember the truck in perfect detail. It's that the truck keeps tugging. It keeps saying, go back. Finish me. And that tug can feel like anxiety, especially for someone like you who, in your own words, has always had "this anxiety of not wanting to forget."

The big idea of part two, in one line. The memory part is shaky, but the pull to go back and finish is real.

Part three. Making a plan can quiet the pull.

Now we come to the study that I think is the most useful one for your life. It's from 2011, by two psychologists, E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister. The paper's title is great. It's called "Consider it done!"

Here's the setup. They wanted to know what happens in your head when you have an unfinished goal hanging over you. So they got people thinking about an important goal they hadn't finished yet. Something real. An exam to study for, a task they'd been putting off.

Then they gave people other jobs to do. One was reading a passage. While they read, the researchers checked how often their minds wandered off to that unfinished goal. And sure enough, people with an unfinished goal on their minds had more of these intrusive thoughts. Their minds kept drifting back. They also did worse on an unrelated word puzzle, as if part of their brain was busy elsewhere.

That's the nagging. That's the truck tapping you on the shoulder in the middle of second period.

But here's the twist. Some people were asked to do one more thing before the reading. They were asked to make a specific plan for that unfinished goal. Not finish it. Just plan it. When will I do it. Where. What exactly will I do.

And for those people, the intrusive thoughts dropped way down. In the study, making a specific plan more or less eliminated the extra mind-wandering and the interference. They hadn't done the task. They'd only decided how and when they would do it. And their minds let go.

The authors' explanation is simple and, I think, kind of beautiful. Your brain isn't actually demanding that you finish the task right now. It's demanding that you not forget it. Once you've made a real plan, the brain can say, okay, it's handled, I'll pick it back up at the right time. It can stop guarding the task. It can stand down.

Think of it like a guard on a night shift. As long as nobody has taken over the post, the guard can't sleep. He keeps pacing. Making a plan is like the next shift showing up. The guard can finally go home.

You already know this from chess, by the way. Think about a game where you're in a tricky middlegame and you can't decide on a plan. Every move, your mind is scattered. You're calculating three ideas at once, half of them going nowhere, and you feel the pressure. Then, at some point, you settle on a plan. Push the kingside pawns. Get the knight to that outpost. And something changes. Your mind gets quieter. You still haven't won anything. The position is just as open as it was. But now your brain knows what it's doing next, so it stops spinning on every possibility. That's the same thing the study found. A plan doesn't finish the game. It settles the mind enough to play it.

Or think about a long run. If you head out with no route in mind, part of your brain keeps working the question the whole time. Left here or straight? How far am I going? When you've picked the route before you leave, you just run. The decision has been made, so your mind is free.

Now, two honest notes about that study. First, it was the plan that mattered, not just writing the goal down. A vague note, "fix truck," isn't a plan. A plan has a when and a what. Second, the effect on feelings was smaller than the effect on thoughts. In other words, people didn't necessarily feel calmer about the goal. But their minds stopped drifting to it. They could focus. So it's less like a pill for worry, and more like clearing the noise off the line.

Also, this is one research team and one set of studies. Good studies, published in a serious journal, but psychology has learned to be humble about single findings. So treat it as strong evidence, not as a law of nature.

The big idea of part three. You don't have to finish a loop to quiet it. You have to make a real plan for it.

Part four. Why you're obsessed with systems.

You've said it yourself. "I'm obsessed with setting up systems." Lists, apps, trackers, checklists for your class. And you've said, "Forever and always I've always wanted a reminder."

Here's a thought I want you to chew on. What if your obsession with systems is your brain trying to close loops?

Think about what a system really is, for you. It's a place to put the loop so your brain doesn't have to hold it. It's the next shift showing up for the guard. Every time you set up a list, or a reminder, or a new app, you're trying to give your mind permission to let go.

And there's a practical writer who built a whole method on exactly that idea. His name is David Allen, and his book is called "Getting Things Done." It first came out in 2001, and a lot of people who love systems have read it, maybe you included.

Allen is where the phrase "open loops" really took off. He uses it for anything you've committed to that isn't finished. A phone call you owe. A part you need to order. A student you meant to check in with. And his core claim is that these loops drain your attention as long as they live only in your head. His answer is what he calls a trusted system. A place outside your head where every open loop gets written down, along with the very next physical action you'd take on it. And then you review that system regularly, so your brain learns to trust it.

He has a phrase for the goal, borrowed from martial arts. "Mind like water." A calm mind that responds to what comes, in proportion, without overreacting.

Now, I want to be careful here. David Allen is a practitioner, not a researcher. "Getting Things Done" is a method from years of coaching people, not a set of experiments. But notice how well it lines up with that "Consider it done" study. The study says a specific plan quiets the pull. Allen says, write down the next action and put it in a system you trust. Those are close cousins. The study gives Allen's idea some backing, at least for the planning part.

So here's the upside of your system love. When it works, a system really can take loops off your mind. You're not crazy for wanting one. You're doing what your brain is begging you to do. Give me somewhere to put this.

But here's the trap. And you probably already know it.

Building the system can feel like closing the loop. Even when nothing actually got done.

Think about it. When you set up a new to-do app, and you spend an evening making categories, choosing colours, entering every task, what does that feel like? It feels great. It feels like relief. It feels like, finally, I've got it handled. And part of that relief is real, because you've made some plans. But part of it is borrowed. Your brain gets a little hit of closure from the act of organizing. And the truck is still in the driveway with the hood up.

Here's why that happens. Remember, the brain wants assurance that the task won't be forgotten. Setting up a system gives you that assurance in a big, satisfying rush. So the pull goes quiet for a while. But if the system doesn't lead to doing, the loops start tugging again. And then what do you do? You guessed it. You rebuild the system. New app. New categories. New rush. And around we go.

It's a bit like reorganizing your toolbox instead of fixing the truck. A tidy toolbox is good. It helps. But at some point you have to pick up the wrench.

Here's a test you can use. After you work on your system, ask yourself one question. Did this make the next action on something clearer, or did it just make my list prettier? If it made the next action clearer, great, that's real planning, and the study backs you up. If it just made it prettier, you've been reorganizing the toolbox.

And here's the other side of the trap. A system you don't trust doesn't quiet anything. If you have three apps, a notebook, a whiteboard in your classroom, and some sticky notes on the dash, your brain doesn't actually believe any one of them has the full picture. So it keeps guarding everything. The guard can't go home if he doesn't trust the next shift. That's why Allen keeps talking about trust, and about reviewing the system. Fewer places, checked regularly, beats many places checked never.

Now, you mentioned something else in your notes. You liked the idea of having one category, and not starting a new loop in that category until the last one was closed. You said that actually sounds really good. Hold onto that. We're going to spend all of part four of this series on it, one loop at a time, highest priority first. For now, just notice why it appeals to you. It's another way of reducing the number of guards on duty. Fewer open loops at once means less tugging.

The big idea of part four. Your love of systems is your brain trying to close loops, which is good, as long as the system leads to doing and not just to feeling done.

How it lands in your actual week.

Let's walk through your week with this lens. Four loops. The truck, the marking, the Marketplace listing, and the to-do app itself.

Start with the truck. The loop in your head is "fix truck." That's not a plan. That's a cloud. Your brain can't stand down on a cloud, because it doesn't know what to watch for. So turn it into a next physical action. Something like, "Saturday after breakfast, bolt the new part on, then torque it." Or, if you're missing something, "Wednesday after school, stop at the parts store for the gasket." Now there's a when and a what. The guard can go home until Saturday.

Next, the marking. Half-finished marking is a nasty loop, because it's sitting right there in your bag. Every time you see the bag, tap tap tap. So give it a plan. "Tomorrow, during my prep, mark the last eight from where the green sticky note is." Notice how specific that is. You know where you'll start. You know when. Your brain can stop holding the whole stack.

Third, the Marketplace listing. This one is sneaky, because it's small. A message sitting unanswered for three days doesn't seem like a big deal. But small loops add up. And honestly, some loops can just be closed right away. David Allen has a rule of thumb for this. If something takes about two minutes or less, just do it now. Answering "Yes, it's still available, I can meet Thursday after five" takes thirty seconds. That's not a loop to plan. That's a loop to close. So close it, and enjoy the real thing, not the borrowed feeling.

And fourth, the to-do app. This is the loop about loops. You've rebuilt it more than once. So here's the gentle question. Is the app helping you name next actions, or is it the place you go to feel organized? If you notice yourself opening it to rearrange instead of to act, that's the trap. And the fix isn't a better app. The fix is making each entry an actual next step and then doing one.

Let me add one thing about your students, because you work with kids who struggle. A lot of your students live with open loops they can't close. Missed assignments. Credits they don't have. Stuff at home that's way beyond them. And they don't have a system. So the tug just turns into noise, or into shutting down. Sometimes what looks like not caring is actually a kid drowning in loops. The same tool can help them. Not "get it all done." Just, "What's the very next thing, and when will you do it?" Helping a kid name one next step, with a time attached, may quiet more of the noise than a lecture ever will.

And here's a word for the side of you that does devotions at five thirty every morning. There is a kind of loop that no system closes. Worries about the future, about your kids, your students, your church. Things that aren't tasks at all. A plan can quiet a task. It can't carry a worry that was never yours to carry. Alistair Begg, in a Truth For Life sermon called "A Biblical Approach to Anxiety," points out that in First Peter chapter five, we're not told to hand God every trouble so they disappear, but "to cast the anxiety which the troubles cause." That's a different kind of parking. Some loops go on the list. Some go in prayer. Part of wisdom is knowing which is which.

One thing to try.

Here's your one thing. Only one. A nightly "park it" note.

Each night, before bed, take a few minutes. Grab one piece of paper, or one note on your phone, the same one every night. Write down every open loop that's tugging at you right now. The truck. The marking. The listing. The kid you meant to call home about. Whatever's there.

Then, for each one, write the very next physical action, and when you'll do it. Not the whole project. Just the next thing your hands will do. "Order the gasket online, tomorrow at lunch." "Text the buyer back, tonight, right now." "Call home for that student, Thursday during prep."

If something takes two minutes, don't park it. Do it.

And then, and this is the important part, close the note and say to yourself, out loud if you want, "It's parked." Consider it done. Not done done. Planned done.

Why this works, based on what we've talked about. It's the "Consider it done" study in practice. You're giving each loop a when and a what, which is the very thing that quieted intrusive thoughts in the experiment. It's David Allen's trusted system, shrunk down to one page. And because it's one note, the same every night, your brain can learn to trust it. One place. Checked every day.

And here's how you'll know if it's working. Not by how organized the note looks. By two things. First, do the thoughts stop tapping you at ten at night? And second, the next day, do you actually do one of the parked actions? If the note gets longer every night and nothing gets done, you've slipped back into reorganizing the toolbox. If even one action happens each day, you're out of the trap.

Try it for one week. Just one note, every night.
