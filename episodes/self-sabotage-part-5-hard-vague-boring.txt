Self-Sabotage, Part 5: Hard, Vague, Boring

Cold open.

A few days ago you wrote yourself a short note. Eleven words. "Don't do things for 3 reasons: 1) hard 2) vague 3) boring."

That note is better than most self-help books. It is short, it is honest, and it is a diagnosis. You looked at the pile of things you keep not doing, and instead of saying "I'm lazy" or "I'm broken," you named three causes. Hard. Vague. Boring.

Here is the good news. Each of those three has a fix that research has tested. And under all three sits a fourth thing, which is how you talk to yourself when you miss anyway. That fourth thing is where the Bible has the last word.

So here is the promise for this episode. By the end of this run you will have one fix for hard, one fix for vague, one fix for boring, and a better answer for the morning after you fail than shame.

This is part five of the self-sabotage series. The earlier parts were about why we get in our own way. This one is about what stops it.

Section one: hard. shrink it.

Let's start with hard.

When a task feels hard, your brain is not really reacting to the whole task. It is reacting to the first step. More exactly, it is reacting to how big and unclear the first step looks. If the first step looks like a cliff, you don't start. If it looks like a curb, you step up without thinking.

So the fix for hard is not more willpower. The fix is to shrink the first step until it is almost silly.

Think about the garage. "Organize the garage" is a cliff. Nobody wakes up and organizes a garage. "Carry the one tote by the door to the shelf" is a curb. You can do that in your work clothes on the way in from the car.

Or think about a report you need to write for school. "Write the report" is a cliff. "Open the file and type the student's first initial and today's date" is a curb. It sounds ridiculous. That is the point. The ridiculous step is the one you actually take, and once you are in the file, the next step is usually easier than you feared.

There's a reason this works. Starting is the most expensive part of almost any task. Once you are moving, the cost drops. Psychologists sometimes talk about this as the gap between intending and initiating. People who fail at goals usually don't fail in the middle. They fail at the door. They never get started, or they start too late.

You already know this about your students. When one of them stares at a full page of math and shuts down, you don't give a speech about effort. You fold the page so only one question shows. Or you say, "Just do the first one, and I'll check it." You shrink it. You are good at doing this for a teenager. The work of this section is doing it for yourself, just as kindly.

Here's a test you can use. Ask: "Could I do the first step in under two minutes, without deciding anything?" If the answer is no, the step is still too big. Shrink it again.

And notice the second half of that test. Without deciding anything. A lot of what makes a task feel hard is not effort. It's decisions. Which tote goes where. What to say in the email. Where to start the report. Decisions are tiring, and a task full of hidden decisions feels much harder than it is. So when you shrink a task, pull the decisions out of the first step. Make the first step pure motion.

One line to hold onto: hard usually means the first step is too big. Shrink it until it's a curb.

Section two: vague. make it an if-then.

Here's the second idea. Vague.

Vague is sneakier than hard. A vague task doesn't feel scary. It just never comes up. "Get in shape." "Be more patient with my wife." "Write more thank-you cards." "Call that friend." These are good intentions. And they sit there for months, because nothing about them tells you when.

This is where the work of a psychologist named Peter Gollwitzer comes in. He spent his career on a simple tool he called implementation intentions. That's a fancy name for an if-then plan.

A goal says what you want. "I want to write more thank-you cards." An implementation intention says when, where and how, in a specific format. "If it's Friday after the students leave, then I take one card from my desk drawer and write it before I pack up."

The format is: If situation X happens, then I will do behaviour Y.

Why does that small change matter? Gollwitzer's answer is that the if-then plan hands control to the situation. You are no longer relying on remembering, or on being in the mood. You've linked a cue to an action ahead of time, so when the cue shows up, the action comes to mind on its own. The decision has already been made. You just carry it out.

This is not a fringe idea. In 2006, Gollwitzer and a colleague named Paschal Sheeran pulled together the research on it. They looked at ninety four studies with around eight thousand people. Across all of them, people who made if-then plans did noticeably better at reaching their goals than people who just set the goal. The effect was in the medium to large range, which for psychology is a big deal. Simple plans, a real difference. And it showed up in all sorts of goals: health, study, getting started on things, and staying on track when distracted.

So the study found that if-then planning, a tiny habit of mind, made goals noticeably more likely to happen. That's the headline.

Now, there are two parts to a good if-then plan, and both matter.

The "if" has to be a real, noticeable moment. "If I have time" is not a cue. You never notice that you have time. "If I sit down in the car after work" is a cue. "If I hear the kettle click" is a cue. "If the last student walks out the door" is a cue. Good cues are things you can't miss.

The "then" has to be concrete and small. This is where section one comes back. "Then I'll be patient" is not an action. "Then I'll take one breath before I answer" is an action. "Then I'll work on the garage" is fuzzy. "Then I'll carry one tote" is clear.

Here's something else the research found that you'll appreciate. If-then plans work especially well for the moments where you tend to sabotage. Gollwitzer and others found you can make plans aimed straight at your weak spot. "If I notice I'm about to make a quick move without checking for a better one, then I'll ask myself, what else could I do here." You've probably noticed that's a chess move, and also a life move. That is exactly the kind of plan this research says helps. You name the trap ahead of time and you put a small action right at the edge of it.

Let me give you one more example from a school day. Say you want to stop ending the afternoon in a scramble. The vague version is "be more organized at the end of the day." The if-then version is "If the room is empty after dismissal, then I write tomorrow's first task on a sticky note and put it on the keyboard." That's it. The cue is clear. The action is tiny. And tomorrow morning, the first step is already decided.

One line to hold onto: vague means there's no when. Write it as "if this, then that."

Halfway recap.

Let's stop and gather up what we have so far.

You said you don't do things for three reasons: hard, vague and boring.

For hard, shrink the first step until it's a curb, not a cliff, and take the decisions out of it.

For vague, turn the goal into an if-then plan. A cue you can't miss, and a small action you'll do when it shows up.

Two down. Now boring.

Section three: boring. pair it.

Here's the third idea.

Boring is the most honest of the three. Some things are not hard and not vague. You know exactly what to do and when. You just don't want to, because it's dull. Filing paperwork. Timesheets. Cleaning out the inbox. Laundry. Stretching. Sorting receipts.

The usual advice is "just discipline yourself." That works a little, for a little while.

A better idea comes from a researcher at the Wharton School named Katy Milkman. She calls it temptation bundling. The idea is simple. Take something you want to do but shouldn't overdo, and something you should do but don't want to. Then only allow yourself the first one while you're doing the second one.

Her famous study was at a university gym. The researchers gave people gripping audiobooks, the kind of page-turners you can't stop listening to. One group could only listen to those audiobooks at the gym. The books were locked on a device that stayed at the gym. So if you wanted to know what happened next in the story, you had to go work out.

The result was that this group went to the gym about half again as often as the comparison group in the first weeks. About fifty percent more. That's a big jump for something so simple.

But there's a second finding that matters just as much. Over the Thanksgiving break, gym visits dropped for everyone, including the bundling group. The effect faded once routines were broken. So temptation bundling is a real help, not a magic fix. It makes the boring thing pleasant enough to start. It doesn't run forever on its own. You have to restart it after a break, like after a holiday, a sick week or a busy stretch.

What does this look like in your life? You already do a version of it. You listen to these episodes on your run. The run is good for you. The episode is the thing you look forward to. That's a bundle.

You could build more. A favourite podcast only while you fold laundry. A good coffee only while you do timesheets. A playlist you love only while you sort the garage. The rule is the key. The treat has to be only for the boring task. If you can have it anytime, it stops pulling you toward the task.

There's a quieter version too, and it's worth naming. You can pair boring with people. Doing a dull job next to someone else makes it less dull. Folding laundry while your wife tells you about her day. Cleaning up after a church event with a friend. Even a student working beside you on a task they don't love goes better than a student alone at a desk. Company is a kind of bundle.

One line to hold onto: boring needs a partner. Bundle it with something you like, and keep the treat for that task only.

Section four: the morning after. self-compassion over shame.

So far we have three fixes. Shrink hard. Make vague an if-then. Pair boring.

Now here's the honest part. You'll still miss. You'll make the if-then plan and walk right past the cue. You'll shrink the step and still not take it. The bundle will fade after a holiday.

And what happens next matters more than almost anything else in this episode. Because how you treat yourself after a miss decides whether you get back up tomorrow, or whether the miss turns into a slide.

There are two common reactions to failing. One is shame. "I always do this. I'm never going to change. What's wrong with me." The other is what researchers call self-compassion.

Self-compassion has been studied most by a psychologist named Kristin Neff. She describes it in three parts. Kindness toward yourself instead of harsh judgment. Seeing your failure as part of being human, not proof that you're uniquely broken. And noticing the painful feeling clearly, without blowing it up or pushing it away.

Now here's the worry most driven people have, and maybe you have it too. "If I'm kind to myself, won't I just let myself off the hook? Isn't the harsh voice what keeps me going?"

A study from the University of California, Berkeley, tested exactly that. The researchers were Juliana Breines and Serena Chen, in 2012. In one experiment they gave students a hard vocabulary test, hard enough that most people did poorly. Then they split them into groups. One group got a self-compassion message, something like: it's common to struggle with tests like this, try not to be too hard on yourself. Another group got a self-esteem boost: you must be smart if you got into this school. A third group got nothing extra.

Then they gave everyone a chance to study for a second test, as long as they wanted.

The self-compassion group studied longer than the others. Not shorter. Longer. Being kind to themselves about failing did not make them lazy. It made them more willing to try again.

In other parts of the same research, people who thought about a past moral mistake with self-compassion were more motivated to make it right and to not repeat it. Kindness after failure made people more likely to change, not less.

Why would that be? Here's the simple version. Shame makes failure about who you are. If the problem is who you are, then there's nothing to fix, only something to hide. So you avoid the thing. You don't open the file. You don't look at the inbox. Avoidance is self-sabotage's best friend.

Self-compassion makes failure about what happened. If the problem is what happened, then you can look at it, learn from it, and try again. It's safe to look.

Think of the way you'd talk to a student who missed a deadline because their week fell apart. You wouldn't say, "You're hopeless." You'd say something like, "Okay. That happened. What's the smallest piece we can get done today?" That is self-compassion, and it's also good teaching. The research says the same voice works on you.

One line to hold onto: shame makes you hide from the task. Kindness makes it safe to try again.

Section five: what scripture says about growing out of it.

Now let's go deeper than research can.

Self-compassion is helpful, but as a Christian you might feel a tension. Isn't the Bible serious about sin and failure? Shouldn't I feel bad? And where does real change come from, if not from trying harder?

Two passages speak right into this. James chapter one, and Romans seven into eight.

Start with James. James opens his letter with a strange line. "Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds, for you know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness. And let steadfastness have its full effect, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing."

Notice what James assumes. He assumes growth takes time and pressure. Steadfastness has to have its full effect. It's a process. He is not surprised that you are not finished yet.

A few verses later he says: "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him." Without reproach. That means God doesn't scold you for asking. When you're stuck, the first move is to ask, and he gives without a lecture. That's not permission to be lazy. It's a picture of a Father who is generous with people who are still learning.

And later in the chapter James says something that sounds a lot like an if-then plan. "Be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves." He says a person who hears but doesn't act is like someone who looks in a mirror, walks away, and forgets what he looks like. James cares about the gap between intending and doing. That's the same gap Gollwitzer studied. Faith is meant to show up in concrete action, at concrete times.

So James gives you two things at once. A high bar: be a doer, not just a hearer. And a patient God: he gives wisdom without reproach, and he expects growth to take steadfastness over time.

Now Romans seven. This is the passage every Christian who has ever sabotaged themselves recognizes. Paul writes: "For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate." And again: "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing."

That is self-sabotage, described by an apostle. Paul is not a lazy man. He's one of the most driven people who ever lived. And he still feels the gap between wanting and doing.

Then comes the cry: "Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?"

And then, right away, the answer: "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!"

And then chapter eight opens with one of the great sentences in the Bible. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."

Here's why this matters for this episode. The research says shame makes you hide and kindness makes it safe to try again. Romans goes further. For a believer, the final verdict on your failures is already in. No condemnation. That's not you being nice to yourself. That's God's own judgment, based on what Christ has done, not on how well your week went.

That changes the morning after. When you miss the if-then plan, or let the inbox pile up, or make a quick move without thinking, you don't have to decide whether you are condemned. That question is settled. Which frees you to do the practical thing: look at what happened, shrink the next step, and try again.

And Romans eight goes on to say where the power for change comes from. Not from the law, and not from trying harder on your own, but from the Spirit. Paul says those who live by the Spirit are being changed. Growth is real, and it's God's work in you, even as you show up and take the small steps.

So put the two together. James says: be a doer, and expect it to take steadfastness. Romans says: you will still feel the gap, but there's no condemnation, and the Spirit is at work. That's the Christian version of self-compassion. It's not "go easy on yourself because you're great." It's "you are not great, you are forgiven, and you are being changed. So get up and take the next small step."

There is a real difference between those two. Worldly self-compassion can feel like lowering the bar. The gospel keeps the bar high and puts a hand on your back.

One line to hold onto: no condemnation is not a lower bar. It's the floor that makes it safe to climb.

How it lands in your week.

Let's bring this home.

In the classroom: when a student shuts down, you already shrink the task. Try naming the three reasons out loud with students sometime, in your own words. "Is this hard, is it fuzzy, or is it just boring?" Kids can answer that. And each answer has a different fix. Hard gets a smaller piece. Fuzzy gets a clear first step. Boring gets a partner or a reward after. It's a quiet way to teach them that being stuck has causes, and causes can be fixed. That's a moral lesson too, about not giving up on yourself.

At home: pick one thing on your list that keeps sliding. The garage, a repair, a stack of papers. Ask which of the three it is. Usually it's more than one. Shrink it, give it an if-then, and pair it with something you like.

With money and paperwork: timesheets and receipts are almost always boring, not hard. So they need a bundle, not a plan. A good drink, a favourite podcast, a set time each week.

In faith: when you notice you've failed again, try praying Romans seven, twenty four and twenty five back to God. "Wretched man that I am. Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord." Then ask for wisdom, the way James says, knowing he gives without reproach. Then take one small step.

One thing to try.

Only one. Here it is.

Take your own note, "Don't do things for 3 reasons: hard, vague, boring," and turn it into a single if-then plan.

"If I notice I'm putting something off, then I ask: is it hard, vague or boring?"

That's the whole thing. One cue, one question. You don't need to fix it right then. Just naming which one it is will usually show you the fix. Hard, shrink it. Vague, give it a when. Boring, pair it.

Write that if-then plan on a card and put it somewhere you'll see it, maybe in your desk with the thank-you cards. Try it for one week.

Closing recap.

Here's the whole episode in three sentences.

You don't do things because they're hard, vague or boring, so shrink the hard ones, turn the vague ones into if-then plans, and pair the boring ones with something you like.

When you miss anyway, kindness helps you try again where shame makes you hide, and the research backs that up.

And for the believer, the deepest answer is Romans eight: no condemnation, the Spirit at work, and a God who gives wisdom without reproach, so you can get up and take the next small step.

Let me close with a line from Alistair Begg, of Truth For Life, writing on Romans seven and the frustration of our ongoing failures. He says: "Do not suppress or ignore your disappointment at your ongoing flaws and failings." And then: "Let it drive you back to Jesus, in gratitude and relief."
