Part one. Stubborn is usually a shield.

Let's start with what's underneath. Because when a kid crosses his arms and says, this is stupid, it looks like defiance. And sometimes it is. But very often, it's something else wearing defiance as a costume.

A psychologist named Martin Covington spent years studying why students avoid effort. And he came up with what's called self worth theory. The basic idea is this. Kids, like adults, want to protect their sense of being capable. And for a kid who's failed a lot, trying is risky. If you try hard and fail, that says something painful. Maybe I'm just not smart. Maybe I'm just not good at this. But if you don't try, and you fail, you've got an out. I didn't even try. I don't care. It's stupid.

So not trying is a kind of armour. It protects the kid from the worst possible conclusion about himself. Covington found that students would sometimes rather be seen as lazy than as unable. Lazy is a choice. Unable feels like a verdict.

Part two. Why pushing harder backfires.

Now, the natural response to resistance is pressure. He won't move, so you push. And here's the uncomfortable thing. Pushing often makes it worse. Not because you're doing it wrong. Because of how people are wired.

In nineteen sixty six, a psychologist named Jack Brehm described something he called psychological reactance. The idea is simple. When people feel their freedom is being threatened, they push back to protect it. Sometimes by doing the exact opposite of what they're told. Tell a teenager he has to do something, and part of his brain immediately looks for a way to not do it. Not because he's thought about it. Because the pressure itself is the problem.

You've seen this a thousand times. You've probably felt it yourself. Someone at church tells you, you really should take on this ministry, and something in you stiffens, even if it's a good idea.

There's a big body of research, built on what's called self determination theory, from Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, that explains why. People have three basic needs when it comes to motivation. Autonomy, which means feeling that you have some choice and ownership. Competence, which means feeling able to do the thing. And relatedness, which means feeling connected to the people around you. When those three needs are met, motivation tends to grow from the inside. When they're blocked, motivation shrinks, or turns into resistance.

A researcher named Johnmarshall Reeve has spent years testing this in real classrooms. He and his colleagues train teachers in what they call an autonomy supportive style, and then compare their students with students of teachers who didn't get the training. One of their studies was in phys ed classes, with over a thousand students. And the pattern across these studies is consistent. When teachers become more autonomy supportive, students tend to engage more and disengage less.

It means things like explaining why a task matters, offering real choices within the task, acknowledging that something is hard or boring when it is, and using language that invites rather than commands. Part three. Shrink the first step.

So you've lowered the pressure. Now how do you actually get the kid moving? This is where the second need comes in. Competence. The kid needs to feel able.

And here's the key insight. The first step is usually too big. From your side, pick up the hammer and frame a wall is one simple job. From his side, it's a big public test he might fail. So shrink it. Make the first step so small that it's almost impossible to fail.

Not, frame the wall. But, hold this stud while I tack it. Not, run the mile. But, walk one lap with me. Not, go to youth group. But, come with me to drop something off at the church, and if you want to stay for ten minutes, great, if not, we'll leave.

A small first step does two things. It gets the body moving, which matters more than you'd think, because starting is often the hardest part. And it gives the kid a small win. And small wins build on themselves.

You know this from your own running. Think about coming back after an injury, or after a long winter off. If the plan on day one is ten kilometres, you might never lace up. It feels like a mountain. But if the plan is, put on the shoes and run for ten minutes, you'll go. And once you're out the door, you often run longer than you planned. The ten minutes wasn't really the goal. It was a door small enough to walk through. Every runner who's been away knows that the hardest part of the first run back is the front step.

A researcher named Teresa Amabile, at Harvard, studied this in workplaces. She and her colleague Steven Kramer had hundreds of people keep daily diaries about their work. And what the study found was that, of all the things that lifted people's mood and motivation on a given day, the biggest one was simply making progress on meaningful work. Even small progress. They called it the progress principle. It was adults at work, not teenagers in a shop, but the idea carries. People get motivated by seeing themselves move forward.

For a kid who's used to failing, a small win is powerful. It's evidence against the story in his head. Maybe I can do this. And once he's got one nail in, the second one is easier. You've seen this. There's another tool here, borrowed from a counselling approach called motivational interviewing, developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick. The basic idea of motivational interviewing is that you don't argue a person into change. You help them hear their own reasons. One simple technique from it is a ruler question. You ask, on a scale of one to ten, how much do you want to get this credit? And the kid says, I don't know, maybe a three. Part four. The kid who won't go out.

With a kid who avoids, there's a trap that loving adults fall into all the time. It's called accommodation.

Accommodation is when we change our behaviour to help a kid avoid what makes him anxious. It sounds kind. And it comes from love. He doesn't want to go to the party, so you let him stay home. He's nervous about ordering at the restaurant, so you order for him. He doesn't want to call about the job, so you call for him. He doesn't want to change for gym, so you let him sit out. Each one, on its own, is a small mercy.

But together, they send a message. You can't handle this. And they keep the anxiety alive, because the kid never gets the chance to find out that he actually could handle it. The world gets smaller and smaller.

A psychologist at Yale named Eli Lebowitz developed a program built around this idea. It's called SPACE, which stands for Supportive Parenting for Anxious Childhood Emotions. And here's what's unusual about it. The therapist doesn't work with the child at all. Only with the parents. The parents learn to slowly reduce accommodation, while being very supportive.

In a trial published in two thousand twenty, Lebowitz and his colleagues compared SPACE with standard therapy for the child, cognitive behavioural therapy, which is one of the best established treatments for childhood anxiety. And the study found that the parent only program worked about as well as the child therapy. In both groups, a majority of kids no longer met the criteria for an anxiety disorder afterward. Just by changing how the parents responded.

The heart of SPACE is something they call a supportive statement. It has two parts. Acceptance, and confidence. Acceptance says, I know this is really hard for you. I get it. Confidence says, and I know you can handle it. Both together. Not just, it's fine, don't worry, which dismisses the fear. And not just, toughen up, which dismisses the kid. Both halves.

Something like, I know going to youth group feels awful right now, and I'm sure you can handle it. Or, I know changing for gym is really uncomfortable, and I think you can do it.

Then, rather than suddenly dropping all the accommodations, you pick one, and you change it, gently, with warning. Starting next week, I'm not going to call about the job for you. I'll sit with you while you call. And then you stick to it, kindly, even when it's hard.

Now an important caution. If a kid's avoidance is severe, if he's not leaving the house for weeks, if he seems deeply sad, if there's any talk of self harm, this is beyond what a teacher or parent should handle alone. That's when you bring in a counsellor or a doctor. The ideas in this part are for the ordinary kind of stuck, the common kind. Not for a kid in crisis.

But for the ordinary kind, the shift is powerful. Instead of clearing every obstacle out of the kid's path, you walk beside him while he climbs over one. And as he does, his world starts to grow again.

Teachers accommodate too, and often for good reasons. Take the kid who's terrified of presenting in front of the class. The easy move is to excuse him. Just hand it in written. Small, planned steps toward the thing that scares you, each one held until it feels a little more normal.
