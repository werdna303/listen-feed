Wait, Did I Already Tell You That?

Part one. The moment.

You are partway into a story. Maybe it is at lunch with a colleague. Maybe it is on the couch with your wife after work. You are telling it well. You are just getting to the good part. And then you see it. A small smile. A polite nod that comes half a second too early. And they say, kindly, "Yeah, you told me."

And you feel a little flush of embarrassment. Not a big one. Just enough to make you wonder. Why does this keep happening? Is it a memory thing? Is it a sign I talk too much? Is it because I share things so openly and so quickly that I lose track of who has heard what?

That is the question you asked. And it is a good one, because it has a real answer. There is a small, clear line of research on exactly this. Not on forgetting in general. On this one specific kind of forgetting. Forgetting whom you told.

Here is the promise for the next half hour. By the end of this run, you will know why this happens to almost everyone, what it does say about an open sharer like you, what it does not say, and one simple thing to try this week.

We will go in four parts. First, the name of this kind of memory and the main study. Second, why it happens, which is the most interesting part. Third, what it says about you as a person who shares easily, and what it does not say. Fourth, some practical fixes, and one to try.

Let's go.

Part two. Destination memory.

Here is the first big idea. Your brain keeps two different kinds of records about conversations. One record is about where information came from. The other record is about where information went.

Psychologists have studied the first kind for a long time. They call it source memory. Source memory is remembering who told you something. You read a fact, and later you remember, oh, I heard that from my dad, or I read that in the news.

The second kind is newer to research. It is called destination memory. Destination memory is remembering whom you told something to. Where the information went when it left you.

The main study here came out in 2009. It was done by two researchers in Ontario, Nigel Gopie and Colin MacLeod, at the University of Waterloo. It was published in the journal Psychological Science, and its title says it all. It was called, "Destination memory: Stop me if I've told you this before."

Here is what they did. It is a simple setup, and it is worth picturing.

They sat university students in front of a computer. On the screen, a face would come up. A famous face. A well known actor or public figure. Then a fact would show up. Just a random, interesting fact.

One group of students was the telling group. They read each fact out loud, to the face on the screen, as if they were telling that person. So you would see a famous face, and you would say a fact to it.

The other group was the hearing group. For them, it was as if the face was telling them the fact. The fact came from the person on the screen.

About fifty faces. About fifty facts. Then came the test. They showed people faces and facts and asked them to match them up. Which fact went with which face?

Now here is what they found.

First, both groups remembered the faces about equally well. And both groups remembered the facts about equally well. So nobody's memory was just worse overall.

But when it came to matching the fact to the face, the telling group did worse. Noticeably worse. Popular write ups of the study put it at roughly fifteen percent worse. The telling group was worse at remembering whom they told than the hearing group was at remembering who told them.

Let that land for a second. Same faces. Same facts. Same amount of time. The only difference was the direction. Did the information come in, or did it go out? And when it went out, people lost track of where it went.

So here is the first idea in one line. You have a memory for where things went, it is called destination memory, and for almost everyone it is weaker than memory for where things came from.

That already answers part of your question. If you often forget whom you told something, you are not broken. You are running into a weak spot that is built into how human memory works.

Part three. Why it happens.

Here is the second big idea, and it is the heart of this episode. The reason destination memory is weak is attention. Specifically, where your attention goes when you are the one talking.

Think about the difference between listening and telling.

When someone tells you something, your attention is pointed outward. You are looking at them. You are taking in their face, their tone, the situation. The person is the main event, and the information comes attached to them.

When you tell someone something, your attention flips. It points inward. You are thinking about what you want to say. You are finding the words. You are watching how the story is going. You are thinking about the next line. You are, without meaning to, focused on yourself.

And attention is limited. You only have so much of it. If most of it is going to running the story, there is less left over to notice and store the simple fact of who is in front of you. Your brain is busy being the speaker. It is not busy filing the listener.

The researchers tested this idea directly. In a second experiment, they changed what people were telling. Some people told facts about the world. Others told personal facts about themselves.

And you can guess what happened. When people shared personal facts, things about their own lives, destination memory dropped even further. Again, the popular reports put it at roughly another fifteen percent. Talking about yourself pulls your attention even more inward. And so you are even less likely to remember who you said it to.

Then they flipped it the other way. In a later experiment, they gave people a way to push their attention back out toward the listener. For example, getting them to say the listener's name as they told the fact. And when attention went back out to the listener, destination memory got better.

A later review of this research, by Mohamad El Haj and colleagues in 2014, sums it up the same way. When we are giving out information, our attention is mostly on ourselves. That leaves fewer resources for tying the information to the person who received it. And when attention is turned from ourselves to the listener, errors go down.

So here is the second idea in one line. When you tell, your attention turns inward, and the more personal the thing you are telling, the more inward it turns. That is why you forget who heard it.

One more piece here, and it matters. The same research line looked at confidence. In a follow up study in 2010, by Gopie with Fergus Craik and Lynn Hasher, older adults did this task alongside younger ones. The older adults were worse at destination memory, which you might expect. But the interesting part was this. When people made mistakes, they were not just unsure. Often they were confident. They believed they had told someone something when they had not.

That is worth knowing even at your age. Your feeling of "I definitely told them" or "I definitely didn't" is not a reliable record. It feels like a memory. Often it is a guess wearing a memory's clothes.

Part four. Halfway recap.

Let's pause and gather what we have so far.

One. There is a kind of memory called destination memory. It is memory for whom you told. For almost everyone, it is weaker than memory for who told you.

Two. The reason is attention. When you are the one talking, your attention goes inward, to the story and to yourself. Less is left to notice the listener. Personal stories make it worse. Turning your attention back to the listener makes it better.

And a small bonus point. Your confidence about whether you told someone is not a great guide.

So far, two things. Now let's get to the part you were really curious about. What does this say about you?

Part five. What it says about an open sharer.

Here is the third big idea. You wondered whether this happens because you share things openly, quickly and easily with people. The research does not test that exact question. Nobody has run a study on "open sharers" specifically. So we need to be careful. But we can reason from what the research does show, and from how memory works in general. And there are a few honest things we can say.

First, what it probably does say.

It says you tell a lot of things. That sounds obvious, but it matters for memory. The more times you tell things, the more tellings there are to keep track of. And here is a basic principle of memory that holds up across a lot of research. Similar memories interfere with each other. If you have told a story once, it is easy to remember who heard it. If you have told five versions of it to six people across two weeks, those tellings start to blur. They look alike in your head. Was it at lunch or at church? Was it your wife or your friend? Was it the long version or the short one?

So an open sharer, almost by definition, is going to have more blurred tellings than a guarded person. That is not a flaw in your memory. It is just math. More tellings means more chances to mix them up.

Second, it says you probably tell things in a way that is self focused in the moment. Not selfish. Self focused. Remember the study. Personal facts hurt destination memory the most. And when you share openly, a lot of what you share is personal. What you are thinking. What happened to you. What you are excited about. That is exactly the kind of telling that pulls attention inward. So the very thing that makes you open, sharing from your own life, is the thing that makes you lose track of who heard it.

Third, it may say you share quickly. You used that word, quickly. Quick sharing often means less pause before you speak. And less pause means less of the moment when you would naturally think, "Who am I talking to? Have they heard this?" That little check takes a beat. If you speak fast and easily, the beat can get skipped.

Now here is what it does not say.

It does not say you have a bad memory. Remember, in the study, people remembered the faces and the facts just fine. The weak link was only the connection between them. Your memory is not failing. One specific link is weak, and it is weak in nearly everyone.

It does not say you are self centered as a person. This is important, because it would be easy to hear "self focus" and feel judged. The study's self focus is a matter of where attention goes for a few seconds while you talk. Everyone does it. Every speaker does it. It is how speaking works. It is not a verdict on your character.

And it does not say you are a poor listener. Those are different skills. You can be a careful listener and still forget whom you told something to, because remembering who told you and remembering whom you told run on different tracks.

So here is the honest picture. Forgetting whom you told is mostly normal. Being an open, quick sharer probably makes it happen more often, because you have more tellings, more personal ones, and less pause. That is a reasonable guess from the research, not a proven fact about you.

And there is a gentle flip side worth naming. Your openness is a gift. People tend to trust someone who shares. Teachers who share bits of their lives often connect better with students. So the goal here is not to share less. The goal is to share in a way that keeps the other person in view.

Here is the third idea in one line. Open, quick, personal sharing likely makes destination memory slips more common, but it says nothing bad about your memory or your character.

Part six. Where this shows up in your actual week.

Let's bring this into your life. Here are three places it probably shows up.

The first is the classroom. You tell your students a lot. Instructions, reminders, little stories, encouragement. In a small class where kids come and go, and where some days a student is absent or arrives late, it is very easy to believe you told the whole group something when only some of them heard it. And then a student says, "You never told us that," and you are sure you did.

Now, with this research in mind, you can hold that more lightly. Your sure feeling might be right. But it might be confident and wrong, just like in the follow up study. That is a good reason to have things like due dates and plans written down where students can see them, not just said out loud. The written version does not depend on your destination memory or theirs.

The second place is home. Your wife hears a lot from you. And here is the thing about the people closest to you. They hear the most tellings. So they are the most likely to hear repeats. When she says, "You told me," that is not a sign something is wrong. It is a sign she is the person you share the most with. That is a good thing about your marriage, not a bad one.

But there is a flip side at home too. Sometimes you will think you told her something, a plan, a change in schedule, and you did not. You told a colleague. Or you thought about telling her so many times it felt like you had. That can cause real friction. The fix is not a better memory. The fix is a shared place where plans live, like the shared calendar, so the telling does not depend on memory at all.

The third place is church and friendships. When you co lead a group, or meet with someone regularly, you share a lot of the same news with different people. Life updates. Prayer requests. What you have been learning. That is exactly the setting where tellings blur. Was it this friend or that one who heard about the rental search? Did I share that prayer request at the group, or just with one person?

And here faith gives a helpful angle. Scripture says a lot about speech. James tells us to be quick to hear and slow to speak. Now, forgetting whom you told something is not a sin. It is just how memory works. But the research and the verse point in the same direction. When attention is on the other person, more of what passes between you sticks. Being quick to hear and slow to speak is not only good for the soul. It turns out to be good for memory too.

So here is the fourth idea in one line. In class, at home and at church, the fix is less about remembering harder and more about keeping the other person in view and writing down what matters.

Part seven. Practical fixes.

Let me give you three fixes from the research and from common sense. Then I will pick just one for you to try.

Fix one. Look at the person and use their name. This comes straight from the study. When attention went back to the listener, destination memory improved. Saying someone's name while you tell them something is a simple way to do that. "Hey, here's something funny that happened today." Or with a colleague, using their name as you start. It sounds small. But it forces a split second of attention onto who is in front of you, and that is the moment the memory gets tied together.

Fix two. Ask before you tell. This is the old trick, and it works because it does not rely on memory at all. "Did I tell you about the thing with the car?" Most people would rather you ask than repeat. And it has a bonus. It turns your telling into a small conversation instead of a speech. It puts the listener back in the picture.

And note what to avoid here. Do not ask in a way that puts the other person on the spot or makes them feel bad. "I already told you this, right?" can sound like a test. "Stop me if I've told you this" is warmer. It gives them an easy way out, and it shows you care about their time.

Fix three. Write down the big ones. For things that really matter, a plan, a decision, a commitment, a piece of sensitive news, do not trust destination memory. Write it down where both people can see it. Or send a short text after you tell someone something important. "Like I mentioned at lunch, the meeting moved to Thursday." That text is a record. You will never have to wonder whether you told them.

And one thing that seems like it would work but probably will not. Trying to just remember harder. Telling yourself, "I need to pay more attention to who I tell things to." That sounds good. But in the moment, when you are in the flow of a story, your attention will go inward. That is how speaking works. Willpower is not a great tool here. Small habits that happen automatically, like using a name or asking first, are much better tools.

Part eight. One thing to try.

Here is the one thing for this week. Just one.

When you are about to tell a story or share some news with someone, start with their name and a quick check. "Hey, did I tell you about...?"

That is it. Name, then check.

It does two jobs at once. The name turns your attention toward the listener, which is what the study showed helps the memory get stored. And the check catches any repeats before they happen.

Try it with your wife, with a colleague and with a friend this week. Notice how often the answer is "yes, you told me," and how often it is "no, what happened?" That alone will teach you something about how your sharing works.

Part nine. Closing recap.

Here is the whole episode in three sentences.

Forgetting whom you told something is normal, because memory for where information went is weaker than memory for where it came from, and it is weak because your attention turns inward when you talk. Being an open, quick, personal sharer probably makes it happen more often, because you have more tellings and more personal ones, but it does not mean you have a bad memory or a self centered heart. The best fixes are small habits that put the other person back in view: say their name, ask before you tell, and write down the things that really matter.

I will leave you with one line from Alistair Begg, in a Truth For Life devotional on James chapter one, called A Call to Listen. He writes:

"If we ever find ourselves doing all the talking, we can be sure of one thing: we're not listening."
