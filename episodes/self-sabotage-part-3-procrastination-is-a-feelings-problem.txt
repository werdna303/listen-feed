Self-Sabotage, Part 3: Procrastination Is a Feelings Problem

Cold open

A lot of us grew up with a parent who had a saying. Work first, play later. Homework before hockey. Chores before the TV goes on. You heard it so many times it stopped being a sentence and became part of the furniture.

And here is the funny thing. You probably still believe it. You probably even live by it, most days. But you have also noticed something strange. There is a whole category of tasks that look like work, feel like work, and even go on your to-do list as work. Checking on the plants. Tidying the desk. Reorganizing a shelf in the garage. Answering an easy email. And when you do those things, you feel productive. You feel like the kid who did his chores first.

But underneath, something else is going on. Those little tasks are not really the work. They are the play. They are the pleasant thing you reach for so that you do not have to feel the unpleasant thing. And the big task, the one that actually matters, sits there one more day.

So here is the promise for today. By the end of this run, you will understand why procrastination is not a commitment problem or a laziness problem, but a feelings problem. And you will have one simple thing to try this week that works with your feelings instead of against them.

This is part three of the self-sabotage series. Let's go.

Section one: Procrastination is mood repair

Here is the first idea. Procrastination is not mainly about time. It is about mood.

Two researchers have spent a lot of their careers on this. One is Fuschia Sirois, and the other is Tim Pychyl, a psychologist at Carleton University in Ottawa. In 2013 they wrote a paper with a long title. The short version of the title is this: procrastination is giving priority to short-term mood repair.

Let me say what that means in plain words.

When you look at a task and you feel something unpleasant, your brain wants that feeling to stop. Maybe the task is boring. Maybe it is confusing. Maybe it makes you feel like you might fail. Maybe it is just vague, and you do not know where to start. Any of those feelings is a small sting. And the fastest way to stop the sting is to turn away from the task.

So you turn away. And right away, you feel better. That is the mood repair. It works. It really does make you feel better, for a few minutes.

Pychyl has a handy list of what makes a task sting. He calls them triggers. A task is more likely to get put off when it is boring, frustrating, difficult, vague, or unstructured. Also when it does not feel meaningful to you, or when there is nothing rewarding in doing it. That is seven, which is too many to remember on a run, so let me squeeze them into three. Boring. Foggy. Hard. If a task is one of those, your brain will want to look away. If it is two or three of them at once, it will want to look away very badly.

Notice what is not on that list. Laziness is not on the list. Not caring is not on the list. You can care a great deal about a task and still put it off, because caring does not remove the sting. In fact, caring can make the sting bigger. If a task really matters to you, then doing it badly would really hurt. So the most important tasks can be the most avoided ones. That is one of the cruel little jokes of procrastination.

That is why procrastination is so sticky. It is not a bug in your brain. It is your brain doing exactly what it was built to do: get away from pain and toward relief. The problem is that the relief is short, and the cost comes later.

Now, there is an older study that makes this really clear, and I love it because it is so clever. It was done by Dianne Tice and some colleagues around 2001. They put people in a bad mood on purpose. Then they gave them a chance to practise for an upcoming test, or to goof off with puzzles and games instead.

Here is the twist. Some of the people were told that their mood was frozen. They were given a pill, really just a harmless placebo, and told that it would lock their mood in place for a while. Nothing they did would change how they felt.

And what happened? The people in a bad mood who thought their mood could still change, they procrastinated. They went for the games. But the people who believed their mood was frozen did not procrastinate nearly as much. Why would they? If playing games cannot make you feel better, there is no reason to avoid the work.

Sit with that for a second. The procrastination went away when the mood payoff went away. That tells you what the procrastination was for. It was for feeling better.

So here is the first idea again in one line. Procrastination is a way of fixing your mood right now, and that is why it feels so good and works so badly.

Section two: Why the small pleasant tasks feel like work

Here is the second idea. The tasks we use to escape are often disguised as work.

Go back to that parent's saying. Work first, play later. Most of us learned this rule well. So when we want to escape, we do not usually go straight to something that looks like play. That would break the rule, and we would feel guilty. Instead, we pick something that looks like work but feels like play.

Checking the plants is a perfect example. It is a real task. The plants do need water. You can tell yourself you are being responsible. But notice how it feels. It is calm. It is clear. You know exactly what to do. There is no risk of failing. And at the end, you can see that you did it. Green leaves, wet soil, done.

Now compare that to the big task. Maybe it is a lesson plan for next week that you have not figured out yet. Maybe it is a phone call that might be awkward. Maybe it is a form with a lot of small boxes. Those tasks are foggy. They have risk in them. You cannot see the finish line. And they might make you feel a little dumb, or a little exposed.

So when you stand between those two tasks, the plants win. Every time. And because the plants look like work, you do not even notice that you just broke your own rule. You think you did work first. But really, you did play first. You just dressed it up.

Some people call this productive procrastination. There is a philosopher named John Perry who wrote a funny little essay about it, called structured procrastination. He admitted that he got a lot done by avoiding the one thing he was supposed to do. And he is right that this is not all bad. The plants do get watered. The desk does get tidy.

But here is the catch. If the important thing never gets done, the busy feeling is a trap. It feeds the part of you that wants to be productive, while the real task sits there and gets heavier.

There is one more piece to this that I think matters for you. When you care a lot about being productive, this trap is even stronger. Because the small tasks give you a little hit of the feeling you care about. You get to check a box. You get to feel useful. So the pull of the fake work is not weak. It is tuned exactly to what you want most.

So here is the second idea again in one line. The tasks we escape into often look like work, but they are really play, because what they give us is relief.

Section three: Your future self feels like a stranger

Here is the third idea. When you procrastinate, you are handing the hard feeling to someone you do not really know. And that someone is you, later.

Before we get to that, let me give you the name economists use for the bigger pattern. It is called present bias. It means that things happening now feel much bigger than things happening later, even when the later thing is objectively bigger.

Here is the classic way to see it. Imagine I offer you a hundred dollars today, or a hundred and ten dollars next week. A lot of people take the hundred today. Now imagine I offer you a hundred dollars a year from now, or a hundred and ten dollars a year and a week from now. Almost everyone waits the extra week for the extra ten.

It is the same trade. One week, ten dollars. But when the wait starts today, it feels huge. When the wait starts a year from now, it feels like nothing. That is present bias. The present gets a special weight that the future never gets.

Now put feelings in the place of dollars. Feeling better right now, by avoiding the task, is the hundred dollars today. Feeling much better later, because the task is done, is the hundred and ten next week. And right now always wins, because right now is the only moment that feels real.

So present bias explains why the trade feels good. But Hershfield adds something important about who you are trading with.

This comes from a researcher named Hal Hershfield. He is a psychologist at UCLA, and he has spent years on one question: how do we think about our future selves?

In one study, he put people in a brain scanner. He asked them to think about themselves now. Then he asked them to think about themselves ten years from now. Then he asked them to think about other people, including a stranger.

What he found was striking. When people thought about their future selves, their brains looked less like they were thinking about themselves, and more like they were thinking about someone else. For many people, the future self looked a bit like a stranger.

And that matters, because we are not always kind to strangers. We do not feel their pain the way we feel our own. So if the future you is a bit of a stranger, then it is easy to give him the hard task. Let him deal with it. He will have more time. He will be more rested. He will be more in the mood.

Sirois and Pychyl tied this right into procrastination. Their paper says that when we put off a task to feel better now, we are trading our present mood against the well-being of our future self. And because the future self feels distant, the trade seems fine. Until tomorrow comes, and you are the stranger now, and the task is still there, and it is heavier.

Hershfield did another study that shows a way out. He took photos of young people and used software to age them. So people saw what they might look like in their late sixties. Then he asked them how they would split some money between spending now and saving for retirement. The people who had seen their aged faces put about twice as much into retirement as the people who had not.

Twice as much. Just from seeing the future self as a real person.

So the lesson is not that you need more willpower. The lesson is that you need to see future you more clearly. When the future self is close and real, it is a lot harder to dump things on him.

In his book, called Your Future Self, Hershfield suggests small ways to do this. One is writing a short letter to yourself a year from now, or a letter from your future self back to you. It sounds silly. But it makes the stranger into a person.

So here is the third idea again in one line. We procrastinate more when future us feels like a stranger, and less when we can see him clearly.

Halfway recap

Let's stop and gather what we have so far. Three ideas.

One. Procrastination is mood repair. We turn away from a task because turning away makes us feel better right now.

Two. The tasks we escape into often look like work, like checking the plants, but they are really play, because what they give us is relief.

Three. We hand the hard feelings to our future self, and we do it easily because the present feels bigger and that future self feels like a stranger.

So if procrastination is a feelings problem, then the fix is not to try harder or make bigger promises. The fix has to deal with the feelings. That is where we go next.

Section four: What actually helps

Here is the fourth idea. The things that actually help are small, and they all work on the feeling, not on your character.

Let me give you three. Not ten. Three.

The first is self-forgiveness. This one surprises people. There is a study by Michael Wohl, along with Tim Pychyl and a colleague, from about 2010. They followed university students through their first midterm exams. They asked them how much they had procrastinated on studying, and how much they had forgiven themselves for it.

Then came the second set of exams. And the students who had forgiven themselves for procrastinating the first time, they procrastinated less the second time.

Why? Think about it from the mood angle. If you beat yourself up about a task, then that task now carries even more bad feeling. Guilt and shame get attached to it. So the next time you look at it, the sting is bigger, and the pull to escape is stronger. Self-criticism makes the task worse to look at.

But if you say to yourself, okay, I put that off, that happens, I am going to start now, then the task stays lighter. And a lighter task is easier to start.

This is not about letting yourself off the hook. It is about taking the extra weight off the hook so you can actually lift it.

The second thing that helps is just getting started, on the smallest possible piece. Pychyl says this a lot. He even has a short phrase for it: just get started. And there is a reason it works. Our feelings about a task are usually worst before we start. Once we are a few minutes in, the task is often less bad than we predicted. Sometimes it is even fine.

So the trick is to make the starting step so small that it barely stings. Not write the lesson plan. Just open the document and type the title. Not make the phone call. Just find the number and put it on the screen. The goal is not to finish. The goal is to get through the door, because the feeling at the door is the worst feeling in the whole task.

The third thing that helps is a plan that is so specific it decides for you. Peter Gollwitzer, a psychologist, calls these implementation intentions. They have a simple shape. When this happens, I will do that. When I sit down at my desk after lunch, I will open the report card file. When I get in the car after work, I will make the call before I start driving.

The reason this helps is that it skips the moment of choosing. And the moment of choosing is exactly where the feelings ambush you. If you have to decide, in the moment, whether to start now or water the plants first, the plants will win. But if the decision was already made, there is nothing to argue about.

There is one more thing I want to add here, and it ties back to the parent's saying. Work first, play later is actually a real principle in psychology. It is called the Premack principle, after David Premack, and teachers use it all the time. First this, then that. First the worksheet, then the computer. It works because the pleasant thing becomes a reward for the less pleasant thing.

So you do not have to throw out the saying. You just have to be honest about which task is which. If checking the plants is the play, then let it be the play. Put it after the hard task, on purpose. Let it be the reward. First the hard thing, even just ten minutes of it. Then the plants.

So here is the fourth idea again in one line. What helps is forgiving yourself, starting with a tiny step, deciding ahead of time, and putting the pleasant little tasks after the hard one as a reward.

How it lands in your week

Let's bring this down into an ordinary week.

In the classroom. You already know this from your students, even if you have not called it this. When a student will not start a worksheet, it is almost never because he does not care, or because he made a commitment and broke it. It is usually because the worksheet makes him feel something. Confused. Behind. Watched. So he sharpens his pencil for the third time. He asks to go to the washroom. He tidies his desk. He is checking the plants.

And when you see it that way, the response changes. Lecturing about effort adds more bad feeling to the task, which makes it harder to start. What helps is shrinking the first step. Just do the first question. Just write your name and the date. And it helps to keep the shame low. Okay, you put that off yesterday, that happens, let's start now. Same tools, same reasons, just aimed at someone else.

At home. Think about the projects that keep rolling over. The garage. A form. A call you keep meaning to make. Ask one question about each one: what is the feeling? Is it boring? Is it foggy? Is it a little scary? Once you name the feeling, you know what kind of small step will help. A foggy task needs a first step that makes it clear. A scary task needs a first step that is so small it is safe. A boring task can be paired with something nice, like music, or put right before something you enjoy.

With money. Hershfield's work started with retirement savings, and that is where the stranger effect is strongest. Future you, the one in his sixties, really does feel far away. If you have a money task you keep putting off, like setting up a savings transfer or sorting a tax form, try picturing him. Not a vague future. The actual man, older, who will be glad, or not, about what you did this week.

In your faith. There is something gentle in the self-forgiveness finding that fits the gospel well. You are not saved by your productivity, and you are not made right by a clean to-do list. You can look at a task you put off, and admit it plainly, without piling shame on top. Grace does not make the work less important. It makes the work lighter to pick up. And it frees you from needing every small task to prove that you are useful.

One thing to try

Here is the one thing. Just one.

This week, pick one task that has been rolling over. The one you have put off more than once. When you notice yourself reaching for a plants task, a small pleasant thing that looks like work, stop and ask one question out loud or in your head: what am I feeling about the real task?

Then name it in one word. Foggy. Boring. Scary. Awkward.

And then do the smallest first step of the real task for five minutes. Only five. After that, you are allowed to go water the plants, as the reward.

That is it. Name the feeling, take a five-minute first step, then play.

Closing recap

So here is the whole episode in three sentences.

Procrastination is not a commitment problem but a feelings problem: we turn away from tasks to repair our mood right now, often by escaping into small pleasant tasks that look like work.

We get away with it because the present always feels bigger and our future self feels like a stranger, and it is easy to hand a stranger the hard part.

What helps is forgiving yourself, naming the feeling, taking a tiny first step, deciding ahead of time, and saving the pleasant tasks for after, as the play that comes after the work.

To close, here is a line from Alistair Begg, Truth For Life, from a devotional called Now Is the Time.

"You are not to live as though you will always have a tomorrow in which to do what you should be doing today."
