Listen. Authority Without the Standoff.
For your run. About 30 minutes.

Picture a kitchen in your hometown. You're small. There's a mess on the floor, maybe toys, maybe a spilled bowl of cereal, and you want your mom to clean it up. She won't. She says something like, "You made it, you clean it." And you lose it. You kick, you scream, you tell her she's being totally unreasonable.

Now fast forward. You're grown up, and you look back on that, and two things are true at once. One, she was right. She was holding a principle, and it was a good one. Two, it was also a bit much. There's a version of that afternoon where nobody had to dig in their heels at all.

That's the question you asked. Not "was Mom right," but "how do you avoid getting to the spot where you have to dig in your heels as a parent?" And it's a question for your classroom right now, and for your home later, with your wife, and maybe with kids who come to you through fostering.

So here's the promise of this episode, in one sentence. Authority that rarely needs a standoff is built mostly before the moment, not in it.

We'll take that in four ideas. First, warm and firm are not opposites. Second, set expectations early, so they feel like how things are, not a ruling you made up on the spot. Third, have very few hills, and hold them calmly. Fourth, when a standoff comes anyway, don't pick up the rope.

Let's run.

Here's the first idea. Warm and firm are not opposites.

A lot of us carry around a picture where warmth and firmness sit on two ends of one line. On one end is the strict parent. On the other end is the soft parent. And every decision is a choice of where to stand on that line. Do I give in, which feels warm? Or do I hold the line, which feels strict?

The research says that picture is wrong. They aren't one line. They're two separate dials.

A psychologist named Diana Baumrind studied parents and families starting back in the 1960s. She described parenting along two dimensions. One is how much a parent expects, how much they ask of a child, how much structure and follow through there is. The other is how warm and responsive the parent is, how much they listen and tune in to the child.

When you put those two dials together, you get four rough styles. High expectations and low warmth is what she called authoritarian. Think "because I said so." Low expectations and high warmth is permissive. Think "whatever you want, sweetheart." Later researchers added a fourth, low on both, which is neglectful. And high on both is what she called authoritative. That one, high warmth and high expectations together, is the style that keeps showing up in the research with the best outcomes for kids.

The same thing shows up in classrooms. Robert Marzano and his colleagues did a big review of more than a hundred studies on classroom management. They wrote it up in a well known article called "The Key to Classroom Management." What they found is that teachers with high quality relationships with their students had about 31 percent fewer discipline problems over a year. Roughly a third fewer. Just from the relationship.

But here's the part people miss. Marzano didn't say the relationship was just about being nice. He said a good teacher student relationship has two parts. One part he called cooperation, which is concern for the kids and a sense that you're a team. The other part he called dominance. And that word sounds harsh, but he defined it as the teacher's ability to provide clear purpose and strong guidance, both for the work and for behaviour. In one study he cites, students in grades four to seven actually said they preferred strong teacher guidance over permissive teachers.

Kids want both dials turned up. They want to know you like them, and they want to know you're in charge.

So think back to that kitchen. Your mom had the expectation dial turned up. "You made it, you clean it." Good. The standoff wasn't because she was firm. A standoff tends to happen when, in that one moment, the child can only feel one dial. All he can feel is the "no." He can't feel the "I'm with you" at the same time.

And you've seen this in your own classroom. You told me back in October that when kids were upset, you'd say, "Thank you for sharing how you feel," to stay consistent. And the kids heard it as you not caring. The firmness was there. But in that moment, the warmth dial didn't come through. And so it felt like a wall, and walls get kicked.

So the first idea, in one line. Warm and firm are two dials, not one, and standoffs happen when a kid can only feel one of them.

Here's the second idea. Set expectations early, so they feel like how things are, not a ruling you made up on the spot.

There's a classic set of studies out of the University of Texas around 1980, by Edmund Emmer, Carolyn Evertson, and Linda Anderson. They watched teachers in the very first weeks of the school year, and then followed those classrooms through the year. The finding, put simply, is that teachers who got off to a good start, who taught their rules and routines right at the beginning, tended to have more orderly classrooms in January, and better student achievement too.

The effective teachers didn't wait for a problem to make a rule. They taught the routines like content. Here's where your bag goes. Here's how we start the day. Here's what happens when you finish early. They practised it. They came back to it.

Now why does that matter for standoffs? Because there are two very different ways a child can hear a rule.

The first way is when the rule shows up in the middle of the moment. The kid makes a mess, you say, "Clean that up," and to the kid, that sounds like a personal ruling. It's you versus them, right now. And when it's you versus them, the kid's pride is on the line. If he backs down, he loses. So he digs in. And now you have to dig in, because if you back down, you lose. Two people, two sets of heels, one kitchen floor.

The second way is when the rule was there before the moment. The kid has heard it a hundred times, in calm times, in fun times. "In our house, whoever takes it out puts it back." Now when the mess happens, you don't have to make a ruling. You just point at the thing that was already true. "What's our rule?" The rule is the bad guy, not you. Nobody's pride is on the line, because nobody just invented anything.

Think of it like a speed limit sign. The police officer doesn't decide how fast you can go when he pulls you over. The sign was there before you got in the car. That's why most people don't argue with the sign. They argue with the officer when they think he's being arbitrary.

This is the thing you noticed about yourself in October. You said you want to be, and I'm quoting your caps here, "so deadly predictable" every day for your students, even where you sit and when. And you noticed that on a day when you were calm and controlled, the kids had a really good day. That's this idea exactly. Predictability is expectations set early. It means fewer moments where you have to rule on something live.

It's also why a heads up works so well. You've used it with snack. "If the dishes aren't done, we'll have no clean bowls for snack." You're not making a ruling at snack time. You're naming a consequence ahead of time, and then stepping back. The bowls are the bad guy.

And here's a hopeful thing about this. You're in November. You've told me you think of the school year like a week. September is Monday morning, October is Monday afternoon, November is Tuesday morning. Tuesday morning is still early. It is not too late to re teach a routine. Teachers do a reset after a break, after a long weekend, after a rough week. You can say, "Hey, I want to go back over how we do a few things." That isn't weakness. That's a teacher who knows what he wants.

What does a reset sound like? Something like this. Monday morning, after the sheet, before anyone is upset about anything. "Hey, quick thing. I've noticed bowls getting left on the counter, and I don't want to be the guy nagging you about it all day. So here's how it works from now on. Your bowl goes in the sink before break. That's it. If it's not there, break waits a minute while you do it." Then you show it. You literally walk a bowl to the sink. It feels silly with teenagers. Do it anyway, with a little humour. Then for the next week, you catch it early and you catch it gently. "Bowl." One word. Smile.

Notice what happened there. The rule came out at a calm time. It came with a reason. It came with a consequence that's small, logical, and known ahead of time. And the reminder later is one word, not a speech. That's the whole move. By the time the hard moment shows up, there's almost nothing left to fight about.

Same thing at home someday. A five year old who has heard "toys go back in the bin before dinner" every night for a year isn't hearing a ruling at six o'clock. He's hearing the way the evening works. He might still whine. Kids whine. But whining about a fact is very different from fighting a ruling.

So the second idea, in one line. A rule taught in a calm moment is a fact, but a rule invented in a hot moment is a fight.

So far we've got two things. Warm and firm are two dials, not one. And expectations set early feel like facts, not rulings.

Let's do a quick halfway recap, then keep going.

You asked how to avoid the spot where you need to dig in your heels. So far the answer is this. Most of the work happens before that spot. You turn up both dials, warmth and expectations, so a kid can feel both at once. And you set the expectations in calm times, so that when the hard moment comes, you're pointing at something that was already true, not inventing a ruling on the spot.

Two more ideas to go. One about choosing your hills. And one about what to do when the standoff comes anyway, because it will.

Here's the third idea. Have very few hills, and hold them calmly.

There's a child psychologist named Ross Greene who wrote a book called The Explosive Child, and later Lost at School, which is the version for teachers. He has a simple framework that fits your question really well. He says that when an adult has an expectation a kid isn't meeting, the adult basically has three options. He calls them Plan A, Plan B, and Plan C.

Plan A is when the adult solves the problem alone and imposes the answer. "Clean it up, because I said so." Greene says Plan A is very popular. And with a kid who is already struggling, commanding often backfires.

Plan B is when you solve it together. It has three steps. First, you find out the kid's concern. What's hard about this? Second, you share your own concern. Third, you invite the kid to come up with a solution with you, one that both of you can actually do. And the key detail is that Plan B works best ahead of time, in a calm moment, when the problem isn't happening.

Plan C is when you set the expectation aside, at least for now. You let it go, or you shrink it, or you come back to it later. And that's not giving up. It's deciding this one isn't worth the fight today.

Now here's the connection to digging in your heels. If every expectation is a Plan A expectation, you will spend all day with your heels in the dirt. Every small thing becomes a hill. And kids are very good at finding out which hills you'll die on.

But if you decide ahead of time that only a few things are Plan A, the true non negotiables, then you can hold those calmly. You're not tired. You're not stretched across fifty little battles. Most things become Plan B, worked out with the kid at a calm time. And some things become Plan C, let go on purpose.

Let's go back to the kitchen one more time. And I want to be gentle here, because your mom isn't here to tell her side, and she was a good mom who held good principles. But just as a thought experiment. "You made it, you clean it" is a fine rule. Was it a Plan A rule for a small kid who was falling apart? Maybe. Or maybe it could have been Plan B on a calm Saturday morning. "Hey, I notice cleaning up is really hard for you. What's hard about it?" And maybe the answer is, "It's too much stuff, I don't know where to start." And then the solution is, "OK, you do the Lego, I'll do the books." The principle stays. The standoff doesn't happen.

You actually said this yourself in a different way. You told me you see the same stubbornness in yourself that your mom had. Hosting all the food and leading the Bible study too. And you asked, "Am I going through pain just for pain's sake?" That's the same question in a different room. A principle held on every single thing becomes a weight. A principle held on a few things becomes a backbone.

And in your classroom, Ross Greene has one more line that's worth carrying. He says, "Kids do well if they can." The idea is that when a kid isn't meeting an expectation, it's usually not that he doesn't want to. It's that something is getting in the way, a skill he's missing, or a problem nobody has solved yet. You noticed that with a student. His trigger is being asked to do something, and you wondered if maybe he doesn't know how, or doesn't want to. That's a Plan B conversation waiting to happen. Not in the moment. At a calm time.

What might that sound like? Greene's first step is just getting the kid's concern, and he suggests starting with an observation, not an accusation. Something like, "I've noticed that when I ask you to start the sheet, it gets hard. What's up?" And then the hard part for you. Shut up and wait. You've already written that one down for yourself, that sometimes the best thing you can do is let the kid talk first. Maybe he shrugs. That's fine. You can guess gently. "Is it that you don't know where to start? Is it that it feels pointless? Is it the being asked part?" When you finally hear the real concern, then you put yours next to it. "My concern is that you get your credits." And then you ask, "What could we do about that, that works for both of us?" It might take three tries over three weeks. That's normal. But every one of those conversations is a standoff that won't happen later.

So the third idea, in one line. Pick a few hills on purpose, hold those calmly, and solve the rest together or let them go.

Here's the fourth idea. When a standoff comes anyway, don't pick up the rope.

Because it will come. You can turn up both dials, set expectations early, and pick your hills, and some days a kid will still pick a fight. That's being a kid. And that's being a teenager in your classroom, many of whom have had a lot of adults let them down.

You already have the picture for this one. It came from a colleague. There's a rope on the ground. The kid picks up one end and wants a tug of war. Are you going to pick up the other end?

And you have another line of your own from October. "If a student fights with me and I reply with logic, I am giving them a fight to win." That's exactly right. In a standoff, explaining yourself is picking up the rope. Every reason you give is something to argue with.

So what do you do instead? Three small moves.

One. Slow down. You told yourself in October to slow down your voice, your pace and your tone when a kid is escalating, instead of ramping up with him. That's huge. A calm adult is like a lid. A hot adult is like more fuel.

Two. Say less. One short sentence, then wait. "Cleaning up isn't optional. I'll be over here when you're ready." No speech. Silence does a lot of the work.

Three. Give a choice inside the boundary. And this one has good research behind it. A study in 2010 by Hyungshim Jang, Johnmarshall Reeve, and Edward Deci watched about 130 high school classrooms. They looked at two things teachers did. One was structure, meaning clear expectations and guidance. The other was autonomy support, meaning giving students choice, listening, and explaining the why. And people often assume those two pull against each other. The study found they actually went together. The teachers who gave lots of structure also tended to give lots of autonomy support. And both of them predicted students being more engaged. The title of the paper says it all. It's not autonomy support or structure. It's autonomy support and structure.

What that means in a standoff is this. The boundary doesn't move. But inside the boundary, the kid gets a real choice. "The mess gets cleaned before snack. Do you want to start with the Lego or the books?" "The sheet gets an attempt before free time. Do you want to do it at your desk or the hallway desk?" You're not giving up authority. You're giving the kid a way to keep his dignity while he does the thing.

And that's really the whole secret of avoiding the heel digging spot. A standoff is a fight over dignity as much as it's a fight over the mess. If the kid can follow through without feeling like he lost, he usually will.

So the fourth idea, in one line. When it gets hot, slow down, say less, and offer a choice inside the line.
