Now here's the thing. God wrote that exact law. Three thousand years ago, in the book of Deuteronomy, he told Israel: when you build a new house, put a railing around your roof. But your student on the deck would understand it in about four seconds.

Here's the promise of this episode. By the end of this run, you'll see that the laws we quietly skip are not random. Each one was built on a reason, and the reason still stands even when the rule doesn't.

You asked a real question. You said you understand that the law gets divided into moral, social and practical categories, or something like that. You know the laws show us that God is particular. But you want more weight than that. You want to know if every one of those ignored laws actually means something. Not just "God is picky," but "here is why, and here is what it asks of me."

That's a good question, and it deserves a better answer than most of us got in church. So this is part one of three. Part one. The three drawers.

Let's start with the framework you already half know.

For a long time, Christians have sorted the Old Testament law into three drawers. The first drawer is the moral law. That's the stuff that's always right and always wrong, the Ten Commandments being the summary. Don't murder. Don't steal. Don't lie. Love God. The second drawer is the civil law, sometimes called the judicial law. That's the law that ran the nation of Israel as a country. Property, courts, penalties, inheritance, what to do when your ox gores your neighbour. The third drawer is the ceremonial law. That's the worship system. Sacrifices, priests, festivals, clean and unclean foods, the temple.

You'll find that division in Thomas Aquinas in the Middle Ages, and Calvin used it in the Institutes. But the place it really gets nailed down for Reformed folks like you is the Westminster Confession of Faith, from the sixteen forties. Chapter nineteen of the Confession is about the law of God, and it lays out all three drawers.

On the ceremonial law, it says those laws were types, pictures pointing forward to Christ, and they are now "abrogated" under the New Testament. Cancelled. The sacrifice they pointed to has come.

On the moral law, it says that one binds everyone, forever, believers included. Not as a way to earn salvation, but as a rule for life.

And then comes the line that matters most for your question. On the civil or judicial laws, the Confession says this. God gave Israel, as a body politic, "sundry judicial laws, which expired together with the State of that people; not obliging any other now, further than the general equity thereof may require."

Let's slow that down, because it's worded carefully.

"Expired together with the State of that people." When the nation of Israel as a political state ended, its civil code ended with it. Canada doesn't run on Israel's property law. Your town council doesn't have to follow the rules for Israelite courts.

But then: "not obliging any other now, further than the general equity thereof may require." In other words, the specific rules are gone, but the general equity is not. Equity is an old word for fairness, for the principle of justice inside the rule. So the Westminster divines are saying something more interesting than "ignore those." They're saying: the rule expired, but the reason inside the rule still has a claim on you.

That phrase, general equity, is going to be our key for the whole episode. Hold onto it.

You fix your own truck. Say you've got an old service manual for a model that's twenty years older than yours. A lot of it doesn't apply anymore. The torque spec on a part your truck doesn't even have. The procedure for a carburetor you don't own. But the manual is still full of things that transfer. Why you torque in a star pattern. Why you don't overtighten a drain plug. Why you check the simple thing first. The specific instruction expired with the old model. The general equity, the reason under the instruction, still works on your truck.

That's what the Confession is reaching for. Not "this part of the Bible is useless." More like "this part of the Bible was written for a specific vehicle, and you need to know how to pull the principle out."

So, one line. The rules for Israel's nation expired, but the wisdom inside them did not.

Part two. Where the drawers stick.

Now, I promised you honesty, so let's talk about where this framework creaks.

The first problem is that the Bible itself doesn't sort the laws into drawers. Open Leviticus chapter nineteen sometime. In one chapter, you get "love your neighbour as yourself," which Jesus calls the second greatest commandment. Right next to it, you get "don't mix two kinds of seed in your field." Right next to that, rules about harvest edges, honest weights, and how to treat a foreigner. Moral, civil and ceremonial are all braided together. Israel didn't experience these as three separate codes. They experienced them as one way of life under one God.

So the three drawers are a tool we bring to the text. A useful tool, but a tool. Like sorting a student's file into attendance, behaviour and credits. Helpful for paperwork, but the kid is one person.

The second problem is that some laws won't stay in one drawer. Take the Sabbath. It's in the Ten Commandments, so that sounds moral. But it also had ceremonial features tied to Israel's calendar, and civil penalties attached. Christians have argued for centuries about which part carries forward. Or take the food laws. They're usually filed as ceremonial, but they also shaped Israel's social life, who you could eat with, which made them civil too. We'll dig into those in part two.

The third problem is that Christians who love the Bible genuinely disagree about how to use this. I want to be fair to them.

Some in the Reformed world, usually called theonomists, Greg Bahnsen being the best known, argued that the civil laws still apply quite directly, including many of the penalties, unless the New Testament changes them. That's a minority view, and most Reformed churches have rejected it, but it's a serious position and they appeal to the same Confession you do.

Others, including many Baptists and folks who hold what's called New Covenant Theology, say the whole law of Moses came as one package with one covenant, and that package ended at the cross. What binds Christians now is the law of Christ. They'd say we don't keep any Mosaic law because it's Mosaic. We keep what Christ and his apostles teach, which overlaps a lot with the moral law.

Notice something, though. Almost all of these positions agree on the practical point I want to make today. Nobody serious thinks the parapet law is binding on your house in Ontario. And nobody serious thinks it's meaningless either. The argument is mostly about the route by which the principle reaches us. Through general equity. Through natural law. Through the law of Christ. Different roads, but they arrive at a lot of the same places.

The scholar who's helped me most here is Christopher Wright. He's an Old Testament scholar, an Anglican, and his big book is called Old Testament Ethics for the People of God. Wright isn't against the three drawers, but he thinks they can make us lazy. If a law goes in the civil or ceremonial drawer, we close the drawer and walk away. And we miss what's inside.

Wright's alternative is to treat Israel as a paradigm. A paradigm is a model case. Think of how you learn a language. You memorize how one verb works, all its forms, and then you use that pattern to handle hundreds of other verbs you've never seen. Wright says Israel's law works like that. God took one nation, in one land, at one time, and showed what a society looks like when it lives under him. The details were for them. But the pattern is for everyone. So when we read an Israelite law, the question isn't "do I have to do this exact thing?" The question is "what was God doing here, and what would that look like in my world?"

Wright is basically teaching us how to find the general equity.

So, one line. The three drawers are a useful sorting tool, but the real work is opening each law and asking what it was for.

Part three. Three laws, three reasons.

Now let's actually do it. Three laws. For each one, we'll ask the same three questions. What did the rule say? What was the reason inside it? And what does that reason ask of you now?

First law. The parapet on the roof.

It's in Deuteronomy chapter twenty-two. When you build a new house, make a parapet, a low wall or railing, around your roof, so that you don't bring the guilt of blood on your house if someone falls from it.

In Israel, a flat roof was living space. People slept up there on hot nights. They dried flax and grain. They hosted guests. Remember Rahab hiding the spies under the stalks of flax on her roof. So a roof was like a deck or a patio.

Now look at what the law does. It doesn't say "if someone falls, you're guilty." It says "build the railing before anyone falls, or you're guilty." It puts the responsibility on the person who controls the danger, and it puts it there in advance. And notice the phrase, "the guilt of blood on your house." God is saying that if you could have prevented a death with a little foresight and a little cost, and you didn't, that death is on you. Not just as bad luck. As guilt.

That's the general equity. You are responsible for the foreseeable dangers you create or control. Love for your neighbour includes the boring work of prevention.

And here's what gives this one gravity. That principle is basically the whole foundation of modern safety law. Building codes. Railings on stairs. Guards on table saws. Duty of care. You teach this every day in your construction class. When you make a kid put the blade guard back on, when you won't let anyone up a ladder without someone footing it, you are living out the general equity of Deuteronomy chapter twenty-two. That's not a stretch. That's the law doing exactly what it was meant to do.

And it goes further than buildings. Your truck. You fix it yourself. Are the brakes actually done right, or done good enough? The things you sell on Marketplace. If you know something's got a defect that could hurt someone, the parapet principle says you tell them. Your classroom. Some of your students are walking along the edge of a roof all the time, emotionally, socially, with substances, with home life. A parapet in that setting might be a clear routine, a check-in, a rule you enforce even when it's inconvenient, because you can see the drop and they can't.

So the specific rule, put a railing on your flat roof, doesn't bind you. Your roof is pitched and covered in snow half the year. But the reason binds you completely. Build the railing before someone falls.

Second law. Gleaning, and leaving the edges of the field.

This one's in Leviticus chapter nineteen and again in chapter twenty-three, and in Deuteronomy chapter twenty-four. When you harvest your field, don't reap all the way to the edges. Don't go back and pick up what you dropped. Don't strip your vineyard bare or go over the olive tree a second time. Leave it for the poor, the foreigner, the widow and the orphan.

Now, what was the reason inside it? A few things at once.

One, it was a welfare system without handouts. The poor weren't given a cheque. They were given access. They had to go out and work the edges and pick up the leftovers. That meant dignity. They provided for themselves, with their own hands, on someone else's land.

Two, it limited the landowner. It said to the farmer, you don't get to squeeze every last dollar out of what you own. Your profit has a built-in margin, and that margin belongs to someone else. God is the real owner of the land. You're a tenant who's been told to leave some for the neighbours.

Three, it built relationships. The poor were physically in the rich man's field. They had to be seen. The whole book of Ruth turns on this law. Ruth, a foreign widow, goes gleaning, and ends up in the field of Boaz. And Boaz doesn't just obey the minimum. He tells his workers to drop extra on purpose. That's the law working at its best, a good man taking the principle further than the rule.

So the general equity is something like this. Don't run your life to maximum extraction. Build margins into your work and your money on purpose, and design those margins so that people in need can reach them with dignity.

How does that land for you? You're a systems guy. You love to optimize. That's a gift. But the gleaning law is a check on optimizing everything. When you plan your budget, is there an edge of the field left unharvested? Money that's set aside before the month starts, not as leftover, but on purpose, for someone else? When you sell on Marketplace, do you squeeze every buyer for the last ten dollars, or do you sometimes leave the edge for the guy who's clearly struggling?

And think about your program. Shifting Gears is, in a way, a gleaning field. A lot of your students are the ones the regular system harvested past. They fell off the cart. And what does your program do? It doesn't just give them credits. It gives them a place to work, to earn something with their own hands, with dignity. That's the gleaning principle in an Ontario school. The rule about Israelite barley doesn't bind you. The reason behind it might be the best description of your job you'll ever hear.

So, one line on gleaning. Leave margins on purpose, and make them reachable by the people who need them.

Third law. Don't muzzle the ox.

This one is short. Deuteronomy chapter twenty-five. Don't muzzle an ox while it's treading out the grain.

Here's the picture. Oxen walked in circles over the harvested grain to separate the kernels. They were surrounded by food all day. A stingy farmer might put a muzzle on so the animal couldn't snack. God says no. The one doing the work gets to eat from the work.

Now, on its own, that's a law about animal welfare, and it tells you something about God. He cares how you treat an animal that can't complain. Proverbs says the righteous man regards the life of his beast. That's already meaningful.

But this law is special, because we don't have to guess at its general equity. The apostle Paul tells us. In First Corinthians chapter nine, he quotes it, and then he asks, is it for oxen that God is concerned? Doesn't he say it for our sake? And he applies it to people who work in ministry. The one who plows should plow in hope of sharing the crop. He uses it again in First Timothy chapter five, about elders who teach and preach, the labourer deserves his wages.

Paul isn't saying God doesn't care about oxen. He's arguing from the smaller to the larger. If God cares that much about fairness to an animal, how much more does he care about fairness to a person who works. And notice what Paul just did. He took a so-called civil law, an agricultural regulation, and pulled out the general equity, and applied it in a totally new setting. He showed us how to do it.

So the principle is: people who do the work should share in the fruit of the work. Don't exploit the ones who serve you.

This one lands right in your church leadership. You're part of the group that decides how pastors and staff get treated. The ox law is Paul's own argument for paying them fairly and caring for them well. Not lavishly. But not muzzled either. Are your church's workers walking in circles all day, surrounded by the fruit of their work, and not allowed to eat?

It lands in your classroom too. When your students work, in the shop, on a project, in a co-op placement, do they get to taste the fruit? Do they see the deck finished? Do they get to take something home? Do they get credit, real credit, for what they did? A lot of disengaged kids have spent years being muzzled. Doing tasks that never fed them. The ox law says, let the worker eat.

So, one line on the ox. The one who works should eat from the work, and Paul himself shows us that's how to read these laws.

Part four. Why this gives the law weight.

Now let's go after the heart of your question. You said you know the laws show God is particular. But you wanted more gravity. So here's the gravity.

The laws were not arbitrary. They were wise. And the wisdom transfers.

Let's take those three phrases one at a time.

First, not arbitrary. There's a way of reading the odd laws that makes God sound like a boss who invents rules just to see if you'll follow them. Wear the blue shirt on Tuesdays because I said so. And yes, some laws were meant to test and mark Israel as different, and we'll get to those in part two. But even those had reasons. What we just saw is that the more you look, the more you find that God's rules are fitted to real life. Real roofs. Real harvests. Real oxen. Real people who fall and get hungry and get exploited.

Second, wise. Here's something worth hearing. In Deuteronomy chapter four, Moses tells Israel that when the other nations hear these laws, they'll say, surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people. What other nation has laws as righteous as these? The law was supposed to make Israel look smart. It was meant to be a display window for the world. Look at a society that lives under the true God. It's safer. It's kinder to the poor. It's fairer to workers. It even treats its animals well.

That's a different picture from God is just picky. God is not picky. God is careful. There's a difference. Picky is about the preferences of the one making the rules. Careful is about the good of the people the rules protect. The God of these laws is careful the way a good builder is careful. Every spec has a reason, and most of the reasons are about somebody not getting hurt.

Gordon Wenham, a British Old Testament scholar who wrote a widely used commentary on Leviticus, makes a related point about even the hardest of these laws. He argues that they're held together by the idea of holiness, that God's people were to reflect his own character in every part of life. Not just at the temple. In the field, on the roof, at the threshing floor. That's the other side of the gravity. These laws aren't only wise. They're a portrait. They show you what God is like, by showing you what a community shaped by him does.

And third, the wisdom transfers. This is the part that should move you from interesting to obligated. If the reason inside each law is wise, and the reason comes from God's own character, then the reason didn't expire. God's character didn't change when Israel's state ended. He still cares about the person who might fall. He still cares about the poor at the edge of the field. He still cares about the worker who's been muzzled.

So here's the real answer to your question. Do each and every one of the ignored laws have a meaningful application? I'd say this, carefully. Every one of them had a reason. Not every reason is equally obvious to us, and some we'll only partly understand, because we don't live in that world. But the posture we should take toward every one of them is that there's wisdom inside, and it's worth digging for. We don't skip them. We open them.

That's a big shift. It turns the Old Testament law from a museum into a workshop. In a museum you look at old tools behind glass and say, how quaint. In a workshop, you pick up the old tool, and you figure out what it was for, and sometimes you find it's still the best tool for the job.

So, one line for this whole part. God's laws are not picky, they're careful, and careful wisdom doesn't expire.

Part five. How this lands in your week.

Let's bring it home to your actual week, because this is where it either changes something or it doesn't.

Start with your devotions. At five thirty, when your reading plan drops you into Leviticus or Deuteronomy, you probably feel your attention slide. Most people do. This gives you a way to stay engaged without turning it into a big study. When you hit a law that seems strange, just ask one question. What was the reason? You don't need to solve it. You don't need commentaries. Just ask what it was protecting. A person? The poor? An animal? The worship of God? Fairness? Most of the time you'll get a decent answer in under a minute, and it'll turn a dead passage into a living one.

Then your construction class. You've now got a Bible verse that is basically a safety rule. That's a great thing to have in your pocket, not to preach at your students, you're in a public school, but for you. When you're tired and a kid is pushing back on safety, you can remember that this isn't just Ontario regulation. This is something God cares about. The railing matters to him. That can give you patience when you have to say it for the fortieth time.

Then your Civics and Careers class. You won't quote Leviticus to them. But you already teach the ideas underneath. Responsibility for danger you control. Room for people who fell behind. Fair pay for work. Now you know where some of that came from.

And when the church budget comes up, the ox law is right there. Paul himself made the application.

Notice that none of this is a new rule. You're not adding laws to your life. You're picking up the reason under laws you used to skip, and letting it shape things you already do.

So, one line. The general equity shows up in your devotions, your shop, your classroom, your wallet and your church.

Part six. One thing to try.

Here's one thing to try. Just one.

This week, find one parapet. One place in your life where you control a danger that someone else might not see coming. And build the railing before anyone falls.

It might be literal. A loose stair tread at home. A tool in your shop that needs a guard. The brakes on your truck that you've been meaning to redo properly. It might be on Marketplace, telling a buyer about the flaw before they find it. It might be a student who's walking close to an edge, where the railing is a phone call home, or a check-in on Monday morning, or a firm rule you've been too tired to enforce.

Pick one. Do it this week. And when you do it, say to yourself, quietly, that this is Deuteronomy chapter twenty-two. That's it. You'll have taken a law most Christians skip and actually obeyed its general equity. Once you've done it once, you'll start seeing the reasons everywhere.
