Listen: Why Airing Our Dirty Laundry Feels So Good

Picture the end of a long day. The bell has gone, the room is finally quiet, and a colleague leans on your doorframe and says, "Can I just tell you about my afternoon?" And then it comes out. The student who would not stop. The email from the office. The thing someone said in the meeting. And you jump in with your own. Ten minutes later you are both laughing, shaking your heads, and you walk to the parking lot feeling lighter.

Or it is the evening at home. Something happened today that bugged you, and you get to finally say it out loud to someone who is on your side. There is a little rush in that. A sense of, ah, finally.

You asked a good question about that feeling. Why does it feel so good to air our dirty laundry with people? What do we actually get from it?

Here is the promise of this episode in one sentence. Airing grievances gives us three real gifts, relief, alliance and being seen, but the way we usually do it can keep the anger burning, and the Bible shows us how to keep the gifts without the harm.

We will walk through it in four ideas. First, what we actually get. Second, why the research says venting often backfires. Third, where Scripture draws the line between the good kind of pouring out and the harmful kind. And fourth, how to tell the difference in the moment, in a hallway, at a kitchen table, or in your own head on a run like this one.

Let's go.

Idea one. What we actually get.

Let's be fair to the feeling first. It is not fake. When you air a grievance with someone, you really do get something. I want to name three things.

The first gift is relief. There is a real physical release in putting a feeling into words. Researchers at UCLA, led by a psychologist named Matthew Lieberman, put people in brain scanners and showed them pictures of angry or frightened faces. When people simply named the feeling, said a word like "angry," the alarm centre of the brain calmed down a bit. The study called this affect labelling. Just naming it takes a little of the heat out. So when you say, "I am so frustrated right now," something in you actually settles. That is the first gift. Relief.

The second gift is alliance. When you tell someone what happened and they say, "No way. That is ridiculous," you are no longer alone with it. You have a teammate. A Belgian psychologist named Bernard Rimé spent decades studying what he called the social sharing of emotion. His work found that people share the large majority of their emotional experiences with someone, usually within hours or days, and mostly with the people closest to them: friends, a spouse, family. And his work found that sharing tends to pull people closer together, even when it does not make the painful memory itself fade. So when you vent to a colleague, you are not just processing a feeling. You are building a bond. Us, together, against the thing. That is the second gift. Alliance.

The third gift is being seen. This one runs the deepest. Underneath most grievances is a question: did anyone notice what that cost me? When you tell the story and someone gets it, you feel known. Your effort was real. Your hurt was real. Someone saw it. For a teacher, this matters a lot, because so much of what you do is invisible. Nobody else saw you stay calm with that student for the fourth time. Nobody saw the extra prep. When you tell a colleague, and they nod, it gets witnessed. That is the third gift. Being seen.

So here is idea one in a line. Airing grievances feels good because it gives us relief, alliance and being seen, and all three are real human needs.

Idea two. Why venting often keeps the anger going.

Now here is the twist. For a long time, a lot of people believed in something called catharsis. The idea goes back to the ancient Greeks, and it got popular again in the last century. It says anger is like steam in a pressure cooker. If you do not let it out, it builds up and explodes. So let it out. Punch a pillow. Yell in the car. Tell everyone what that person did. Then you will be empty and calm.

It is a very intuitive picture. And the research mostly says it is wrong.

The best known test came from a psychologist named Brad Bushman, published in 2002. He brought in about six hundred university students. Each one wrote a short essay, and then got it back with a harsh, insulting evaluation from another student. Something like, this is one of the worst essays I have ever read. So now everyone is angry.

Then they split people into three groups. One group got to hit a punching bag while looking at a photo of the person who insulted them and thinking about that person. That is classic venting. Get it out. A second group hit the same punching bag, but while thinking about getting physically fit. Same exercise, different focus. And the third group just sat quietly for a couple of minutes and did nothing.

Then the study measured how angry everyone felt, and gave them a chance to be aggressive back to the person who insulted them, by blasting that person with loud noise through headphones.

So what happened? The venting group, the ones who hit the bag while thinking about the person, came out the angriest of all three groups. And they were the most aggressive. The group that just sat there quietly did the best. Doing nothing beat venting. Bushman has compared venting to using gasoline to put out a fire.

Let that land for a second. The thing we think releases anger actually rehearses it.

And here is why. When you vent by replaying what someone did, you are practising the anger. Every retelling is a rep. You are training the pathway. Psychologists call that rumination, chewing on something over and over. And the more you chew, the bigger it gets.

Now you might think, okay, but hitting a punching bag alone is not the same as talking with a friend. Talking with a friend is good, right? Fair question. So let's look at that.

A researcher named Amanda Rose has studied something she calls co-rumination. That is when two friends talk about problems over and over, egging each other on, dwelling on the bad feelings, speculating about what it all means. It is venting as a team sport. In one study, she followed over eight hundred kids from grade three to grade nine, for six months.

Here is what the study found. Co-rumination made friendships feel closer. That is the alliance gift we talked about. It really works. But for the girls in particular, co-rumination also predicted rising depression and anxiety over those six months. And then those feelings led to even more co-rumination. A loop. Closer, and also worse.

That is the trap in one picture. The same conversation that bonds us can also deepen the wound. You walk away feeling closer to your friend, and a little more convinced that the world is against you.

You have probably seen this at school. A group of students gets going on how unfair a teacher is, and by the end of lunch, a small annoyance has become a whole story. Each one adds a detail. Nobody calms it down. And adults do it too. Staff rooms can do it. Families can do it. A group chat can do it in about four minutes.

So here is idea two in a line. Venting by replaying the grievance tends to rehearse the anger, not release it, and venting together can bond us while making us feel worse.

Here is the halfway recap. So far we've got two things. One, airing grievances gives real gifts: relief, alliance and being seen. Two, the common way we do it, replaying and stirring the story, tends to keep the anger going instead of draining it.

So the question is not, should I ever tell anyone what is bothering me? The question is, how do I keep the gifts without the harm? And this is where the Bible is surprisingly practical.

Idea three. Where the Bible draws the line.

The first thing to notice is that the Bible is not against speaking your pain. Not at all. Some of the most honest complaints ever written are in Scripture.

Think of the Psalms. About a third of them are laments. Psalm 142 says, "I pour out my complaint before him; I tell my trouble before him." That is airing it. Fully. Psalm 13 starts, "How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?" Job curses the day he was born. Jeremiah writes a whole book called Lamentations. Jesus himself, on the cross, prays the opening line of Psalm 22: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"

So God is not asking you to stuff it down and smile. The Bible gives you words for pain that are rawer than most of us would dare use.

And the Bible also invites you to bring your own dirty laundry into the light. James chapter 5 says, "Confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed." That is airing dirty laundry too. But notice whose laundry. Your own.

So on one side of the line we have lament and confession. Both are good. Both are commanded, really.

Now look at the other side of the line. The Bible is also very clear about grumbling and gossip.

Grumbling first. Israel leaves Egypt, sees the sea split, eats bread from heaven, and then complains. Numbers chapter 11 opens with the people complaining about their misfortunes, and God is displeased. Paul later points back to that story in First Corinthians 10 as a warning. And in Philippians 2 he says plainly, "Do all things without grumbling or disputing."

Then gossip. Proverbs 26 says, "For lack of wood the fire goes out, and where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases." Notice that image. Fire and wood. It is the same picture as the gasoline line from the punching bag study, three thousand years earlier. Whispering is fuel. Stop adding it, and the fire dies down on its own.

So what is the difference? Lament and grumbling can use almost the same words. "This is hard. Why is this happening?" How do you tell them apart?

Here is the simplest way I can put it. There are three questions.

The first question is, who am I talking to? Lament is spoken to God, or with God in the room. It goes up. Grumbling is spoken about God, or about people, sideways, to whoever will agree. Psalm 142 says "before him." The Israelites in the desert were talking among themselves.

The second question is, whose laundry is it? Confession airs my own failures. Gossip airs someone else's. Matthew 18 tells us that if someone sins against us, we go to them, just the two of us. The direction is toward the person, not around them.

The third question is, where is it heading? Lament almost always turns. Read Psalm 13. It starts with "How long?" and six verses later it ends with "I will sing to the Lord, because he has dealt bountifully with me." The complaint is real, and it moves toward trust. Grumbling does not turn. It circles. It is rumination with a religious accent. It stays in the story and keeps adding wood.

So here is idea three in a line. The Bible welcomes lament and confession, honest words that go up to God, about my own stuff, and that move toward trust, and it warns against grumbling and gossip, words that go sideways, about other people, and that circle.

Idea four. How to keep the good without the harm.

Now let's bring the three gifts back. Relief, alliance, being seen. Here is the good news. You can get all three without the harm. You just get them a different way.

Relief first. Remember the labelling study. Naming the feeling helps. Naming it is not the same as replaying the story. "I am angry and discouraged" brings relief. Retelling every line of what that person said, for the fifth time, does not. So you can get relief by saying what you feel, briefly, honestly, and then stopping. The Psalms do exactly this. They name the feeling hard and plain.

Alliance second. The alliance we get from venting is real, but it is built on a shared enemy. That kind of bond has a cost. It quietly turns colleagues into a team against a student, or a family into a team against a neighbour. A better alliance is built on a shared hope. A friend who says, "That sounds really hard. What do you need?" is still on your side. They are just on the side of the real you, not the angry version of you. Proverbs 27 says "faithful are the wounds of a friend." The best ally is not the one who adds wood. It is the one who helps you see clearly.

There is a useful piece of research here too. Ethan Kross, a psychologist at the University of Michigan, has studied something called self-distancing. In one set of studies, with Brad Bushman again, people were made angry, and then asked to think about what happened in one of two ways. Some replayed it as if they were in it again, through their own eyes. Others were told to picture the scene from a distance, like a fly on the wall, watching themselves. The fly on the wall group felt less angry, and they were less aggressive afterward. They still thought about it. They just stepped back.

That is what a good friend does for you. They help you be the fly on the wall. And it is what lament does too. When you bring it to God, you step back and see it from a bigger place.

Being seen third. This is the deepest gift, and here the gospel has the best news. The hunger underneath most grievances is to be noticed. To have someone say, I saw what that cost you. And the Bible says God does. Hagar, alone in the wilderness, calls God "the God who sees me." Psalm 56 says God keeps count of our tossings and puts our tears in his bottle. Jesus sees the widow put in two small coins when nobody else noticed. Hebrews says God is not unjust to forget your work and the love you have shown.

So if the deepest thing you want from airing grievances is to be seen, there is someone who already sees all of it. Every quiet moment of patience with a hard student. Every extra hour. You do not need a crowd to witness it, because you already have a witness. That takes a lot of the pressure off. You can tell a friend, but you do not need the whole staff room to agree.

So here is idea four in a line. You can keep relief by naming the feeling instead of replaying the story, keep alliance by choosing friends who help you step back, and keep being seen by remembering that God already sees.

Now, how does this land in your actual week?

Let's start in the classroom. Your students vent constantly. Some of them have very good reasons to. And now you know something useful. When a student is replaying a grievance with a group, adding wood, you do not have to shut down the feeling. You can help them name it and step back. "Sounds like you are really angry about that. If you were watching this from the back of the room, what would you see?" That is the fly on the wall move, and it is something you can teach. It also gives you a way to think about your own reactions at the end of a tough day.

Then the staff room, or the hallway after the bell. You do not have to become the person who never says anything is hard. That would be a lie, and it would cut you off from the real gifts. But you can notice when a conversation turns from lament into the circling kind. A simple test: are we naming how we feel, or building a case against a person? If it is the second, you can be the one who stops adding wood. Sometimes just a question does it. "What do you think is going on for that kid?" That one question turns the conversation toward understanding.

Then home. Home is where we air the most laundry, because it is where we feel safest. That is good. Home should be a place where you can say, "Today was hard." But it is worth watching for the replay loop at the dinner table. If the same grievance comes up three nights in a row, it is not being released. It is being rehearsed. That might be a signal it needs to go somewhere else. Up, to God. Or forward, to the actual person, Matthew 18 style.

And money, quickly. Grievances about money are some of the stickiest. The cost of something, the bill that came in, what someone else has. Those are often grumbling in disguise. Numbers chapter 11 is literally about food. "We remember the fish we ate in Egypt." Gratitude is the antidote the Bible keeps pointing to, not because the problem is fake, but because grumbling forgets what has already been given.

And faith. This might be the biggest shift. Many Christians feel they should not complain to God at all. That it would be disrespectful. But the Psalms show the opposite. God would rather hear your honest complaint than your polite silence. Lament is a form of faith. It is saying, I still believe you are there, and I still believe you care, which is why I am telling you this. Grumbling, by contrast, has quietly stopped believing that. So the move is not from complaining to silence. It is from complaining sideways to complaining upward.

Here is one thing to try. Only one.

The next time you feel the pull to air a grievance, before you tell anyone else, say it to God first, in one or two plain sentences, like a psalm. Name the feeling. Name what it cost you. Then end with one line of trust, even a small one, like Psalm 13 does. Something like, "Lord, I am angry about what happened today, and I feel like no one noticed how hard I worked. But you saw it, and I trust you with it." You can do it on a run, in the car, or walking to the parking lot. Then, if you still want to tell a friend, go ahead. Just notice whether it feels different. Most people find the second telling is shorter and lighter, because the deepest need has already been met.

That's it. Up first, then sideways.

Let's close with a recap in three sentences.

Airing grievances feels good because it gives us real gifts: relief, alliance and being seen. But replaying and stirring the story, alone or together, tends to rehearse the anger instead of releasing it, which is what the research and Proverbs both say about adding wood to the fire. The Bible welcomes lament and confession, honest words that go up to God, about your own stuff, and that turn toward trust, and that is how you keep the good without the harm.

I want to leave you with one line from Alistair Begg, of Truth For Life, from his devotional on the cost of complaining, written about Israel grumbling in the wilderness and about us. He says: "The cross lies behind you, heaven lies before you, and the Spirit dwells within you."
