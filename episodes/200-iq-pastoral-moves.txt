Cold open

It's Sunday morning. The service has ended. People are standing around with coffee, and you're standing with them. You can see maybe forty people from where you are. Some are laughing. Some are talking about the hockey game. One man is standing near the back wall, holding his cup with both hands, not really talking to anyone. He was there last week too. You noticed him then. You meant to say something. You didn't.

Now here's the question. What's the smart move right now? Not the friendly move. Not the obvious move. The smart move. The one an old, wise shepherd would make without even thinking about it.

That's what this episode is about.


The promise

Here's the promise, in one sentence. By the end of this run, you'll have four quiet, non-obvious moves that wise Reformed pastors use to care for people, and one small thing to try this week.

These come from people like Tim Challies, writers at The Gospel Coalition and 9Marks, the Puritan pastor Richard Baxter, and a counselor named Michael Emlet. And I'll tie a couple of them to some research from psychology that lines up surprisingly well.

One more thing before we start. You've told yourself something about the way you play chess. You make a move without asking whether there's a better one. And you've said you do the same thing in life. So as we go, I want you to hear each of these four moves as a better move. Not a rule. A better move. The kind you'd find if you paused and asked, is there a better one here?

Let's go.


Section one. The list that turns into a text.

Here's the first idea. Pray with a list, and then let the list push you into contact.

Most of us pray for people in a fog. We say, Lord, bless our church. Be with everyone who's struggling. And that's not wrong. But it's vague. And vague prayer tends to stay in your head. It doesn't reach anybody's phone.

The wise move is to make the prayer specific, and then to make it known.

9Marks has a short article by an elder named Alex Bloomfield, who serves at a church in Toronto. He describes something his elders call a care list. It's simple. At the elders meeting, the chair asks one question. Does anyone here need special attention? And the elders bring up names. A marriage that's under strain. Someone whose health is going downhill. Someone who's drifting.

Here's the part I want you to notice. A name goes on the list only when the elders agree. And it stays on the list until the situation is resolved. Being on the list means the elders keep praying for that person, and keep checking in on them, regularly.

Do you hear what that does? It turns prayer from a feeling into a system. The list remembers when you forget.

And Bloomfield points out something even smarter. When there's a big crisis, like a cancer diagnosis or a death, the church floods in at first. Meals. Cards. Visits. And then, after a few weeks, it all fades. But the grief doesn't fade. The care list keeps the person in front of the church after the first wave of help is gone. That's a 200 IQ move. Anybody can show up in week one. The wise shepherd shows up in week seven.

Now let's add the second half of this move. Let the list push you into contact.

Here's where the research comes in. In 2022, a researcher named Peggy Liu, at the University of Pittsburgh, published a set of studies on what she called the surprise of reaching out. People were asked to reach out to someone they hadn't talked to in a while. A short text, a short note, a small gift. Then the researchers asked the senders how much they thought it would be appreciated. And they asked the receivers how much they actually appreciated it.

The pattern was the same across many experiments. The senders guessed low. The people who got the message appreciated it more than the senders expected. And the more surprising the message was, the bigger the gap.

There's an older study that matches it. In 2018, Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley at the University of Chicago had people write letters of gratitude. The writers predicted how the person getting the letter would feel. They guessed the person would feel a bit awkward. They guessed it would be a small thing. They were wrong. The people getting the letters felt much better than the writers predicted, and much less awkward.

So put those two together. You're probably underestimating how much a short message matters. Which means you probably send too few of them.

Here's what this looks like as a pastoral move. You pray for someone by name in the morning. Then, before you put your phone down, you send them two lines. Something like, I prayed for you this morning. Is there anything specific I can be praying about?

That's it. That's the move.

What works here is that it's short, it's true, and it asks a real question. What doesn't work is turning it into a paragraph, or adding a Bible verse they didn't ask for, or making it sound like a check-in from the church office. It should sound like you. And here's one that seems like it would work but doesn't. Saying, let me know if you need anything. It sounds kind. But it hands all the work back to the hurting person. They almost never answer it. A specific question is better than an open offer.

So, the first move in one line. Pray from a list, then send the two-line text.


Section two. Know the sheep, in their own homes.

Here's the second idea. You can't shepherd people you don't know, and you won't know them from the lobby.

This one goes back to Richard Baxter. Baxter was a Puritan pastor in the town of Kidderminster, in England, in the 1600s. He's famous for something very simple. He and his assistant set out to visit every family in the parish, in their homes, and talk with them about their souls. Not a sermon. A conversation. Family by family.

A pastor named Jeramie Rinne wrote about how his church tried this. He calls it the Baxter Project. His elders had been acting more like a board of trustees, managing the building and the budget. They wanted to become shepherds. So they set a goal. Over one year, every member would get a visit at home, or be hosted at an elder's home, or be taken out for dinner.

The visits were about an hour. And here's the key. They were meant to be informal conversations, not interviews. Nobody had a clipboard.

But they did have a few good questions in their back pocket. Let me give you some of them, and you can just let them sit in your mind.

How did you come to faith in Jesus?

What have you read in the Bible lately that hit you?

What do you want to grow in this year?

Do you feel like you know people here?

What burdens can we pray for?

That's it. Simple questions. But notice what they do. They move from story, to Scripture, to growth, to belonging, to burdens. They go from easy to deeper.

Now, the smart part of this isn't the questions. The smart part is the setting. The home. When you sit at someone's kitchen table, you see things you'd never see at church. The kids' drawings on the fridge. The pile of bills on the counter. The way a husband and wife talk to each other. You learn more in an hour at a kitchen table than in a year of Sunday handshakes.

This is where Tim Challies comes in. Challies writes about hospitality as part of the character of every Christian, not just elders. He goes back to the Greek word for hospitality, which means love of strangers. And he has this line I like. Hospitality is a tangible, outward display of godly character.

Tangible. Something you can touch. A table. A meal. A couch.

Challies also says hospitality means making yourself available, so that people don't sense you're too busy for them. That's a quiet one. People can feel whether you're available. They can tell if you're looking past them to the next thing. So part of hospitality happens before anyone walks in your door. It's in your face and your posture on Sunday morning.

Here's what works. Inviting people who aren't already your friends. Challies is direct about this. Welcome people who are different from you, even people who make you a little uncomfortable. What doesn't work is hosting only the easy people, the ones you'd have over anyway. That's not bad. It's just not shepherding.

And here's one that seems like it would work but doesn't. Waiting until your house is perfect. Waiting until you've got a great meal planned. Waiting until there's a natural opening. That opening almost never comes. The simple invite this week beats the perfect dinner someday.

So, the second move in one line. Know the sheep at their tables and yours, with a few good questions.


Halfway recap

Let's pause. We're about halfway through.

So far we've got two moves. First, pray from a list, and let the list push you into a two-line text. The research says people value that kind of message more than you'd guess. Second, get to know people in homes, not lobbies. Use a few simple questions that go from easy to deeper.

Both of those are about noticing people. Now we turn to the harder part. What do you do once you've noticed?


Section three. Every person is three people.

Here's the third idea, and it's the one that I think is most 200 IQ of all.

Michael Emlet is a counselor and doctor who works with an organization called CCEF, the Christian Counseling and Educational Foundation. He wrote a book called Saints, Sufferers, and Sinners. And the whole book is built on one idea. Every Christian you care for is all three at the same time.

A saint. Someone made new in Christ, with the Spirit living in them, who belongs to God.

A sufferer. Someone living in a broken world, carrying pain, loss, weakness, and things that were done to them.

A sinner. Someone who still wanders, still chooses wrong, and still needs to repent.

All three. All the time. In every person. Including you.

Here's why this is so smart. Most of us have a default lens. We see people through one of the three, and we stop there.

Some people see everyone as a sinner. Every problem is a sin problem. Every conversation turns into a correction. Those people can be right on the facts and still crush someone who's hurting.

Some people see everyone as a sufferer. Every problem is pain. They're very kind. But they never say the hard thing, even when it's needed. And people stay stuck.

Some people only see the saint. Everything's great. You're doing awesome. God's got this. That feels nice, but it's thin. It doesn't touch what's actually going on.

The wise shepherd holds all three. And here's the practical move. Before you walk into a hard conversation, ask yourself one question. Which one am I about to miss?

Let me make that concrete. Say there's a man in your church who's been short with people. He snapped at someone in your small group last week. Your first instinct might be to treat him as a sinner. Talk to him about his anger. And maybe that conversation needs to happen. But if you ask, which one am I missing, you might remember that his dad is in the hospital. He's a sufferer too. And you might remember the time he quietly fixed somebody's car for free. He's a saint too.

Now the conversation changes. You can start with the saint. Hey, I've seen the way you serve people. You start with the sufferer. How's your dad doing? How are you doing with all of it? And then, from inside that care, you can talk about the snapping. And he's much more likely to hear it.

Here's what works in hard conversations. Asking before telling. Starting with what's true and good in them. Naming the hard thing plainly, once, without a long windup. And here's what doesn't work. Starting with the problem cold. Saying, I've been hearing some things. That phrase almost always puts people on defence, because now they're wondering who's been talking about them.

Here's one that seems like it would work but doesn't. The compliment sandwich. Something nice, then the hard thing, then something nice. People can smell it. They learn to brace every time you say something nice. That's different from what Emlet is saying. He's not saying wrap the hard thing in sugar. He's saying actually see the whole person, because the whole person is real.

And notice how this connects to your chess thing. The impulse move is to react to the one lens you see first. The better move is to pause, and ask, what else is true about this person? It's a two-second question. But it changes the whole board.

So, the third move in one line. Every person is a saint, a sufferer, and a sinner, so ask which one you're about to miss.


Section four. The ministry of just being there.

Here's the fourth idea. Sometimes the wisest pastoral move is simply to be there, and to keep being there.

Tim Challies wrote a piece called The Ministry of Presence. And the setting is interesting. His church sent a pastor and about forty members to help a shrinking church nearby. And that church started to grow again. When Challies looked at who made the biggest difference, it wasn't always the gifted people. It wasn't the great speakers or the musicians. Some of the most valuable people were the ones known for just being there.

He puts it like this. The ministry of presence is the ministry of being there, of simply gathering with the church on Sunday. And about those people, he said, they were there, always there.

What did they actually do? They came early. They stayed late. They greeted people. They talked to people. They came to the midweek things too, the Bible studies, the prayer meetings. They saw the church as brothers and sisters, not as an event they attended. And you can't do one another ministry if you're not with one another.

Now here's the part that's quietly brilliant. Challies says the ministry of presence starts the night before. Many people mean to be at church, but they're not ready. So he gives practical advice. Stop the tiring stuff the night before. Get to bed on time. Set an alarm. Make sure the kids have something to wear.

That might sound like the least spiritual advice in this whole episode. But think about it. Your best pastoral work on Sunday morning might depend on what time you turn off the lights on Saturday night.

So let's go back to the cold open. The man by the back wall, holding his cup with both hands. The ministry of presence says you can only notice him if you're there, and not rushing out. Arriving early gives you time before the service, when people are a little more open. Staying late gives you the coffee time, when the lonely person is easiest to spot. The person leaving right at the end of the last song will never see him.

And now let's tie this back to the first move. Remember the care list, and how help fades after the first few weeks. Presence is the same idea, stretched out over time. Anyone can show up once. The 200 IQ move is to keep showing up. Week after week. Month after month. That's what makes people trust you when the hard conversation finally comes.

Here's what works. Being predictable. Being the person who's always there, in roughly the same spot, with time to talk. What doesn't work is trying to do big, dramatic care once in a while, and then disappearing. And here's one that seems like it would work but doesn't. Being so busy serving on Sunday that you never stand still. Running the sound, setting up, tearing down, always moving. That's real service. But a person who's always moving is a hard person to approach. Sometimes the shepherd needs to stand still with a coffee.

So, the fourth move in one line. Be there early, stay late, and keep being there.


How this lands in your week

Let's bring this home. Where does this actually fit in your week?

Start with your elders meetings. You already take the minutes and send them out. That's a quiet, powerful spot. What if the minutes carried a short care list? Just names the elders agreed on, with one line each, so nobody falls off the radar between meetings. You're already holding the pen. The list could live right there. Of course, that's something to suggest to the other elders, not something to just start doing on your own. That's the kind of move where you pause and ask, is there a better way to bring this up?

Then your missional community. You co-lead it. The Baxter questions fit there really well. Not as a quiz. Just one question, held in your pocket, for when there's a quiet moment with someone. What have you been reading in the Bible lately that hit you? Or, what's one thing you'd like prayer for this month? Over a few weeks, you'd know your group in a whole different way.

Then Sunday mornings. Presence. You already meet with someone early on Sundays. So you're already there early. The question is the back end. Do you stay through the coffee time with your eyes up, looking for the person by the back wall? Or are you already thinking about lunch?

Then home. Hospitality isn't just an elder thing. It's a married thing. And it's a team thing with your wife. The smart move there is to plan it together, not to spring it on her. Ask her who she's been noticing. She probably sees people you miss.

And even your classroom. The saint, sufferer, sinner idea doesn't map perfectly onto students, since you don't know where each of them stands with God. But the heart of it does. Every student is made in the image of God, carrying real pain, and making real choices. When a student blows up in class, the impulse is to see only the choice. The better move is to ask, what else is true about this kid today?

And here's a razor worth holding up against all of this. You tend to treat productivity as the highest thing. But none of these four moves is productive in the way a to-do list is productive. A two-line text, a question at a kitchen table, standing still with a coffee. They don't feel like getting things done. That's exactly why they're easy to skip. And that's exactly why they matter. Love is slow, and it's meant to be.


One thing to try

Here's the one thing. Only one.

This week, pick three names from your church. Not your closest friends. Three people you don't talk to much. Pray for each one by name, on three different mornings. And right after you pray, before your phone goes down, send that person two lines. I prayed for you this morning. Is there anything specific I can be praying about?

That's it. Three names. Three mornings. Three texts.

Remember the research. You'll probably guess it matters less than it does. Send it anyway.

And if someone answers with something heavy, you've just found the first name for a care list.


Closing recap

So let's land it in three sentences.

Pray from a list and let it push you into short, specific contact, because people value it more than you think. Know people at real tables, and see each one as a saint, a sufferer, and a sinner, so you don't miss the part that matters most. And keep showing up, early and late, week after week, because presence is what earns you the right to speak.

Here's a line to finish on. On the Truth For Life blog, Alistair Begg is quoted saying this. The shepherding responsibility of an elder must always take place with the crook of God's Word.

That's Alistair Begg, Truth For Life.

Every move in this episode is just a way to bring the Word close to someone. A text that says I prayed for you. A Bible question at a kitchen table. A hard truth spoken to a whole person. A familiar face, every Sunday. The crook is God's Word. The moves are just how you carry it.
