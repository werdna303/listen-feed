What builds up in your drain pipes, and whether Vaseline helps.


Cold open

Picture the bathroom sink on a weekday morning. You brush your teeth, rinse, and the water sits there for a second longer than it used to. Not a clog. Just a pause. A little swirl that hangs around before it drains. Nobody calls a plumber for that. You shrug, you leave for work, and the sink keeps getting a little slower every week, the way a tire loses air.

A few days ago you said you were just curious about pipe scum buildup. You jotted down two words beside it: washer, and Vaseline. So this episode is about that slow sink. What is actually down there. Why it builds up. What the research says about stopping it. And whether Vaseline, or a washer trick, helps or hurts.

Here's the promise in one sentence. By the end of this run you will know the three things that clog a house drain, the one chemical reaction behind most of it, and the one small habit that does the most good.


Section one. What is actually down there.

Let's start with the big idea. Pipe scum is not one thing. It's three things that team up. Fat, soap, and slime. And hair is the net that holds them all together.

Let's take them one at a time.

The first is fat. In the kitchen that means grease from a frying pan, butter on a plate, the oil left in a bowl after salad, the fat from a pound of ground beef. When it's hot it's a liquid. It pours like water. That's what fools people. As soon as it hits a cool pipe, it starts to thicken and cling.

The second is soap. In the bathroom that's bar soap, body wash, shampoo, shaving cream, and the foam from toothpaste. Soap on its own is meant to rinse away. But soap meets the minerals in your water, and that changes things. We'll come back to that, because it's the heart of the whole episode.

The third is slime. The proper word is biofilm. Bacteria love a wet, dark pipe with a steady trickle of food going past. They stick to the pipe wall and build a kind of glue around themselves. That glue is the grey or black gunk you see on a sink stopper when you pull it up. It smells a bit like a swamp. That's biofilm.

And then there's hair. Hair doesn't dissolve. It catches on the stopper, on rough spots in the pipe, on the bend under the sink. Once a few hairs catch, they become a net. The net catches soap scum. The soap scum feeds the slime. The slime makes everything stickier, so more hair catches. That's the loop.

So here's the big idea again, in one line. Pipe scum is fat, soap and slime, held together by a net of hair.


Section two. The reaction that turns grease into stone.

Here's the second idea, and it's the most interesting one. Some of the worst buildup in pipes is not grease. It's soap. Soap that the pipes made on their own.

This comes from sewer research, the big pipes under the street. Cities have a problem they call fatbergs. Huge, hard lumps that block sewers. For years people assumed those lumps were just cold grease. Grease goes down, grease cools, grease sticks. Simple.

But around 2011, a team at North Carolina State University, led in part by an engineer named Joel Ducoste, looked closely at what those hard deposits were made of. They used a lab tool that shines infrared light on a sample and reads which chemical bonds are there. What they found surprised a lot of people. The hard deposits were mostly calcium soap. Not grease. Soap.

Here's how it works. Fat, out in the pipes, slowly breaks down into pieces called fatty acids. Those fatty acids then meet calcium. Calcium is in water, especially hard water, and it's also in concrete. When fatty acids and calcium meet, they react and form a hard, waxy, insoluble soap. Insoluble means water can't wash it away. The study put it plainly. Grease can't form these deposits on its own. It has to break down first, and then it reacts with calcium.

A British team published a similar finding in the journal Water Research in 2012. They also found the deposits were largely calcium soaps of fatty acids.

So the sewer basically does a slow version of what soap makers do on purpose. Fat plus a mineral makes soap. The difference is that soap from a store is the kind that dissolves. The soap your pipes make is the kind that doesn't. It's closer to candle wax, or a crayon, stuck to the wall.

Now, why does that matter in your house, not just under the street?

Because the same chemistry happens in miniature in your own drains. Think of the ring in a bathtub. That grey film that won't rinse off? That's soap scum. Your body wash or bar soap meets the calcium and magnesium in your water, and they form a sticky solid. It's the same kind of reaction. Fatty acid, plus a hard water mineral, gives you a soap that won't wash away.

In the bathroom sink, the film coats the pipe. Hair sticks to it. Toothpaste adds a little grit. In the kitchen, cooking fat hangs on in the trap under the sink and the pipe after it, and over time some of it turns into that harder waxy stuff.

This is also why the advice to run hot water after pouring grease doesn't really work. Water utilities say this over and over. Hot water just carries the grease a bit further, past the trap, into a pipe in the wall or under the basement floor, where it cools, sticks, and starts that slow reaction. You didn't fix it. You moved it somewhere harder to reach.

The same goes for dish soap. A squirt of dish soap breaks grease into tiny droplets for a while. Great for a pan. But down the pipe, the droplets can come back together and stick again.

So here's the second idea in one line. A lot of hard pipe gunk is soap your pipes made themselves, out of fat and the minerals in your water. Hot water and dish soap just move it along.


Section three. The slime that climbs.

Here's the third idea. The slime in your drain is alive, and it moves.

This comes from hospital research, which sounds unrelated but is the best data anyone has on sink drains. Hospitals care a lot about sink drains because bad bacteria can grow in them and splash out onto counters.

In 2017, a team at the University of Virginia, led by an infectious disease researcher named Amy Mathers, built a lab full of real sinks to watch what happens inside the drain. They published it in a journal called Applied and Environmental Microbiology. What they saw was this. Bacteria settled first in the bend of the pipe under the sink, the trap. That's the U shaped part that always holds a little water. From there, the biofilm grew upward, toward the drain opening, at roughly an inch a day. About an inch a day. So within about a week, it had climbed all the way up to the strainer. Once it reached the top, running water could splash bits of it onto the sink and the counter.

Why does it grow? Because the trap is the perfect home. It's always wet. It's dark. And every time someone rinses a dish, washes their hands, or spits toothpaste, a little food goes by. Soap residue, skin cells, bits of food, toothpaste. The biofilm feeds on all of it.

Now, there's a second reason this matters. The biofilm doesn't just sit there. It acts like glue for everything else. Remember the loop from section one? Hair catches, soap scum sticks to hair, the slime grows on the soap scum, and the slime makes the next hair stick better. Each of the three helps the others.

Two practical points come out of this.

First, the stuff you pull off a stopper is mostly slime and hair and soap. That's normal. Pulling it out every so often is one of the most useful things you can do, and it's free.

Second, you don't need to sterilize a drain. You can't. Bleach kills some of the surface for a short while, but biofilm is built to survive that, and it comes back. The goal is not a germ free pipe. The goal is to stop feeding the loop, and to clear the net before it gets big.

So the third idea in one line. The slime in your drain lives in the trap, climbs about an inch a day, and glues everything else together.


Halfway recap

Let's stop and gather what we've got. Three ideas so far.

One. Pipe scum is fat, soap and slime, and hair is the net that holds them.

Two. A lot of hard buildup is a soap the pipe makes itself, when fat breaks down and meets calcium. That's why hot water and dish soap just push grease further down.

Three. The slime is alive. It grows up from the trap at about an inch a day and acts like glue.

Now let's get to the two words you wrote down. Vaseline, and washer.


Section four. Vaseline and the washer trick.

Here's the fourth idea. Vaseline is great on the outside of plumbing and bad on the inside of it. And even on the outside, there's usually something better.

Let's start with what Vaseline is. Petroleum jelly is a blend of waxes and oils from petroleum. That matters for two reasons.

The first reason is that it doesn't mix with water. At all. That's why it works on chapped lips. It forms a barrier water can't get through.

The second reason is that, unlike kitchen fat, it isn't made of fatty acids. So it can't turn into soap. That sounds like good news, but it isn't. Kitchen fat at least has a slow path to breaking down. Petroleum jelly mostly doesn't. Soap won't lift it well. Bacteria barely touch it. Hot water softens it for a moment, and then it firms up again further down.

So what happens if Vaseline goes down a drain? Say from washing your hands after using it, or wiping a jar, or someone cleaning a greasy tool in the laundry sink. It coats the pipe wall with a sticky film that water can't rinse. And what does a sticky film in a pipe do? It catches hair. It catches lint. It gives the slime somewhere to start. In other words, it feeds the exact loop we just talked about. A little bit on your hands is nothing to worry about. But scooping it or wiping it into a drain is a bad idea. Paper towel, garbage.

Now the washer part. There are two ways to read the word washer, so let's do both quickly.

The first meaning is the little rubber washer or O ring inside a tap, a drain stopper, or a slip joint under the sink. You may have heard the trick of rubbing a bit of Vaseline on a washer so it seals better and turns smoothly. And that trick is half right. A thin coat of grease on a rubber seal does help it slide into place, seal well, and not tear.

But here's the catch. Petroleum based products can cause some kinds of rubber to swell, go soft or break down over time. Pool and spa makers warn about this a lot for their O rings. And the common rubbers in plumbing seals are among the ones that don't love petroleum. That's why hardware stores sell something called plumber's grease, which is made with silicone. Silicone grease is water resistant, safe on rubber, and safe for drinking water fittings. It does what people want the Vaseline to do, without slowly wrecking the seal.

So for a washer or O ring in a tap or a drain fitting, the honest answer is this. Vaseline will probably work for a while. Silicone plumber's grease is the right tool. A small tube costs a few dollars and lasts years. And if you already own one, which is worth checking before you buy anything, that's the one to use.

The second meaning of washer is the washing machine. And that one has its own buildup story.

A washing machine drain carries out three things. Lint, which is tiny fibres from clothes. Detergent. And, if you use it, fabric softener. Lint acts like hair in a bathroom sink. It's the net. Detergent, especially when people use too much, leaves a film. And liquid fabric softener is built from waxy, fatty compounds meant to coat fabric. Some of that coats the hose and the pipe too. Many appliance repair guides point to over dosing detergent and heavy softener use as a common cause of gunk in washer drains and standpipes.

The well known washer trick here is cheap and simple. A mesh lint trap on the end of the drain hose. It looks like a little sock that slips over the hose and catches lint before it reaches the pipe. You empty or replace it every so often. That trick actually does what it says. It catches the net before the net forms.

And the other washer trick is even simpler. Use the amount of detergent the bottle says, or a bit less. Most people use more than they need. More soap doesn't mean cleaner clothes. It means more film.

So the fourth idea in one line. Keep Vaseline out of drains, use silicone grease on rubber washers, and catch lint before it gets into the washing machine drain.


Section five. What actually prevents buildup.

Here's the last big idea, and it's the practical one. The research on prevention is less about products and more about habits. And the single biggest habit is keeping fat and hair out of the pipe in the first place.

Let's be honest about the evidence. Most of the solid research on pipe buildup comes from sewer systems. Cities spend a lot of money on fat, oil and grease programs. What those programs keep finding is that the best fix is upstream. Stop it at the sink. Scrape plates. Wipe pans. Let fat cool and put it in the garbage. Many water utilities run campaigns on just that.

For the inside of a house, the evidence is thinner and mostly comes from plumbers and testing labs, not big studies. So here's what holds up, with how sure we can be.

The strongest one. Strainers and hair catchers. A simple mesh or silicone catcher in the bathroom sink, the tub and the kitchen sink stops hair and food before they reach the trap. If hair is the net, a strainer means the net never gets built. This is cheap, it works, and nobody really argues about it.

Second. Wiping greasy pans and plates with a used paper towel before washing. This keeps the fat out completely. It's what water utilities ask for, and it lines up with the chemistry. No fat in the pipe, no calcium soap.

Third. Pulling and cleaning stoppers every few weeks. That's the part of the drain where the slime and hair meet. A quick pull and rinse breaks the loop before it grows.

Now, some things that are popular but weaker.

Baking soda and vinegar. It fizzes, and that feels like something is happening. But mostly what you get is salty water and carbon dioxide. It may loosen a little surface gunk. It won't dissolve a hair clog or hard soap. Not harmful. Just not strong.

Boiling water. On metal pipes it can soften fresh grease for a moment. But many house drains are plastic, and very hot water poured straight in can be hard on plastic fittings over time. And as we said, it mostly moves grease further down. So a kettle of boiling water is not the fix it's often made out to be.

Enzyme or bacteria drain cleaners. These claim to eat buildup slowly. There's some sense to the idea, but there's not much independent research on how well they work in a house drain. If you try one, treat it as an experiment, not a sure thing.

Harsh chemical drain openers. These are strong, they work on some clogs, and they're hard on pipes, on skin and on eyes. Plumbers tend to suggest a cheap plastic drain snake first. It's a thin strip with little barbs that you push down the drain and pull back out, and it brings the hair net up with it. It's a bit gross. It costs a few dollars. And it usually works better than a bottle.

So here's the fifth idea in one line. Prevention is mostly habit. Catch hair, wipe fat, pull the stopper. Products come second.


How this lands in your week

Let's bring this home. Literally.

At home. Your house has three kinds of drains and three kinds of buildup. The kitchen is the fat drain. The bathroom is the soap and hair drain. The laundry is the lint and detergent drain. If you think of each one by what feeds it, you know what to catch. Strainer in the bathroom. Paper towel by the stove. Lint trap on the washer hose. That's the whole system.

And there's an easy joint habit here with your wife. Not a lecture, just a shared rule. Fat goes in the garbage, not the sink. A jar or an old can by the stove for cooled grease makes it automatic. Small, quiet, and it saves you both a plumber call later.

On money. A plumber visit for a clogged drain can easily run a couple hundred dollars, and more after hours. A strainer, a drain snake and a tube of silicone grease together cost less than a takeout lunch. And before buying any of them, it's worth a look in the garage or under the sink. You may already own a strainer or a snake. No need to buy what's already in a drawer.

In the classroom. There's a lesson hiding in here for students, and it's a good one for a class that works on practical life skills. The idea that hot water just moves the grease somewhere else is a perfect picture of a problem we push down the line instead of solving. Kids get that right away. It also makes a decent, quick science moment. Fat plus a mineral makes soap. That's chemistry they can see in a bathtub ring. If you use it, keep it small and see whether it lands. Some students will love the gross factor. Some won't care. Both are fine.

And in faith. There's an old picture in the Song of Songs about the little foxes that spoil the vineyards. Not the big wolves. The little foxes. A drain doesn't clog from one big mistake. It clogs from a thousand small rinses that each seemed fine. A little grease. A few hairs. One more squirt of soap. Nothing big enough to notice, until the water stops moving.

That's how most drift works in a life too. Not a dramatic fall. A slow film. A little neglect that each day seems too small to matter. And the answer is the same as in the pipe. Not a dramatic purge once a year. A small, steady habit of keeping the net from forming. Catching things early, while they're still just a few hairs.

And here's one of your own principles that fits right here. You've said you tend to make a quick move without asking if there's a better one, in chess and in life. A drain is a tiny daily version of that. The pan is greasy, the sink is right there, and the quick move is to rinse it. The pause is one second long. Is there a better place for this? Usually yes. The garbage.


One thing to try

Just one thing. For the next week, every time a greasy pan, plate or bowl is about to go in the sink, wipe it first with a used paper towel or a napkin, and put that in the garbage. That's it. Not the strainer. Not the snake. Just the wipe.

Why this one? Because it hits the biggest source of hard buildup, it costs nothing, and it builds the exact pause you've said you want to practise. At the end of the week, ask yourself one question. Did it become automatic, or did it slip? If it slipped, put the roll of paper towel right beside the sink so the better move is also the easy one.


Closing recap

Three sentences to take with you.

Pipe scum is fat, soap and slime held together by hair, and much of the hard stuff is a soap the pipes make from fat and the minerals in your water.

The slime lives in the trap and climbs, so the real fix is catching hair and keeping fat out, not hot water or a bottle of something.

Keep Vaseline out of the drain, use silicone grease on rubber washers, and put a lint catcher on the washing machine hose.
