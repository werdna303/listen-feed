Listen. The forgetting curve and retrieval practice.
For your run. About 30 minutes.

It's Monday morning. Your class is settling in. Someone is still eating a granola bar. Someone else has their hood up and their head down.

Last Thursday, you taught a good lesson in Civics. It went well. They were with you. You talked about the three levels of government, and who handles what. A kid even made a joke about the mayor fixing potholes, and everyone laughed. You walked out of that room thinking, they got it.

So now you ask a simple question. Which level of government runs the schools in Ontario?

Silence. A shrug. One student says, the mayor? Another says, the prime minister. The kid who made the pothole joke looks at you like he has never heard the word government in his life.

And you feel that little drop in your stomach. Where did it go? It was there on Thursday. You saw it.

Here's the promise for this run. By the time you finish, you'll know why most of what we learn disappears, and you'll have one simple habit that makes learning stick, for your students, and for you.

Part one. The forgetting curve.

Let's start in Germany, in the 1880s. A man named Hermann Ebbinghaus wanted to measure memory. Not talk about it. Measure it.

He had a problem, though. Real words come with baggage. Some words you already know. Some remind you of things. So he invented his own material. Short, made up syllables. Three letters each. A consonant, a vowel, a consonant. Things like ZOF, or WID. Nonsense. Nothing to hang them on.

And he was his own test subject. For years. He would sit down, learn a list of these nonsense syllables until he could say it perfectly, and then wait. Twenty minutes. An hour. A day. A month. Then he would come back and learn the list again, and time how long it took the second time.

That was his clever trick. If the second learning went faster, some memory was still there. He called that the savings. The more he saved, the more he had kept.

What he found has a famous name now. The forgetting curve.

Here's the shape of it. Picture yourself running down a steep hill that flattens out into a long, gentle road. Right after learning, memory drops fast. Very fast. Much of the loss happens in the first hours and the first day. Then the drop slows down. What survives that first crash tends to hang around a lot longer.

By the next day, Ebbinghaus had lost most of the benefit of his first learning. His savings were down to about a third. So two thirds of the work, in a sense, had to be done again.

Now, I want to be fair to the study. It was one man. Learning nonsense. Your students are not learning nonsense, and meaningful material is easier to hold. But here's the remarkable part. Over a century later, researchers in the Netherlands repeated his experiment, with one person, carefully, the old way. The curve came out with almost the same shape. Steep drop. Long flattening.

So the shape is real. The exact numbers change with the person and the material. The shape doesn't.

Let's bring it home. Think about your Thursday Civics lesson. It was clear. It was engaging. They understood it in the moment. And then they walked out the door into lunch, and drama, and phones, and a weekend. By Monday, they were well down the steep part of that hill.

That doesn't mean your lesson failed. It means your lesson was the first step, not the whole job.

Or think about yourself. You read a chapter of a book on money. Something on budgeting, or how mortgages work. It made total sense while you read it. A week later, your wife asks you a simple question about it, and you say, I read something about that, hang on. And you can't quite find it.

That's the forgetting curve. It's not a character flaw. It's the default setting of the human brain.

And honestly, it's a good setting. Think about how much comes at you in one day. Every licence plate you pass. Every conversation in the hallway. Every price at the grocery store. If you kept all of it, you'd drown. Your brain is constantly deciding what to keep, and its main clue is simple. Does this keep coming back? Do I keep needing it?

If something shows up once and never again, the brain quietly lets it go. If something keeps being needed, the brain holds on. That's the whole game. And it's the thread that runs through the rest of this episode. Our job, as teachers and as learners, is to send the brain a clear signal. This one matters. Keep it.

One more thing about that curve. Notice where the danger is. It's not a month from now. It's tonight, and tomorrow. The steepest part of the hill is right after the lesson ends. Which means the most valuable moment to do something about forgetting is sooner than most of us think.

So here's the first idea in one line. Forgetting is fast, it's normal, and it starts right away.

Part two. Pulling it out beats putting it in.

Here's the second idea. And it's the big one.

If forgetting is the default, what fights it? Most of us assume the answer is more input. Read it again. Review the notes again. Go over it one more time.

The research says something different. The thing that fights forgetting best is not putting information in. It's pulling information out.

Researchers call this retrieval practice. When you try to remember something, without looking, the act of reaching for it changes the memory. It makes it stronger. It makes it easier to find next time. The test is not just a measuring stick. The test is a workout.

Let me tell you about one study. In 2006, two researchers at Washington University in St. Louis, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, ran an experiment with college students. The students read short passages. Science style prose. The kind of thing you'd find in a textbook.

They split the students into groups. I'll keep it to the two that matter most.

The first group just studied. Read the passage, read it again, read it again, read it again. Four study periods. All reading.

The second group read it once, and then did three recall tests. No passage in front of them. Just a blank page and the instruction, write down everything you remember.

Think about how that feels. The first group is soaking in the material. The second group is struggling. They're staring at a page, digging for scraps.

And in the study, the reading group did, in fact, see the material way more. Something like fourteen times through, on average, compared with three or four times for the testing group.

Then the researchers waited. Some students were tested five minutes later. Others were tested a week later.

At five minutes, the rereaders did a little better. Makes sense. They had just been soaking in it.

But at one week, it flipped. The testing group remembered about sixty percent of the passage. The rereading group remembered about forty percent.

Read that again in your head. The group that read the material far less remembered half again as much a week later. Sixty percent versus forty percent. Because they had practised pulling it out.

And there's one more detail I love. Before the final test, the students were asked to predict how well they'd do a week later. The rereaders were the most confident. The testing group was the least confident. They had it exactly backwards.

Hold onto that. We'll come back to it.

Now let's put this in your classroom. Think about your construction course. You teach the names of tools, and safety rules, and how to read a tape measure to the sixteenth of an inch. You could put up a slide and go over the parts of a circular saw again. Or you could hold up the saw, cover the slide, and say, name three parts and one safety rule. No notes. Turn to your partner. Go.

The second way feels messier. Kids will get things wrong. Some will groan. But the second way is the one that builds the memory.

Now, a fair question. Isn't this just testing? Aren't these kids already sick of tests?

Here's the distinction that matters. The study wasn't about grades. Nobody's mark depended on those practice recalls. The power came from the act of remembering, not from the pressure of being judged. So when I say retrieval practice, don't picture a unit test. Picture a quick, safe, no marks moment where everyone tries to pull something back out.

That can look like a lot of things. A blank page and two minutes to write everything you remember about yesterday. A partner who asks you three questions while you keep your notes closed. A sticky note on the way out the door with one thing you learned today. All of those are retrieval. None of them need a grade.

And one more piece makes it work better. After students try, show them the answer. Let them check. When you reach for something, miss it, and then see the right answer, that correction tends to stick. The miss isn't wasted. It sets the hook.

Or think about chess. You know this one in your bones. You can read through a line of an opening in a book, move by move, nodding along. It all makes sense. Then you sit down at a real game, your opponent plays the third move of that line, and your mind goes blank.

Compare that with covering up the moves and asking yourself, what's the next move here, and why? That's harder. It's slower. You'll miss some. And that's exactly the practice that shows up when the clock is running.

It works in faith too. You can read a psalm ten times. Or you can read it twice, close the Bible, and try to say the first few verses from memory on your run. The second way is the one that puts the words where you can reach them in the middle of a hard day.

So here's the second idea in one line. Every time you pull a memory out, you make it stronger.

Halfway recap.

Let's take a breath. We're about halfway.

So far we've got two things.

First, forgetting is fast. The forgetting curve drops steeply right after learning, then levels off. That's normal. It's how everyone's brain works, including yours, including your students'.

Second, the best way to fight that curve is retrieval. Not rereading. Pulling it out of your head. In the study from 2006, students who practised recall remembered about sixty percent a week later. Students who just reread remembered about forty percent. And the rereaders felt more confident.

Two more ideas to go. One about timing. One about that strange confidence.

Part three. Space it out.

Here's the third idea. When you practise matters almost as much as how you practise.

Think about two ways to study for a test. Option one. You sit down the night before and go hard for three hours. Option two. You do one hour on Monday, one hour on Wednesday, one hour on Friday. Same total time. Three hours either way.

Which one wins? For anything you need to remember for more than a few days, the spaced version wins. Not by a little. Often by a lot.

This is called the spacing effect. And it may be the most well replicated finding in all of memory research. It's been studied for well over a hundred years.

In 2006, a team led by Nicholas Cepeda, who is now at York University in Toronto, went through the research. They pulled together hundreds of experiments. The verdict was clear. Spreading out your practice beats cramming it together. The effect is often large.

But they found something more useful than that. The best gap between study sessions depends on how long you need to remember.

That team followed up a couple of years later with a big study, more than a thousand people, and tested memory as long as a year later. Here's the plain English version of what they found. If you need to remember something for a week, review it again after a day or two. If you need to remember it for a year, the best gap is more like a month.

The rule of thumb is this. The longer you want to keep it, the longer you can wait before you review it. Not forever. But longer than you'd think.

Now, why would waiting help? Isn't the whole point that we forget?

Here's the key. Go back to the forgetting curve. When you review something right after learning it, it's still sitting right there. Pulling it out is easy. Too easy. Your brain doesn't have to work, so it doesn't get much stronger.

When you wait a bit, and some of it has started to fade, pulling it out takes effort. You have to dig. And that digging is what strengthens the memory. Each time you review at the right gap, the curve flattens. You forget a little slower. Then a little slower again.

So spacing and retrieval work together. Spacing makes the retrieval harder. The harder retrieval makes the memory stronger.

Let's bring it into your week.

In phys ed, you're building fitness habits and teaching the reasons behind them. Say you teach the idea of heart rate zones in September. If you never bring it up again, it's gone by October. But if you ask about it again the next week, and then a few weeks later, and then before the unit test, it sticks. Same content. Same total minutes. Just spread out, with retrieval each time.

Or chess again. Say you're learning a new opening. The cramming way is to spend all of Saturday afternoon on it, then not touch it for two months. The spaced way is to drill it for fifteen minutes today, again on Wednesday, again the following week, and then once a month. Same opening. Less total time, probably. But the spaced version is the one that will still be there when you sit down across the board from someone in March.

Here's something worth noticing, though. Spacing feels less productive in the moment. When you cram, you leave the session feeling like you know it cold. When you space, you come back on Wednesday and realise you've forgotten half of it. That's discouraging. It feels like you're going backwards. You're not. That rusty feeling on Wednesday is exactly the moment the memory is getting rebuilt stronger.

Or in your own running. You know this already, actually. You can't cram fitness. Three hard runs on one Saturday don't do what three runs spread across a week do. Your body needs the gap to adapt. Memory is surprisingly similar. The gap is where the building happens.

Or at home with money. Maybe you and your wife sit down in January and talk through the budget. Big numbers. Mortgage, groceries, giving, savings. Then you don't really talk about it again until something goes wrong in June. By then, neither of you remembers what you decided. A short check in once a month, where you each try to say the numbers before you look at the spreadsheet, would keep it alive. Spaced. With retrieval.

So here's the third idea in one line. Spread it out, and let a little forgetting happen before you review.

Part four. The illusion of fluency.

Here's the fourth idea. And it explains why almost nobody does any of this.

Remember the rereaders in the 2006 study? They read the passage many times. They felt great about it. They predicted they would do well. And they were wrong.

Why? Because rereading creates a feeling. Researchers call it fluency. When you read something for the third time, the words flow easily. They look familiar. Your brain says, I know this.

But recognising something is not the same as being able to recall it. Familiar is not the same as learned. That's the illusion. The ease of reading tricks you into thinking the knowledge is in your head, when really it's only on the page.

A few years later, Karpicke and Roediger, with another researcher, Andrew Butler, asked college students how they actually study. Most said they reread their notes or their textbook. About half said rereading was their number one strategy. Very few said they tested themselves as a way to learn.

So the most popular study method is one of the weakest. And the strongest method is one that people avoid. Why? Because it feels worse. Retrieval feels like struggle. Rereading feels like progress.

Think about your students. Many of them are in your program because school hasn't worked for them. A lot of them have a story in their head that says, I'm bad at remembering stuff, or I'm just not smart. Now picture what happens when you give them a quick retrieval quiz and they can't remember. If you don't frame it well, they hear, see, I'm dumb.

But here's what you can tell them, and it's true. That struggle is the learning. The blank feeling when you reach for an answer is your brain building the road. It's supposed to feel hard. If it feels easy, you're probably not learning much.

That's a powerful message for a struggling teen. It turns the feeling of difficulty from proof of failure into proof of effort.

You could even show them. Do a quick experiment in class. Give them a short paragraph about something from Civics. Half the room reads it three times. The other half reads it once, flips it over, and writes what they remember, twice. Ask everyone to rate how confident they feel. Then, two days later, without warning, ask everyone to write what they remember. You probably won't get clean results with fifteen kids. But you'll get a great conversation. And the kids who did the harder thing may notice that the harder thing worked.

The illusion catches teachers too. When you reread your own lesson plan five minutes before class, it all feels clear. Then a student asks a question from the side, and you realise you only knew it in the order it was written.

And it catches us in faith. You can hear a sermon on Sunday that moves you deeply. It feels like it went right in. But if on Wednesday someone asked you, what was the main point, could you say it? Hearing it clearly is fluency. Being able to say it back is learning.

So here's the fourth idea in one line. Easy feels like learning, but effort is what actually builds it.

Part five. How this lands in your week.

Let's make this concrete. Three places. Your classroom, your home, and your faith.

First, your classroom.

Your students live right on the steep part of the forgetting curve. Many have gaps in attendance. Many have a lot going on at home. Many are carrying stress that takes up mental space. That means what you teach on Tuesday is very fragile by Thursday.

So your job is not just to teach it well once. It's to bring it back. Pull it back out of them, a little later, and then a little later again.

The good news is this doesn't need to be a big quiz. It doesn't need marks. In fact, it works best when it's low stakes. A quick question on the board as they walk in. A turn and tell your partner. A thumbs up if you can name the three levels of government, then prove it.

You also have a built in advantage. You do morning desk sheets. That's a natural home for retrieval. Most teachers would have to invent a routine. You already have one.

Think about Careers, too. Some of your students will be sitting in a job interview in the next year or two. A manager will ask, tell me about a time you solved a problem. That is a retrieval question. Pure and simple. The kid who has practised saying his answer out loud, a few times, spread over a few weeks, will find the words. The kid who read a handout about interview tips once will freeze. You can tell them that directly. Reading about an interview is not practice. Saying your answer without looking is practice.

And the same goes for safety in the shop. You don't want a student to recognise the right answer on a multiple choice sheet. You want him to recall it with a saw in his hand. That's the difference between familiar and learned, and in your shop it can be the difference between a good day and a trip to the hospital. So the safety rules are the first thing worth bringing back, again and again, spaced out, from memory.

Second, your home.

Think about the things that matter at home that keep slipping. Decisions you and your wife made about money. Things you said you'd do around the house. What the kids need, or what your wife told you about her week that you meant to follow up on.

Here's a small shift. Instead of writing it down and never looking at it again, try saying it back. At supper, or in the car, ask each other. What did we decide about the budget for Christmas this year? What was that thing you said was stressing you out on Monday? Remembering out loud is retrieval. And for a marriage, it does something else too. It tells your wife you were listening.

Money is a good place for this. Most of us could not say, right now, without looking, roughly what our family spends on groceries in a month. Or how much is in the emergency fund. Or what the interest rate is on the mortgage. Those are numbers that shape real decisions. And because we only ever look them up, and never pull them from memory, they never stick. Try guessing first, then checking. You'll be surprised how fast those numbers start to live in your head. And when they live in your head, they start shaping your choices in the store, not just on the spreadsheet.

Third, your faith.

You get up at 5:30 for devotions. That's real discipline. But be honest with yourself for a second. By lunch, could you say what you read this morning?

Here's a simple use of everything we've talked about. After you read, close the Bible and try to say the main point in one sentence. Then later, maybe on your run, or in the car on the way to school, try to recall it again. Then on Saturday, try to recall the whole week. What did God show me Monday? Tuesday? That's retrieval. And it's spaced.

The Bible is full of this, by the way. Again and again, God's people are told to remember. Set up stones. Tell your children. Keep these words on your heart. Do this in remembrance of me. Those are retrieval practices. God built us, and he knows we forget.

Part six. One thing to try.

You get one thing. Only one. Here it is.

Put a three question warm up at the top of every morning desk sheet. Call it something simple, like Remember.

The first question comes from yesterday. The second question comes from last week. The third question comes from a month ago.

No notes. No looking back. Students just try. Then you go over the answers together, quickly, in a couple of minutes. No marks. The point is the reaching, not the score.

Keep the questions small. One fact or one idea each. Which level of government runs schools. Name one part of a circular saw and what it does. What's one sign you're in your target heart rate zone. If a question needs a paragraph, it's too big for the warm up.

And keep a simple running list for yourself of what you taught each day. That list becomes your question bank. When you build tomorrow's sheet, you glance back one day, one week, and about four weeks, and pull one question from each. Five minutes of planning. Maybe less.

That's it. Yesterday, last week, last month. It hits retrieval, because they have to pull it out. And it hits spacing, because the questions come back at growing gaps.

When a student blanks, say something like, good, that's your brain working. Now you'll remember it better.

And watch what happens over a month. The Monday morning silence starts to get smaller. The kid who made the pothole joke starts to say, the province. The province runs the schools.

If you want to try it on yourself too, use the same idea on your run. Yesterday's devotion, last week's sermon, last month's chess opening. But start with the desk sheet. That's the one thing.

Closing recap.

Here it is in three sentences.

We forget most of what we learn, and we forget it fastest right after we learn it. Pulling it back out of memory, with gaps in between, is what fights that curve far better than rereading. It will feel harder, and that feeling is the sign that it's working.

I'll leave you with one short line from Alistair Begg, Truth For Life.

"Many of our problems arise when we forget to remember."

Enjoy the rest of your run.
