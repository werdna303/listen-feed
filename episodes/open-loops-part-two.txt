Part one. A joke that turned out to be true.

The phrase you've heard is Parkinson's law. Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion.

It comes from a man named Cyril Northcote Parkinson. He was a British naval historian. Not a psychologist. Not a productivity guru. A historian who spent years looking at how navies and governments actually ran. And in November of nineteen fifty five, he wrote an essay for The Economist magazine. It was funny. It was meant to be funny. It was satire.

He opened with an example you'll like. He described an elderly lady with nothing else to do, who could spend an entire day sending one postcard to her niece. An hour finding the postcard. Another hour hunting for her glasses. Half an hour looking for the address. A long time writing it. A long debate about whether to take an umbrella to the mailbox. And so on, until the whole day was gone. Meanwhile, Parkinson said, a busy person could do the same job in about three minutes.

His point was that the job doesn't have a fixed size. The job swells. It swells to fill whatever space you give it.

But the real target of the essay wasn't old ladies. It was government offices. Parkinson was poking at the British civil service. He noticed that the number of officials in some departments kept going up, even when the actual work went down. The navy had fewer ships and fewer sailors over the years, but the office staff running the navy kept growing.

And he offered two tongue in cheek reasons. First, an official wants to add helpers under him, not rivals beside him. Second, officials make work for each other. One person writes a memo, two people have to read it, a third has to summarize it, and now four people are busy with something that didn't need doing.

So the original law was really about bureaucracy. About how organizations grow. But the line that stuck, the one people quote, is the first one. Work expands to fill the time available. And that's the one that describes your Saturday.

Parkinson later turned the essay into a book, and the idea spread everywhere. Office walls, management courses, productivity blogs. Which is a little funny, because it started as a joke. But it's the kind of joke that gets a laugh because everybody recognizes it.

Think about your own staff room. A meeting gets booked for an hour. Has a one hour meeting ever ended in twenty minutes because the business was done? Almost never. The meeting fills the hour. Someone always has one more thing. The time was there, so the talk found a way to use it.

Or think about Marketplace. You've got a chair in the garage you want gone. If you tell yourself you'll list it sometime this month, it'll sit there all month. Every time you walk past it, there's that little tap from last episode. Remember me. But if your wife says her sister is coming Saturday and needs the garage, the chair is listed by Thursday night, photos and all. Nothing about the chair changed. The time around it changed.

So, one line for part one. Parkinson's law started as satire, but it stuck because everybody has lived it. The job grows to fit the time.

Part two. What the research actually found.

Now, a joke in a magazine isn't evidence. So the fair question is, has anybody tested it? And the answer is yes, a little. Not a mountain of studies. But enough to say something useful.

The best known test came in nineteen sixty seven. Two researchers, Judith Bryan and Edwin Locke, published a paper with a great title. Parkinson's law as a goal setting phenomenon. And notice that title. They weren't just asking whether work expands. They were asking why.

Here's the gist of what they found. When people were given more time than a task needed, they worked at a slower pace. When they were given a tight amount of time, they worked faster. That's Parkinson's law, in a lab. But the interesting part is the reason. The study pointed to the goals people quietly set for themselves. When you have lots of time, you set yourself an easy target without even noticing. When time is short, your internal target gets harder. And your pace follows your target.

In other words, the time limit isn't magic. It works by changing your goal. A generous deadline whispers, take it easy. A tight one says, let's go.

That idea grew into one of the most solid findings in work psychology. Edwin Locke later teamed up with another researcher, Gary Latham, and over decades they built what's called goal setting theory. The core of it is simple. Specific, challenging goals tend to produce better performance than vague goals like do your best. Do your best sounds nice. But in study after study, it tends to produce less than a clear, hard target. Because do your best has no edge on it. You can always tell yourself you did your best.

You know this from running. Go out with the plan to run for a while and see how you feel, and you'll often come home early, or plod. Go out with a plan to run a set loop in a set time, and your legs find a different gear. Same legs. Different goal.

Chess shows it even more clearly. Think about the difference between a long game and a blitz game. In a long game, with an hour or more on your clock, you can spend fifteen minutes on a move you'd have played instantly in blitz. You check it, check it again, wander off into lines that don't matter, come back, and play the same move you saw in the first ten seconds. In blitz, with a few minutes for the whole game, you see the move and play it. Is the long game better chess? Usually, yes, overall. But a lot of those fifteen minutes weren't better thinking. They were just thinking that expanded because the clock allowed it. Strong players learn to spend their time where the position is actually hard, and move quickly where it isn't. That's Parkinson's law, managed.

Now I need to be the honest friend for a minute. There's a famous deadline study you might run into. It's from two thousand two, by Dan Ariely and Klaus Wertenbroch, about students who were given deadlines spaced out over a term versus one deadline at the end. It got cited everywhere. But when other researchers tried to repeat it, it didn't hold up, and people who looked closely at the original found serious problems. So I'm not going to lean on it. I mention it only because you'll see it in books, and I'd rather you know it's shaky.

The goal setting work is different. That's a big body of research, many studies, many settings. So the safe version is this. Deadlines don't help because they're scary. They help because they set a goal. And a clear, challenging goal tends to pull better work out of people.

One line for part two. Time limits work by setting your goal, and a clear, hard goal beats do your best.

Part three. The opposite problem.

Now, if you stopped there, you might think, great, I'll just give myself less time for everything. Squeeze it all. And that would be a mistake. Because there's a second pattern that pulls in exactly the other direction. And you need both in your head at once.

It's called the planning fallacy. The term goes back to Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in the late nineteen seventies. And the classic study on it is from nineteen ninety four, by Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross.

They asked university students who were writing their senior thesis a simple question. How long will it take you to finish? The students gave their best guess. On average, they said about a month. Then the researchers waited and watched. On average, the students took closer to two months. And only about three in ten finished within the time they'd predicted.

Here's what makes it sting. The researchers also asked the students for a worst case. If everything goes wrong, how long? And a lot of students blew past even that.

Why does this happen? The study found something telling. When people predicted their own finish time, they mostly thought about the future. The steps ahead. How the plan would go. They hardly thought about the past, about how long similar jobs had actually taken them. And when they did think about past delays, they explained them away. That was a special case. This time will be different.

Interestingly, people were much more realistic when predicting other people's finish times. You can look at a buddy's project and say, oh, that's going to take him twice as long as he thinks. But your own plan, you picture going smoothly.

Now you'll recognize this. You've said at some point, I'll have that truck job done by lunch. And then a bolt seizes. The part is wrong. The video didn't show the hidden clip. And lunch becomes dinner. That's not you being lazy. That's the planning fallacy. You pictured the plan, not the history.

So now you've got two forces. Parkinson's law says, give yourself too much time and the job will swell to fill it. The planning fallacy says, you'll tend to give yourself too little time, because you picture the clean version. Too loose, and you drift. Too tight, and you fail, feel bad, and stop trusting your own deadlines.

That sounds like a contradiction. But it isn't, really. Both are about the same mistake. You're guessing the size of a job from the inside, from your hopes, instead of from the outside, from the record.

And there's a way through it. It's what researchers sometimes call taking the outside view. Instead of asking, how long should this take, you ask, how long did this kind of job actually take me last time? Or, how long does it take people in general? Then you set your deadline from that. Not the dream number. Not the lazy number. The real number.

How do you get the real number? You need a record. And this is where your love of systems can actually earn its keep. Keep a simple log. A note on your phone, or a little notebook in the glovebox. Job, guess, actual. Brake pads, guessed two hours, took three and a half. Marking one class set, guessed one hour, took ninety minutes. After a month, you'll have something better than a feeling. You'll have your own data. And your own data is the best cure for the planning fallacy there is, because it's very hard to argue with your own handwriting.

There's one more trick from the planning research world that's worth knowing. When you can't find a record, add a buffer. Take your honest guess, and add a chunk on top, maybe half again, for the seized bolt you can't see yet. Not because you're slow. Because jobs have surprises, and your plan doesn't.

So the goal isn't a short deadline. It's an honest deadline. A deadline that's tight compared to all the time you have, but realistic compared to what the job really needs.

One line for part three. Too much time makes work swell, too little time makes plans fail, so set your deadline from the record, not from hope.

Part four. The finish line moves too.

Here's something people miss about Parkinson's law. It's not only that the work takes longer. It's that the work gets bigger. The job itself grows.

Think back to that open Saturday. Did the brake job just go slower? Or did it turn into more? You swept the garage. You reorganized the sockets. You thought, while the wheel's off, I should check the rotors, and the boots, and maybe the shocks. The job didn't just stretch. It spread.

That's the second kind of expansion. When there's time, the idea of done keeps moving. The finish line slides away from you.

You see this in your marking too. You give yourself a whole evening for one class set. And the comments get longer. You find yourself rewriting a rubric halfway through. You start thinking about a better assignment for next year. The marking was supposed to be marking. It turned into a curriculum project, and it's eleven at night.

And with a person who loves systems, it shows up in a sneaky way. Give yourself an open afternoon to set up a better to do list, and the to do list can become a whole new system. New tags, new colours, a new app to try. Last time we talked about that trap, reorganizing the toolbox instead of fixing the truck. Parkinson's law is part of why it happens. The time was open, so the job grew.

So here's the fix, and it's simple. Before you start a job, decide what done looks like. In one sentence. Out loud if you want.

Done means the pads are on, the wheels are torqued, and I've pumped the brakes. Not the rotors. Not the shocks. Those go on the list for another day.

Done means every student in this class gets a mark and one comment on what to fix next. Not a paragraph. One comment.

Done means the listing is up with three photos and a price. Not the perfect description.

When you name done before you start, you build a fence around the job. The job can't spread past the fence. And when you hit the fence, you stop. You get to feel finished, which, if you remember part one, is exactly what your brain is begging for. A loop that actually closes.

There's an old saying in software and in building that fits here. Good enough to ship. It doesn't mean sloppy. It means you decided beforehand what the standard was, you met it, and you stopped. A deck that's built to code and safe is done. You don't keep adding trim forever.

There's also a perfectionist version of this, and I suspect you'll recognize it. The last little bit of a job often takes as long as all the rest. The comment you rewrite three times. The listing photo you retake because the light was off. That last stretch can swallow huge amounts of time for very little gain. A fence around done protects you from that too. When you've met the standard you set, the extra polish is a choice, not a duty. Sometimes it's worth it. Usually, it isn't.

One line for part four. Work doesn't just stretch, it spreads, so decide what done looks like before you start.

How it lands in your actual week.

Let's walk through your week and see where Parkinson's law is quietly eating time.

Start at school. Your prep period. If your prep period has no plan, it'll fill with whatever walks in. Email, a chat in the hallway, tidying your desk, checking the news. By the end of it you were busy the whole time, but nothing on your list moved. So try this. Before prep starts, name one job, give it a time, and name what done looks like. Twenty five minutes, the field trip form is filled out and in the office. The rest of prep can be loose. But one job has a fence.

Now your students. A lot of your Shifting Gears kids live inside Parkinson's law, and the planning fallacy at the same time. You give them two weeks for a project. For thirteen days, nothing happens, because two weeks feels like forever. Then on the last night, they discover it's actually a big job, and they panic or give up. Sound familiar?

You can't fix their brains. But you can shape the time. Instead of one deadline two weeks out, give them small finish lines along the way. By Wednesday, you've picked your topic. By Friday, you've got three facts written down. Each one is a little fence. Each one is close enough to feel real. That's not a fancy study, that's just good teaching, and it lines up with what goal setting research says about clear, near targets.

In your construction class, you already do this without thinking. You don't say, build a shed by the end of term. You say, today we're cutting the floor joists, and here's what done looks like. Joists cut, labelled, stacked. You've been beating Parkinson's law in the shop for years. The trick is doing it for yourself at home.

Next, your phys ed class. Ever notice that a warm up given ten minutes takes ten minutes, and a warm up given five takes five? The kids don't do half as much in the short one. They just stand around less. Same law. Use it. Tight time, clear task, and the standing around shrinks.

Now home. The truck, the house jobs, the Marketplace stuff. Here's where Saturdays disappear. The fix is the opposite of what you might expect. Don't give the truck job the whole day. Give it a block. Nine to noon, the brake pads. Then put something real after it. Lunch with your wife. A run. A visit. Something you actually want to show up for. That thing after the block becomes your church work bee. It makes the deadline real.

And use the outside view. How long did the last brake job actually take you? Not how long it should take. How long it did take. If it took three hours last time, don't schedule two. Schedule three. An honest deadline is one you can actually hit, and hitting it is what teaches your brain to trust your deadlines.

Finally, church. You're involved in leadership. You know about meetings. An elders meeting with no end time will expand to fill the evening. One with an agenda and a firm finish tends to get the important things decided and lets everyone get home to their families. If you're ever the one setting up the meeting, you can do everybody a kindness. Put the end time on the agenda, and keep it.

Here's a quiet word for the part of you that gets up at five thirty for devotions. There's a danger with all this deadline talk. You could start to treat every minute like a race. That's not the point. Parkinson's law isn't saying slow time is bad. A slow Saturday with nothing planned can be a gift. A long talk with your wife that goes past the time you expected is not a failure. The problem is only when the slow time is accidental. When you meant to do one thing, and the time leaked away without you choosing it. The goal is to choose. Fence the work, so the rest can be truly free.

One thing to try.

Here's your one thing. Only one. Box one job a day.

Each day this week, pick one job. Just one. It can be at school or at home. The marking for one class. A Marketplace listing. Cleaning out the truck. An email you've been dodging.

Before you start, do three quick things.

First, decide how long it gets. Use the outside view. Not your hopeful number. The number you've actually seen.

Second, say what done looks like. One sentence. Pads on and torqued. Every kid has a mark and one comment.

Third, set a timer on your phone, and put something real after it. A run, dinner, picking up your kid. Something that won't move.

Then work until the timer or until done, whichever comes first. If you hit done early, stop. Don't let the job spread. Enjoy the closed loop. If the timer goes and you're not done, don't just keep going. Stop and ask one question. Was my estimate wrong, or did the job spread? If the estimate was wrong, write down the real time so next time you know. If the job spread, you just learned where your fence was weak.

That's it. One boxed job a day, for one week.

And here's how you'll know it's working. Not by how fast you go. By two things. First, do those boxed jobs actually close, instead of hanging around as open loops? And second, at the end of the week, are your guesses getting more honest? If your estimates are getting closer to reality, you're beating both Parkinson's law and the planning fallacy at once.
