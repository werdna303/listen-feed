Picture first period. It is a grey Tuesday in November. You have been up since five thirty. You have read your Bible, prayed, had your coffee, and you are ready. The room is not.

One kid has his hood up and his head on the desk. Another is staring at the wall like the wall owes her money. A third walked in eleven minutes late with an energy drink and no explanation. You say good morning. Two people grunt.

And somewhere in the back of your mind a voice says, these kids just don't care.

Hold that thought. Because by the end of this run, I want you to hear a different voice. Here is the promise for the next half hour. The teenager slumped in your first period is not mostly lazy or defiant. He is living in a different time zone than you, and his body put him there.

We will go in four parts. Part one is the body clock itself, and why it slides later in the teen years. Part two is the gap between what teens need and what they get. Part three is what that gap does inside your classroom. Part four is what actually helps, and where the science is still argued. Let's go.

Part one. The clock moves later.

Here is the first big idea. Somewhere around puberty, the body clock shifts later. Not a little. Enough to matter.

The person who did more than anyone to show this is a sleep researcher named Mary Carskadon. She has spent decades at Brown University studying teenagers and sleep. And she found two things going on at once. Think of them as two dials.

The first dial is the clock. Your brain runs on a roughly twenty four hour rhythm. One of the signals of that rhythm is a hormone called melatonin. Melatonin rises in the evening and tells the body, it is getting toward night, start winding down. Carskadon's lab found that when you put teens and younger kids on the same light and dark schedule, the teens start making melatonin later in the evening. Same room. Same lights. Later signal.

So when a fifteen year old says, I'm not tired at ten o'clock, he is often telling you the truth. His body has not sent the night signal yet.

The second dial is pressure. From the moment you wake up, a kind of sleepiness builds in you all day long. Researchers call it sleep pressure. The longer you are awake, the heavier it gets, until you fall asleep and it drains away. Carskadon's lab found that in older teens, this pressure builds more slowly. So they can stay awake longer in the evening without feeling it. But here is the catch. Once they are asleep, they still need just as much sleep to drain it. The need does not shrink. Only the bedtime moves.

Put those two dials together and you get a kid who is wide awake at eleven at night and a zombie at eight in the morning. Not because of bad character. Because of biology.

Now, to be honest about the science, researchers still argue about how much each dial matters. Some work says the melatonin shift is the bigger piece. Other work, including a study that followed the same kids for a couple of years, suggests the slower build of sleep pressure may be doing more of the work. What nobody seriously argues about is the result. Teens go to bed later as they get older, and it is not just a choice.

Here is a piece that should make you smile. This later shift does not last forever. A German researcher named Till Roenneberg surveyed about twenty five thousand people about when they slept. He found that sleep timing gets later and later through the teen years, peaks right around age twenty, and then starts sliding back earlier. He actually proposed it as a biological marker for the end of adolescence. The day your sleep starts moving earlier again, you have, in a sense, grown up.

So think about you and your students. You are past that turn. You are well on the early side of it. You wake at five thirty for devotions and you like it. Your students are near the very latest point of their whole lives. When you stand at the front of the room at first period, you are at your sharpest. They are at something like their middle of the night.

That is not an excuse. It is a fact about the room.

So here is part one in one line. Around puberty the body clock slides later, and it does not slide back until around twenty.

Part two. The gap between need and reality.

Here is the second big idea. Teenagers need more sleep than most of them get, and the gap is not small.

Let's start with the need. In twenty sixteen, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine pulled together a panel of experts. They read through hundreds of studies and voted on what healthy sleep looks like for each age. For teens, thirteen to eighteen, they landed on eight to ten hours a night. The American Academy of Pediatrics endorsed that. So did other sleep groups. Eight to ten. Remember that range.

Now the reality. The numbers vary by study and by year, but the pattern is steady. A large share of teens get well under eight hours on school nights. There is an older survey from Ontario high schools, done around the turn of the century, that found about seven in ten students were getting less than eight and a half hours on weeknights. That is old data, and I would not lean on the exact number. But more recent Canadian reporting says the same thing in rougher terms. More than half of teens are below the recommended range. Many are sitting around seven hours, some less.

So the gap is often one to two hours a night. Every school night.

Why does that gap open up? Go back to part one. If the body clock says sleep at eleven, and the alarm says get up at six forty five, the math does not work. You cannot fit nine hours between eleven and six forty five.

Carskadon ran a study on exactly this. She followed a small group of students in Rhode Island across a school change. In ninth grade their school started at eight twenty five. In tenth grade they moved to a school that started at seven twenty. That is about an hour earlier.

Here is what the study found. Their bedtimes basically did not move. The kids still went to sleep around the same time, a little before eleven. But their wake times got earlier, because they had to catch the bus. So they simply lost sleep. And when the researchers measured how sleepy they were in the morning, the kids were sleepier.

You might be thinking, fine, then just go to bed earlier. It is the advice every parent gives. And it is not wrong, exactly. A calmer, darker evening does help. But think about what happens when a teen goes to bed at nine thirty because someone told them to. The night signal has not arrived. The sleep pressure is not heavy yet. So they lie there. They stare at the ceiling. They get frustrated. Then they reach for the phone, because lying awake in the dark for an hour is miserable. Telling a teen to sleep earlier is a bit like telling you to fall asleep at seven at night. You could lie down. You probably would not sleep.

That is the key insight of part two. You can move a teen's alarm clock. It is very hard to move a teen's bedtime. When school starts earlier, the sleep comes off the morning end.

And then the weekend happens. Most teens try to pay back the debt on Saturday and Sunday. They sleep till eleven or noon. Researchers sometimes call this social jet lag. During the week they live on school time. On the weekend they drift back to body time. Then Monday comes and they have to fly back across the time zones again. If you have ever wondered why Monday first period is the worst of the week, there it is.

Now picture your Shifting Gears kids. Many of them have home lives that make all of this harder. A crowded house. A shared bedroom. Younger siblings to get to school. A parent working nights. A part time job that runs late. Noise, stress, maybe not feeling safe. Those things do not cause the body clock shift. But they stack on top of it. A kid who is already biologically late, in a house where it is loud until midnight, is not going to bank nine hours.

So here is part two in one line. Teens need eight to ten hours, many get closer to seven, and the loss comes off the morning.

Halfway. Let's take stock.

You are about halfway through your run. Let's check what we have so far. Two ideas.

First, around puberty the body clock slides later. The night signal comes later, and sleep pressure builds more slowly, so teens are truly not sleepy at ten. That lasts until around age twenty.

Second, the need does not shrink. Teens still need eight to ten hours. But with early starts, the sleep gets cut from the morning end, so many are short by an hour or two every school night, and the weekend sleep in makes Monday worse.

Two ideas. A later clock, and a real gap. Now let's walk into your classroom and see what that gap does.

Part three. What a short night does in your room.

Here is the third big idea. Sleep loss in teens shows up first as mood and self control, not just yawning.

When we think of a tired student, we picture the head on the desk. That is real. Sleep loss does hurt attention. A tired brain drifts. It misses instructions. It reads the same line three times. It forgets the safety step you just explained at the mitre saw. That matters in a shop class.

But the part I really want you to hear is the emotional part. Because that is the part that looks like behaviour.

There is a study by a team led by a researcher named Baum, published in twenty fourteen. It is one of the better ones, because it is an actual experiment, not just a survey. They took about fifty healthy teens, fourteen to seventeen. For one school week, the teens were allowed six and a half hours in bed each night. That is a pretty normal teen week. For another week, they were allowed ten hours. Then the researchers compared them.

After the short sleep week, the teens reported more anxiety. More anger. Less energy. And both the teens and their parents said they were worse at handling their emotions. More irritable. Quicker to blow up. Slower to calm down.

Notice that. The parents saw it too. It was not just the kids saying they felt off. The people living with them noticed.

And these were healthy kids from stable homes, in a careful study. Now think about the students in your program. Kids who already carry a heavy load. Kids who may already have a short fuse because of what happened last night at home. Take away an hour or two of sleep, every night, and you are taking away the very thing that helps them hold it together.

Now, honesty again. The science here is good but not perfect. That study leaned on questionnaires. A later lab study found that sleepy teens could still use a basic calming strategy, like reframing a bad situation, when someone prompted them to. And a big review of many studies found the effect of short sleep on good emotional coping was real but modest. So I would not say sleep loss turns a calm kid into a volcano. I would say it turns the dial. It makes the hard moments a bit harder, and the recovery a bit slower.

Here is what that looks like on a real morning. A student comes in. You ask him to take his hood down. He snaps at you. Old instinct says, defiance, deal with it. And maybe it is. But part of what you are seeing might be a brain running on six hours, at what feels to him like four in the morning. Same words, same snap. Different cause. And the cause changes what helps. A power struggle at eight forty five with a kid in that state usually goes badly for everyone. A calm voice, a little space, and a task that gets him moving usually goes better.

This connects to something you already know as a runner and a chess player. When you are underslept, your long run feels heavier. Your pace drifts. And at the chessboard, you hang a piece you would never hang when rested. You did not suddenly get worse at chess. Your brain just had less in the tank to stop the mistake. Your students are making those same blunders, all morning long, in front of their peers. Except their blunders are words, and attitude, and walking out.

So here is part three in one line. A short night shows up as a short fuse, and that can look a lot like bad behaviour.

Part four. What helps, and what is still argued.

Here is the fourth big idea. The biggest fix is at the system level, the start time. The smaller fixes, like screens, matter less than people think, but they still matter some.

Let's start with start times. This is where the strongest evidence sits.

In the nineteen nineties, two school districts in Minnesota moved their high school start times later. Edina went first. Then Minneapolis moved its high schools from a little after seven to around eight forty. A researcher at the University of Minnesota named Kyla Wahlstrom studied what happened. Students got more sleep, roughly an extra hour on school nights. Attendance went up in several grades. And over the following years, the graduation rate improved. Later work by her team, looking at thousands of students in several states, also linked later starts to more sleep and even to fewer car crashes involving teen drivers.

Then there is a newer study from Seattle, published in twenty eighteen, by Gideon Dunster and colleagues. Seattle moved its high schools from about seven fifty to about eight forty five. The researchers had students wear wrist monitors that track sleep. That is important, because it does not rely on teens remembering how much they slept. The study found that after the change, students slept about thirty four minutes more per night. Their median school night sleep went from under seven hours to nearly seven and a half. In one biology course, final grades were about four and a half percent higher. And at the lower income school in the study, lateness and absences dropped noticeably.

Notice that detail. The school with more struggling families saw the bigger attendance gain. That sounds a lot like your kids.

Now, honest caveats. Neither of those is a perfect experiment. The Seattle study compared two different groups of students, one year apart. The grade boost was in one course. And across the wider research, the effect on grades and test scores is mixed. Some studies find small gains. One Minneapolis follow up found no change on college entrance test scores. Wahlstrom herself has said the link to academic performance is not fully settled. What is very consistent is this. Later start, more sleep. That part holds up again and again.

Because of this research, the American Academy of Pediatrics put out a policy statement in twenty fourteen. It said middle and high schools should aim to start no earlier than eight thirty in the morning. Many schools still start earlier than that.

You do not control your school's bell schedule. I know that. But it helps to know that if you ever sit on a committee, or talk with an administrator, or a parent asks, the research is on your side.

Now screens. This is where you have to be careful, because the popular message is louder than the evidence.

Here is what is solid. Light in the evening pushes the body clock later. Light tells the brain it is still day. A study by Stephanie Crowley and Carskadon tested this. They found that younger teens, those earlier in puberty, were quite sensitive to even ordinary room light in the evening. It held back their melatonin. Older teens were less sensitive. The researchers said even small amounts of evening light, like from screens, could matter, especially for younger kids.

But here is the other side. A pair of Oxford researchers, Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski, looked at time use diaries from thousands of teens in the United Kingdom. They found that each extra hour of screen time was linked to only about nine fewer minutes of sleep on weeknights. And screen use in the half hour before bed was linked to about one minute less sleep. One minute. Their point was that screens get blamed for far more than the data shows, and that things like early school start times probably matter more.

So which is it? Probably both, in different ways. The light itself is a real push on the clock. But for most teens, the average effect of screens on total sleep looks smaller than the headlines. Where screens likely do the most harm is not the glow. It is the scrolling that keeps going past midnight, the group chat at one in the morning, the game that is designed never to end. That is less about light and more about a phone that never says, that's enough.

So here is part four in one line. Later start times have the strongest evidence, and screens matter, but less than the headlines say.

How this lands in your week.

Let's bring all of this home. Three places. Your classroom, your own sleep, and your home.

First, your classroom. You cannot move the bell. But you can design first period for the brains that actually show up.

In your phys ed course, you have a huge advantage. Movement wakes the body up. If your phys-ed class ever lands first thing, that is good news. Get them up and moving early, before you ask for much talking or thinking.

In construction, think about what you put first. A careful demo with a lot of verbal instruction, at the time their brains are foggiest, is a hard ask. And sleepy brains miss safety steps. If you can, put the long explanations a little later in the period, and start with something hands on and familiar. Sweeping, sorting, setting up stations, finishing yesterday's cut. Then teach the new thing once they are actually awake.

In Civics and Careers, this whole topic is a gift. Think about a lesson where you teach your students what you just heard. The two dials. The clock shift. The age twenty turnaround. Many of these kids have been told for years that they are lazy. Hearing that their body clock is real, and is not a moral failure, can be a relief. And it is also a lesson in agency. Once you know how your body works, you can make choices that work with it. That is a careers lesson too. Some jobs start at six. Some start at noon. Knowing your own rhythm is useful when you choose work.

And then there is the way you read behaviour. Before you treat a first period snap as defiance, ask yourself one quiet question. Is this kid tired? It will not always be the answer. But when it is, the response is different. Less confrontation. More structure. A task, not a lecture.

Second, your own sleep. Let's be honest with you for a minute.

You wake at five thirty. You run. You teach a demanding group of kids all day. You are married, and you have a life in the evening. The research says most adults need at least seven hours. If you are up at five thirty, that means asleep by about ten thirty, and in bed earlier than that. Is that happening most nights?

Here is why it matters for your students. The Baum study showed that short sleep shrinks emotional patience. That is not only true for fifteen year olds. It is true for teachers too. A rested teacher has more room to absorb a kid's snap without snapping back. Your sleep is part of your classroom management. It might be the least glamorous part, but it is real.

And there is a faith angle here too. Your early mornings with God are a good discipline. But the same God who meets you at five thirty also made you to sleep. Guarding the night is part of guarding the morning. If devotions are costing you sleep every single night, it might be worth an honest look at the other end of the day, not the morning.

Third, home. If you have kids now, or nieces and nephews, or kids at church, or someday teenagers of your own, this changes how you see them. A sixteen year old who cannot fall asleep at ten is not necessarily rebelling. The kind move is to help them protect the sleep they can get. A calm, dim evening. Phones charged outside the bedroom. A consistent wake time, even on weekends, as much as is realistic. And grace for the grumpiness at breakfast.

So that is your week. Design first period for tired brains. Guard your own sleep, because your patience depends on it. And bring grace home with you.

One thing to try.

Just one thing. Here it is.

For the next two weeks, start your first period with ten minutes of movement and light. Before any instructions, before any worksheet, get your students up. If you can get outside, go outside. If not, open the blinds, turn on every light, and get them moving. A short walk. A warm up in the gym. Setting up the shop. Anything with bodies in motion and bright light in their eyes.

Why this one? Because morning light is one of the strongest signals that tells the body clock it is daytime. It helps pull the clock back toward an earlier schedule, and it wakes the brain up in the moment. Movement does the same. And it costs you almost nothing.

Then pay attention. Do you get fewer snaps in the first half hour? Do the heads come up faster? Do the late arrivals at least walk in to something that is already happening, instead of a silent room? You are a coach. Run the experiment and watch the results.

Ten minutes. Light and movement. Two weeks.
