Listen. Can I actually sit down and teach my kids? The 12 minute lesson.
For your run. About 30 minutes.

Part one. The question.

Picture a normal morning in your room. It is about nine thirty. The kids came in at nine fifteen with breakfast. The morning sheet is on the desks. One kid is done the feelings grid and has drifted to the window. One kid has his hood up. One kid is asking you when break is, even though break is at ten twenty five, every day, no earlier and no later. And you are standing there with a question you typed into your phone half as a joke and half as a confession.

"Is there a chance I can actually sit down and do a lesson with the kids and teach them things?"

That question sits on top of something bigger you have said before. You named it as "the lack of teaching that I'm actually teaching my kids." You are a teacher. You have a Bachelor of Education. You have taught French Immersion history to a full room. And in this room, most days, you hand out clear sheets, you manage moods, you track breaks and rewards, and you rarely stand up and teach a thing.

Here is the promise of this episode, in one sentence. Yes, you can teach real lessons in this room, if you make them short, small, predictable and almost impossible to fail.

We are going to take four ideas. First, why short explicit teaching is not the enemy of a care and treatment room but actually fits it. Second, the shape of a good short lesson: I do, we do, you do. Third, how to keep kids who resist inside the lesson, using something called opportunities to respond. And fourth, how to get started with a kid who is already leaning away from you, using an idea called behavioural momentum. Then we will land it in your week, and I will give you one lesson to try. Only one.

Part two. Idea one. Teaching is not the enemy of this room.

Let's start with a worry you might not have said out loud. The worry goes like this. These kids have had school go badly for years. A lot of them associate a teacher at the front of the room with being bored, being lost, being called on, and being embarrassed. So if I stand up and teach, I am recreating the exact thing that pushed them out. Better to give them sheets they can do on their own and keep the peace.

There is something true in that worry. You told me once that "the best work I get from kids is handouts that are really clear and obvious and kids can do on their own time." That is real. Clear sheets work in your room. Keep them.

But here is what the research says, and it may surprise you. The kids who struggle most are exactly the kids who need the most explicit teaching, not the least.

There is a well known paper from 2006 by three researchers, Paul Kirschner, John Sweller and Richard Clark. The title more or less says it all. It argues that minimal guidance, where you hand learners a task and let them figure it out, does not work well for novices. When someone is new to a skill, their working memory gets swamped. They are trying to understand the task, find the steps, remember the rules and do the work all at once. Experts can handle that. Beginners cannot. Beginners learn better when someone shows them clearly, step by step, and then lets them practise.

Now think about what that means in your room. When you hand a kid a sheet on something new and say, have a go, you are giving the least guidance to the kid who has the least capacity to spare. Some of what you call work refusal may not be defiance at all. It may be a kid who looked at the page, felt the fog roll in, and decided not trying is safer than trying and failing in front of you.

You already sensed this. You wrote that a certain student's trigger is being asked to do something, and you wondered whether maybe he doesn't know how, or doesn't want to. Here is the thing. Those two are often the same. A kid who does not know how often looks exactly like a kid who does not want to. The not wanting is how the not knowing protects itself.

So explicit teaching, done well, is not the thing that hurts these kids. It is the thing that removes the fog. It turns "I can't" into "oh, that's it?"

The second piece of this idea comes from Barak Rosenshine, an American education researcher. In 2012 he wrote a short article called Principles of Instruction. He pulled together research on how the most effective teachers actually teach. A few of his principles matter a lot for you. Present new material in small steps. Ask lots of questions. Model the thing. Guide the practice. And aim for a high success rate. He put that number at about eighty percent. Eighty percent of answers right during practice. Not fifty. Not "we'll struggle through it." Eight out of ten right.

Why does that matter for your kids? Because success is the reward that keeps them in the chair. A kid who gets eight out of ten right feels smart. A kid who gets four out of ten right feels like school is happening to him again.

So here is idea one in a single line. Short, explicit teaching is not the enemy of a care and treatment room; for kids who struggle, clear teaching with a high success rate is the kindest thing you can do.

Part three. Idea two. The shape: I do, we do, you do.

Here is the second idea. A short lesson needs a shape, and the simplest good shape is I do, we do, you do.

This comes from an idea called the gradual release of responsibility. Two researchers, P. David Pearson and Margaret Gallagher, described it back in 1983, mostly about reading. The idea is that responsibility for the work moves from the teacher to the student in stages. At first you carry the load. Then you share it. Then they carry it. You have heard this in teachers college. I want to show you how small it can be.

I do. You show one or two examples. Out loud. You think out loud as you go. "Okay, I worked eight hours. I get paid seventeen dollars an hour. So I go eight times seventeen. Eight times ten is eighty. Eight times seven is fifty six. Eighty plus fifty six is one hundred thirty six. So I made one hundred thirty six dollars before anything comes off." That's it. That's the I do. It takes ninety seconds. The kids are not doing anything except watching you be a little bit slow and a little bit clear.

Notice the thinking out loud. That is the part most teachers skip. Rosenshine calls it modelling. Kids do not just need to see the answer. They need to hear the little voice that finds the answer. Your kids especially have often never heard that voice. Nobody narrated the steps for them.

We do. Now you do one or two more, but together. "Okay, your turn to help me. Six hours, fifteen dollars an hour. What do I multiply?" A kid says six times fifteen. "Good. What's six times ten?" Sixty. "Six times five?" Thirty. "So?" Ninety. "Ninety dollars." You hold the pen. They supply the pieces. If a kid is wrong, you fix it fast and kindly and move on. "Close, it's six times five, so thirty. Good."

This is the guided practice Rosenshine talks about. It is where most of the learning happens. And it's where you check for understanding. He makes a sharp point here. Less effective teachers ask, "Any questions?" and if nobody answers, they assume everyone gets it. Your kids will never answer "any questions." Asking for questions in front of peers is the last thing a kid in your room wants to do. So you do not ask if they understand. You ask them to do a small piece and you watch.

You do. Now they do two or three on their own. Small. On a half sheet or a mini whiteboard. Same kind of problem, nothing new. You walk around and look. Rosenshine calls this monitored independent practice. The important word is monitored. You are not sitting at your desk. You are moving.

And then you stop. You do not add a fourth part. You do not add the deductions and the CPP and the budget on the same day. You stop while they are winning.

How long is all of that? The I do, maybe two minutes. The we do, maybe four or five. The you do, maybe four or five. That is around twelve minutes. That is a lesson. A real one.

So we have two ideas now. One, clear teaching is kind for kids who struggle. Two, the shape is I do, we do, you do, and it can fit in about twelve minutes.

Here is idea two in one line. A lesson does not need to be long to be real; show it, do it together, let them do it, and stop while they are winning.

Part four. Idea three. Keep them in it with lots of chances to respond.

Here is the third idea. The way you keep resistant kids inside a lesson is not by making it more fun. It is by giving them lots of quick, low risk chances to answer.

Researchers call these opportunities to respond. It just means any moment where you ask something and the student does something back. Answer out loud. Hold up a whiteboard. Point. Thumbs up or down. Write one number.

There's a 2015 review by Ashley MacSuga Gage and Brandi Simonsen that pulled together the research on this. Every study they looked at that increased the rate of these chances to respond across a whole class found good things. Less off task behaviour. Less disruption. More on task behaviour, more participation, more correct answers. And that held for students with and without disabilities.

They suggested a rough target. During direct instruction on basic facts or skills, about three to five chances to respond per minute. Let that sink in. Three to five a minute. That is one every twelve to twenty seconds. When kids are writing their answers, the rate can be lower, about one a minute. The authors are honest that nobody has nailed down the perfect rate. These are ballpark numbers. But the direction is clear. More chances to respond, less trouble.

Why does this work? Think about it from a kid's seat. When the teacher talks for five minutes straight, the kid has five minutes with nothing to do. A kid with a hard history fills empty time. With his phone. With a comment. With his hood. With the window. But when a question is coming every fifteen seconds, there is no empty time to fill. The pace itself is the behaviour plan.

There is another piece in that review that fits your room. Some studies used choral responding. That means everyone answers together, out loud, at the same time. One study found that choral responding, and a mix of choral and individual, came with less off task and disruptive behaviour than only calling on kids one at a time.

Now, you have four kids. Choral answering with four teenagers in a care and treatment room may feel a bit like a primary class. Fair. So adapt it. Mini whiteboards are your friend. "Everybody, write the answer, hold it up on three." Nobody is singled out. Nobody is the one kid who got it wrong out loud. You see four answers at once and you know exactly where everyone is. A kid who wrote the wrong number just sees you nod and say, "Most of us got ninety. Let's check it together." No spotlight.

And here is a connection to something you already wrote down. You said, "if a student fights with me and I reply with logic I am giving them a fight to win." Lots of quick small responses take the fight away. There is no big moment to resist. There is just the next tiny question, and the next one. It is hard to start a war over "what's six times ten."

Okay. Halfway recap. Let's say the three ideas again.

One. Explicit teaching is not the enemy of your room. Kids who struggle need clear teaching more, not less, and they need to be right about eight times out of ten.

Two. The shape is I do, we do, you do. Show it with your thinking out loud, do it together, let them try alone, and stop while they're winning. Around twelve minutes.

Three. Keep them in it with lots of quick chances to respond. Something every fifteen seconds or so. Whiteboards beat calling on one kid.

Now the fourth idea, which is about the hardest part. Getting started at all.

Part five. Idea four. Getting started with a kid who is already leaning away.

Here is the fourth idea. When a kid is unlikely to do the hard thing, start with a few easy things he will almost certainly do, and then slide the hard thing in right behind them.

This is called the high probability request sequence. It comes from an idea in behaviour research called behavioural momentum. The picture is a rolling ball. A ball that is already rolling is easier to keep rolling than a ball sitting still.

Here is how it works. You give two or three quick, easy requests that the student is very likely to follow. Low effort. Things he would do anyway. Between them, a quick bit of praise or just a "thanks." Then, right away, at the same brisk pace, you give the request he is less likely to follow. The research says this increases compliance for students with low compliance, across different ages and different kinds of disabilities, and some studies found it reduced disruptive behaviour too. It is not magic. But it has decent support and it costs you nothing.

What does it sound like in your room? Not, "Okay guys, put your phones away, we're doing a math lesson now." That is a cold start with the hardest request first. It practically invites the tug of war. You've used that picture yourself, the rope lying on the ground and the kid picking up one end.

Instead, it sounds like this. "Hey, grab a marker for me." He grabs a marker. "Thanks. Can you pass one to a classmate?" He does. "Perfect. Write your initials in the corner of your board." He does. "Nice. Okay, first one's on me, watch." And now you are into the I do, and he is holding a marker, with his initials on a board, already in the lesson.

Three easy yeses, then the lesson. The kid never had to decide to start a math lesson. He just kept rolling.

This connects to something else you already know about your kids, which is the function of behaviour. You have the cheat sheet. Sensory, escape, attention, tangibles. For a lot of work refusal in your room, the function is escape. The kid wants to get away from something that feels hard or embarrassing. The worst move is to make the start of the lesson feel like a big, high stakes event, because that is exactly what a kid wants to escape. The momentum approach shrinks the start into something too small to bother escaping.

There is one more piece here. Predictability. You wrote, with capital letters, that you want to be "so deadly predictable every day for my students even where I sit and when." That instinct is one of your best. A lesson that happens at the same time every day, in the same shape, with the same reward after it, stops being an event. It becomes the weather. Kids don't fight the weather. They just put on a jacket.

So here's idea four in one line. Don't start with the hard ask; roll the ball with two or three easy yeses, then slide the lesson in right behind them, at the same time every day.

Part six. How this lands in your actual week.

Let's put this into your room, with your kids and your day.

First, where does the lesson go? Your day has fixed points. Kids in at nine fifteen with breakfast and the morning sheet. Break at ten twenty five, no earlier, no later. Smoke break at twelve twenty five. Taxis at one forty five.

The obvious slot is right before the ten twenty five break. Say ten o'clock to ten fifteen. Why there? Because the break is a natural reward that is coming whether you teach or not, and a short lesson that ends right before it has a built in finish line. a colleague's tip was that success equals an attempt at work, then ten to fifteen minutes of free time after a block. The lesson becomes the block that comes before the good thing. Same idea. You are not inventing a new reward system. You are slotting the lesson in front of the one you already have.

Second, what do you teach? Not new content you have to build from scratch. Teach the skill that sits underneath the sheets you already give them. You have the My Pay Stub sheet for the Civics and Careers credit. You have Read the Tape for TCJ2O. Each of those sheets has a skill hiding inside it. Hours times rate. Reading a tape measure to the nearest quarter inch. Adding up a small budget. That skill is your twelve minute lesson. The sheet becomes the you do, on the next day, done independently. The lesson makes the sheet doable. The sheet proves the lesson stuck.

That fixes something you named about yourself, too. You said that clear, obvious handouts are where your best work from kids comes from. Good. Keep that. Now the short lesson is the on ramp to the handout. You are not choosing between teaching and sheets. Teaching makes the sheets work for more kids.

Third, how big? Small. Smaller than you think. You have said that one of your biggest weaknesses as a teacher is not following through on your own plans. That is honest, and it matters here. The way to follow through on a plan is to make the plan so small you can't really fail to do it. Not "a unit on personal finance." One twelve minute lesson, the same time each day, for one week. That's a plan you can keep.

Now, because you asked me to always give you both sides, here is what will likely work and what likely won't.

What will likely work. A fixed time slot right before break. Starting with two or three easy requests. Thinking out loud during the I do. Whiteboards so nobody is singled out. Fast, quiet corrections like "close, it's thirty, good." Stopping while they're winning, even if you have time left. Telling them up front how long it is. "Twelve minutes, then break." A visible timer helps.

What seems like it would work but probably won't. Asking "any questions?" and taking silence as understanding. Calling on one kid at a time to answer cold in front of the others. Opening with "okay everyone, phones away, we're doing a lesson now," which turns the start into a showdown. Making the first lesson fun with a game or a gimmick, which usually adds noise and rules and makes it harder to follow. Adding a second skill because the first one went well. And using the lesson as a consequence, as in "since you won't do your sheet, we're doing a lesson," which teaches them that lessons are punishment.

What to definitely say. "This is a short one, twelve minutes, then break." "Watch me do the first one." "Write it on your board, hold it up on three." "Most of us got ninety. Let's check it." "That's it, you just did it."

What to definitely not say. "This is easy." To a kid who finds it hard, that line says you're slow. "Come on, you know this." "Why aren't you writing?" in front of the others. And no long speech about why math matters for your future. They've heard it.

One more honest note. You told me you don't want me to oversell activities as sure things, and you're right to push on that. Some days a kid will not join. One kid's day may already be gone by ten o'clock. That's okay. The lesson is for whoever is in it. If one kid stays at the window, you teach the other three, and you keep the board and marker on his desk. Don't chase. Don't pick up the rope. Kids in rooms like yours often watch for a few days before they join something new. If the lesson is predictable and safe, the watching kid tends to edge in. Not always. But often enough to keep going.

Part seven. The one thing to try.

Here is the one thing. Only one.

This week, on three days in a row, teach a twelve minute lesson on gross pay. Hours times hourly rate. Right before the ten twenty five break.

Here's how it goes.

Start at about ten o'clock. Say, "Short one today. Twelve minutes, then break." Start a timer they can see.

Roll the ball. "Grab a board and a marker for me. Pass one over. Write your initials in the corner." Three easy yeses.

I do. Two examples. Eight hours at seventeen dollars. Five hours at twenty dollars. Think out loud every step. Break the multiplication into tens and ones, out loud, so they hear how.

We do. Two examples together. Six hours at fifteen dollars. Four hours at eighteen dollars. You hold the pen. They give you the pieces. Boards up on three for the final answer.

You do. Three on their own, on the board. Keep the numbers friendly. Ten hours at sixteen dollars. Seven hours at twenty dollars. Four hours at twenty five dollars. Walk around. Look. Nod.

Stop. Even if there is time left. "That's it. You just figured out gross pay. Break's coming."

The next day, do the same shape with slightly harder numbers. The day after, the My Pay Stub sheet's pay section is the you do, on its own.

And then look honestly at one thing. Did each kid get about eight out of ten right? If yes, the steps were the right size. If not, the next lesson gets smaller, not longer.

Write down in your log, for each kid, using initials only, one line. Did he join? How many did he get right? That's all. Three days of that will tell you more than any amount of planning.

Part eight. Closing recap.

Three sentences. Short, explicit teaching with a high success rate is not the enemy of your care and treatment room; for kids who struggle, it is the kindest thing you can do. The shape is I do, we do, you do, in about twelve minutes, with lots of quick chances to respond, and started with a few easy yeses so the hard part never feels like a fight. Put it at the same time every day, right before break, teach the skill under the sheets you already use, and stop while they're winning.

You asked if there's a chance you can actually sit down and teach your kids things. There is. It just looks smaller and steadier than the lessons you planned for a full classroom. And the small steady thing, done every day, is how the kids start to trust that school can go well for them.

Here's a line to close on. In a devotional called With Utmost Patience, Alistair Begg, from Truth For Life, wrote this about the early church: "What built the church was not so much the miracles of the apostles as the faithful, bold endurance of those apostles."

A twelve minute lesson is not a miracle. Doing it again tomorrow is the endurance.
