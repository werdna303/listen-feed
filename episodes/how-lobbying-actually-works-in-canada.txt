Listen. How lobbying actually works in Canada.
For your run. About 30 minutes.

It's a Tuesday in your Civics and Careers class. You've got a news story up on the screen about some new government rule. Maybe it's about trucking, or farming, or vaping. And one of your kids, slouched at the back, says what half the class is thinking. It doesn't matter what we think. Rich companies just pay off the politicians. That's how it works.

And part of you wants to say, no, that's not how it works. But another part of you isn't totally sure. You've heard the word lobbyist your whole life. It sounds a bit shady. Men in suits in Ottawa, handing over envelopes. And you realize you don't actually know what a lobbyist does all day, or what the rules are in Canada, or whether your student is right.

Here's the promise for this run. By the end, you'll know what lobbying actually is, what the rules are in Canada, what research says lobbyists really do, where the real concerns are, and how you can turn it into a lesson that shows your students they're not as powerless as they think.

Part one. What lobbying actually is.

Let's start with a plain definition. Lobbying is trying to influence a government decision by communicating with the people who make it. That's it. A law, a regulation, a policy, a grant, a contract. If somebody is paid to talk to a government official to try to shape one of those decisions, that's lobbying.

The word itself comes from the lobbies of legislatures, the hallways outside the chamber, where people would wait to catch a politician on the way in or out. The picture of a guy in the hallway buttonholing a politician is pretty much where the word was born.

Now here's the first thing to notice. By that definition, lots of people and groups lobby. Big oil companies, sure. Banks, sure. But also charities, hospitals, universities, farmers' associations, environmental groups, unions, including teachers' unions, and organizations that represent people with disabilities. If you've ever seen a group come to Queen's Park or Parliament Hill to push for funding or a change in the rules, that's lobbying.

So lobbying isn't automatically bad. In a democracy, people have every right to tell their government what they think. The groups that know the most about a subject often have information the government needs. A farmers' association knows things about crop insurance that a bureaucrat in Ottawa might not. A trucking association knows what a new safety rule will actually do on the road. If nobody ever talked to government, government would make a lot of uninformed decisions.

Your construction class is a good example. The building code your students learn didn't drop out of the sky. Codes get updated over time, and the people who build, inspect, insure and supply houses all weigh in. Home builders, trades, manufacturers, fire safety groups. Some of them push for stricter rules, some for looser ones, and some for rules that happen to favour the product they sell. When your students learn why a certain stair rise or a certain railing height is required, they're looking at the end result of years of people telling government what they think. Some of that input is wise. Some of it is self interested. Most of it is both.

The concern isn't that people talk to government. The concern is about who gets heard, how much, and whether it happens in the dark. And that's why Canada has rules.

You can see the same pattern in your own world. The school board makes decisions that affect your classroom. When a group of teachers asks for a meeting with a trustee to push for more money for alternative programs like Shifting Gears, that's a kind of lobbying. Nobody would call it corrupt. It's people with knowledge and a stake speaking up. The question is whether everyone with a stake gets a fair chance to speak.

One line for part one. Lobbying is trying to influence a government decision by talking to the people who make it, and lots of groups do it, not just big companies.

Part two. The rules in Canada.

Canada has had a federal law on lobbying since the late nineteen eighties. It started as the Lobbyists Registration Act. In two thousand six, after a period of scandals and a push for cleaner government, Parliament passed the Federal Accountability Act. That renamed the law the Lobbying Act, and when it came into force in two thousand eight, it tightened the rules and created an independent officer to enforce them. That person is the Commissioner of Lobbying, who reports to Parliament, not to the government of the day.

So what does the law actually require? Let me give you three big pieces. Only three.

First, registration. If you're paid to lobby federal officials, you have to register. There are two main kinds of lobbyists. Consultant lobbyists are hired guns. They work for a lobbying firm or on their own, and clients pay them to lobby on their behalf. In house lobbyists are employees of a company or organization, and lobbying is part of their job. A consultant has to register for each client. A company or organization has to register when its employees, together, spend enough of their time lobbying. And the registration is public. It says who's lobbying, for whom, and on what subjects.

If you pull up a registration, it looks fairly dry. The name of the company or client. The names of the people doing the lobbying. A list of subject areas, things like taxation and finance, transportation, environment, health. The government departments they're contacting. And whether they're using what's called grassroots communication, which means encouraging the public to contact officials, like a campaign asking customers to email their member of Parliament. Dry, but useful. It's a map of who is trying to influence what.

Notice who doesn't have to register. A volunteer who isn't paid. A private citizen speaking for himself. The law is aimed at paid influence, because that's where the worry about unfair advantage lies.

Second, reporting meetings. When a registered lobbyist has an arranged, oral conversation with a senior federal official, a minister, a member of Parliament, a senior public servant, they have to report it, every month. Those reports go into a public registry, online, that anyone can search. Including your students. You can look up a big company and see which officials its lobbyists met with, and when, and on roughly what subject.

Third, the cooling off rule. One of the biggest worries about lobbying is the revolving door. A senior official leaves government on Friday, and on Monday he's being paid to lobby his old colleagues. To limit that, the Lobbying Act says that senior officials, the law calls them designated public office holders, can't lobby the federal government for five years after they leave. Ministers, their senior staff, top public servants. Five years.

Why focus on senior officials? Because that's where decisions get made. A minister signs off on policy. Her chief of staff controls her calendar. A deputy minister runs the department. A lobbyist who can get a meeting with those people has far more influence than one who only meets junior staff. So the law shines the brightest light on exactly those meetings. The idea behind all of it is sunlight. You can't ban people from talking to government. But you can make sure the public can see who's talking, and about what.

On top of the law, there's a Lobbyists' Code of Conduct, updated in two thousand twenty three. It covers things like honesty and conflicts of interest. For example, it sets tight limits on gifts and hospitality lobbyists can give to the officials they deal with. Small things, low dollar amounts, with a yearly cap. And it limits lobbying officials you've recently done political work for, like running their campaign.

And there are teeth. Breaking the Lobbying Act can be a criminal offence. The best known case involved a former senior adviser to Prime Minister Stephen Harper named Bruce Carson. After leaving government, he lobbied while he was still under the five year ban. He was convicted in two thousand sixteen and fined, and the courts upheld the convictions on appeal. It was the first conviction of its kind under the act.

And this is just the federal level. Provinces have their own rules. In Ontario, there's a provincial lobbyists registration law, and the province's Integrity Commissioner acts as the registrar of lobbyists. So lobbying at Queen's Park is registered too. Many big cities, including Toronto and Ottawa, have their own lobbyist registries for city hall.

One line for part two. In Canada, paid lobbyists have to register, report their meetings with senior officials in a public registry, and senior officials can't lobby for five years after they leave.

Part three. What lobbyists actually do.

So what does a lobbyist actually do all day? Your student's picture is the envelope of cash. Vote for this and here's your reward. Let me tell you what research suggests.

Two American political scientists, Richard Hall and Alan Deardorff, wrote an influential paper in two thousand six called Lobbying as legislative subsidy. They looked at a puzzle. If lobbying was mostly about buying votes or changing minds, you'd expect lobbyists to spend their time on the politicians who disagree with them. That's who you'd need to win over. But that's not what lobbyists mostly do. They mostly spend time with politicians who already agree with them.

Why? Hall and Deardorff's answer was that lobbyists mostly act like free staff. A member of Parliament has a tiny office, a few staff, and hundreds of issues to deal with. A lobbyist comes along and says, you care about this issue anyway. Here's research. Here's data. Here's a draft of the amendment. Here's what the industry thinks will happen. Here's who else supports it. In effect, the lobbyist subsidizes the politician's work, helping a politician who already leans their way push harder and more effectively.

That paper was about the United States, but the logic travels. And it suggests something important about where lobbying power really lies. Not mostly in bribes, which are illegal and rare. In information, expertise and time. The groups that can afford to hire people to write the reports, track the regulations, show up at every committee meeting and build relationships over years, are the ones that get the most influence.

And here's another thing that surprises people. A lot of lobbying isn't about big, splashy laws at all. It's about the details. The fine print in a regulation. The way a tax credit is worded. Whether a certain product counts under a rule or not. These details can be worth millions to a company, and almost nobody outside the industry is paying attention. That's where a lobbyist with a good relationship and a well prepared memo can make a real difference.

Canada's rules also make a difference compared with the United States. Federally, corporations and unions haven't been allowed to donate to political parties or candidates since two thousand seven, under that same Accountability Act era. Individual donations are capped at fairly modest amounts. Ontario later banned corporate and union donations too. So the American picture of huge corporate cheques flowing to politicians doesn't really fit Canada. That doesn't mean money doesn't matter here. It means it matters more through paying for lobbyists, research and access than through donations.

So is your student right? Partly. Groups with more money do tend to have more influence, because they can afford the information and the time. But it's not usually envelopes. It's memos, meetings and patience.

Let me paint a picture of what a week might look like for a lobbyist. This is a made up example, but it's built from how the process actually works. Say she works for an association of trucking companies. On Monday, she reads the Canada Gazette, which is where the federal government publishes proposed regulations so the public can comment before they're final. There's a proposed change to rules about how many hours a driver can be on the road. She flags it. On Tuesday and Wednesday, she calls member companies to find out what the change would really cost them, and gathers the numbers. On Thursday, she writes a formal submission during the comment period, with data and suggested wording. On Friday, she has a scheduled meeting with a senior official in the transport department to walk through it, and that meeting goes into her monthly report in the public registry. A few months later, if a bill comes up, she might testify before a committee of the House of Commons, where members of Parliament hear from witnesses in public.

Nothing in that week is secret, and nothing is a bribe. But notice how much work it is, and how much it helps to have someone paid to do it full time. A single trucker who's worried about the same rule probably doesn't even know the Gazette exists.

One line for part three. Most lobbying isn't bribery, it's supplying information and help to officials who already lean your way, and money buys influence mainly by paying for that work.

Part four. The real concerns, and the ordinary citizen.

Even with good rules, there are real concerns about lobbying in Canada. Let me name three.

First, the uneven playing field. A big company can afford a team of lobbyists. A group of parents of kids with special needs usually can't. Both are allowed to lobby. But the company will have more meetings, more memos, more relationships. That's not illegal. It's just unequal. And it means some voices get heard a lot more than others.

Second, the gaps in the rules. The registration rules have thresholds. For years, an organization only had to register when its employees spent a significant part of their time lobbying, a line that let a lot of activity slip under the radar. The Commissioner has recently moved to tighten that, so more organizations have to register. And some kinds of communication, like an unplanned chat at an event, don't have to be reported the same way. Watchdog groups regularly argue the rules should be stronger. Others argue they're already complicated enough. That debate is ongoing.

Third, access. Lobbying works through relationships, and relationships are built over years. A former political staffer who knows everyone in a minister's office has an advantage that's hard to measure, even after the cooling off period. That's why the cooling off rules exist, and why people argue about how long they should be.

So, back to your student. Should ordinary people just give up?

Here's the surprising part. Ordinary citizens can lobby too, and they don't have to register. The registration rules are for people who are paid. If you, as a private citizen, write to your member of Parliament, call your provincial member's office, show up at a town hall, or ask for a meeting about something you care about, that's your right, and it's not regulated lobbying.

And it matters more than people think. Politicians and their staff pay close attention to what they hear from constituents, especially personal, specific messages. A thoughtful letter from someone in the riding, explaining how a decision affects their family or their work, often gets more attention than a form email. And a group of constituents who show up, politely and repeatedly, can be very hard to ignore.

There are other tools, too. Anyone can comment on proposed federal regulations during the comment period, the same way that trucking lobbyist did. And there's the petition. Canadians can start an electronic petition to the House of Commons, and if it gets enough signatures, a few hundred, and is presented by a member of Parliament, the government has to give a written response. That's not a guarantee of change. But it's a formal way to put an issue on the record, and it's available to a high school class as much as to a big company.

The lesson from Hall and Deardorff applies here too. If lobbying works by giving officials useful information, then ordinary people can do that too. A teacher who writes to his member at Queen's Park and says, here's what's actually happening in alternative programs for struggling kids, here's what works, here's what would help, is offering exactly the kind of information that busy officials need and rarely get from the ground level.

As Christians, there's something here worth holding too. In Jeremiah chapter twenty nine, God told his exiled people to seek the welfare of the city where they lived, and to pray for it. Speaking up for your students, your neighbours, and people without a voice is part of seeking the welfare of the city. Not to grab power, but to serve.

And churches are part of this picture too, which is worth knowing as someone in church leadership. Charities in Canada, including churches, are allowed to take part in public policy dialogue. They can speak to government about issues connected to their purposes, like poverty or care for refugees, as long as they don't support or oppose a political party or candidate. That's a line the Canada Revenue Agency watches. So if your church ever wants to speak up on something like a local shelter or a care home rule, it can, carefully, and without endorsing anyone.

One line for part four. The real concerns are uneven access, gaps in the rules and the revolving door, but ordinary citizens can lobby too, and a specific, personal message often carries real weight.

How it lands in your actual week.

So let's go back to your Civics and Careers class.

Your student said, rich companies just pay off the politicians. You can now say, partly right, mostly wrong, and here's how it really works. And then you can show them.

Here's a lesson you could run. Pull up the federal lobbying registry online. It's public and searchable. Have your students pick a company or organization they care about. Maybe a big gaming company, a phone company, a fast food chain, or an organization connected to a cause they care about. Look up whether it's registered, what subjects it lobbies on, and which officials it's met with. Kids are often stunned that this information is just sitting there for anyone to see. It turns a vague suspicion into something concrete.

Then flip it. Have each student pick one issue that affects them or their family. Bus routes. The cost of a driver's licence. Mental health services. Part time job rules for teenagers. And have them write a short, specific letter to their member of Parliament or your member at Queen's Park. Not a rant. A message that says who they are, what the issue is, how it affects them, and one thing they'd like the official to do. You can talk about what makes a message effective. Personal. Specific. Polite. Short.

Some of your Shifting Gears kids have never been asked their opinion by anyone in power. Learning that they're allowed to speak to their representative, and that it's actually how the system is supposed to work, can be a big deal. And if one of them gets a real reply, put it on the wall. Nothing teaches a kid that his voice counts quite like a letter on official letterhead with his name at the top. Even a form reply is a small piece of evidence that someone in power had to read what he wrote.

You could also run a role play. Split the class into groups. One group is a big company that wants a rule changed. One group is a community group that wants it kept. One group plays the member of Parliament and her staff. Each group prepares a short pitch. Afterward, talk about what was persuasive and why. Was it the facts? The personal stories? Who got more meetings? It brings Hall and Deardorff to life without ever mentioning their names.

There's a media literacy lesson in here too. When your students see a news story that says, the industry warns, or, a coalition of groups says, teach them to ask one question. Who is that, exactly, and what do they want? Often you can look them up in the registry and see that the coalition is mostly a few big companies, or that the friendly sounding group is funded by an industry. That doesn't make what they say false. It just tells you whose interests are behind it. That's a skill your kids will use every time they scroll.

And there's a balance to strike. Your student started from cynicism. They all just get paid off. Cynicism feels smart, but it's actually lazy. It lets you off the hook from finding out how things work and from doing anything about it. What you want to build in your kids isn't cynicism or naive trust. It's curiosity. Who's talking to whom? What are the rules? Who's missing from the room? And how could I get in?

Finally, there's you. You know more about struggling teens in an alternative program than almost anyone at Queen's Park. That's valuable information. If there's a policy that affects your kids, a funding change, a rule about credits, a cut to support staff, you're allowed to tell your member at Queen's Park what you see. A short, calm, specific letter from someone on the ground is exactly the kind of information busy officials need.
