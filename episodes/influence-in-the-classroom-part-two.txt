Part one. Your gut is not a lie detector.

Let's start with the hardest truth first. You are not very good at telling when someone is lying. Neither am I. Neither is almost anybody.

In two thousand six, two researchers, Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo, gathered up about two hundred studies on lie detection. Tens of thousands of people judging whether someone was telling the truth or lying. And what did they find? On average, people got it right about fifty four percent of the time. Flipping a coin gets you fifty. So we're a little better than a coin. Not much.

And here's the part that should humble anyone in authority. Professionals didn't do much better. Police officers, judges, customs agents, people whose jobs involve spotting liars. Roughly the same as everybody else. Experience made them more confident, not more accurate.

There was another pattern in that review that's worth knowing. People were better at recognizing truths than lies. When someone was telling the truth, observers said so most of the time. When someone was lying, observers caught it less than half the time. In other words, we lean toward believing people. That's mostly a good thing. Life would be miserable if we doubted everyone. But it means that a confident liar gets past us more often than we'd like to think, and a nervous truth teller can get flagged as a liar. Your gut is tilted, and it's tilted in ways that don't match the kids in front of you.

Why are we so bad at it? Partly because the signs we look for aren't real signs. Part two. Why the tough approach backfires.

So if you can't read faces, what about pressure? What about the approach you see on cop shows? Sit him down, tell him you know he did it, don't accept his denials, wear him down until he cracks.

That's roughly what's called the accusatory approach. In North America, the best known version is called the Reid technique, after John Reid, who developed it in the middle of last century. Police were trained in it for decades. The interviewer confronts the suspect, says they're sure of his guilt, cuts off denials, and then offers him an easy way to confess. Something like, I don't think you're a bad person, I think it just got out of hand. In some places, the interviewer can even pretend to have evidence they don't have.

And it does get confessions. That's the thing. Pressure works, in the sense that people confess. The problem is that innocent people confess too.

Researchers have studied this carefully. Christian Meissner, Allison Redlich and colleagues reviewed the studies comparing two broad styles. The accusatory style, and what they call the information gathering style, where the goal isn't a confession, it's the full story. The study found that in experiments, the accusatory style increased confessions overall, but it increased false confessions too. The information gathering style kept the true confessions, and sometimes got more of them, while cutting down the false ones. A newer update of that review, in two thousand twenty four, found much the same thing.

And here's what matters for you. Young people are especially likely to confess falsely under pressure. Teenagers tend to focus on the short term. Get out of this room. Make this stop. The idea that a confession will cost them something next week feels far away compared to the pressure right now. A psychologist named Gisli Gudjonsson, who has spent his career on false confessions, has written a great deal about how age, stress and a strong wish to please or escape make some people far more vulnerable.

England learned this the hard way. In the nineteen eighties and early nineties, several high profile cases fell apart because confessions turned out to be false or forced. So the police there changed their whole approach. They built a model called PEACE, and under it, officers aren't allowed to lie to suspects about evidence. The goal of the interview is to get accurate information, not to get a confession. Gudjonsson and a former police officer named John Pearse wrote a paper in two thousand eleven arguing that the North American approach should be replaced with something like PEACE.

Part three. Ask for the whole story.

So what does information gathering actually look like? Let me give you the simplified, classroom version of what police trained in the PEACE model do. Three moves.

First move. Get them away from the audience and lower the temperature. No confession happens in front of other kids. That's a face saving thing, and you know it better than I do. So it's a quiet word. Tyler, can you help me with something for a minute? Out in the hall, or by the lumber rack. And you explain, plainly, what's going on. Something like, there's a gouge in the bench that wasn't there twenty minutes ago. I'm trying to figure out what happened. I'm talking to everybody who was here. Notice what that does. It's true. It's calm. And it doesn't accuse him.

Second move. Ask for the whole story, with an open question, and then be quiet. Police interviewers are often trained to use questions that start with words like tell, explain and describe. Tell me what happened in here from when I left. Walk me through it. Describe what you were doing. Not, did you use the router? That's a yes or no question, and it hands him the easy answer. No. An open question asks for a story. And a story has details.

Then comes the part that's hardest for a teacher. Silence. You ask the question, and you wait. Don't fill the gap. Don't offer him an answer. Kids, like adults, tend to fill silence. And what they fill it with is often more than they meant to say. If he gives you a short answer, I don't know, I was on my phone, then ask for more. Okay. Tell me more about that. What did you see? Who was near the router?

Third move. Listen, and don't argue yet. This is where you just gather. Let him tell his version all the way through. Don't challenge it in the middle, even if you're sure part of it is false. You're building a picture. Every detail he gives you is something you can check later. And if he's telling the truth, you're giving him room to say so, which is exactly what you want.

You might find, at this point, that the whole thing resolves. Sometimes kids just tell you. Yeah. It was me. I was messing around with it and it slipped. That happens more often than you'd think when the room is calm, the audience is gone, and the question is honest. A kid who's been accused will defend himself. A kid who's been asked will sometimes just tell.

And there's a step before all three moves, one police interviewers treat as essential. The P in PEACE stands for planning. Before you talk to anyone, take a minute. Literally one minute, by the lumber rack, before you call the kid over. Ask yourself two questions. What do I actually know? And what do I just think? You know the gouge is new. You know four kids were in the room. You know the router is out of its case. You think it was Tyler. That's the hunch, and it goes in a different column. That one minute keeps you from walking into the conversation treating a hunch like a fact. It also tells you which real facts you have to work with later.

What about the kid who just says, I don't know, to everything? That's common with your students, and it's often not lying. It's a shield. A way of not getting involved. Don't fight the shield head on. Make the question smaller and more concrete. Okay. Where were you standing when I left? What was the first thing you did? Who was beside you? Small, specific, easy questions often get a kid talking, and once he's talking, the story tends to come.

Part four. Hold your cards, then show them one at a time.

You've probably seen the old show where Chris Hansen confronted men who'd come to meet a teenager they'd been chatting with online. Whatever you think of the show, his interview style was interesting. He didn't scream. He didn't open with you're a predator. He was calm, almost polite. He'd ask the man why he was there. And he'd let him explain. I was just dropping off something. I didn't know how old she was. And then, calmly, Hansen would pull out a page and read the man's own messages back to him.

That's a dramatic version of something researchers have actually studied. It's called the strategic use of evidence. It was developed by two researchers, Pär Anders Granhag in Sweden and Maria Hartwig in New York. And the core idea is about timing.

Here's the logic. If you tell a kid everything you know at the start, you've given him a map. He can build a story that fits around your evidence. Oh, yeah, I was at the router, but I was just looking. But if you hold what you know, and let him tell his story first, he has to guess what you know. And people who are lying tend to avoid or deny things that are actually known. People telling the truth tend to just say what happened, including the stuff you already know.

The study found that when evidence was revealed late in an interview, rather than at the start, it became much easier to spot who was lying, because liars' stories clashed with the evidence. Later work found it helped to reveal evidence step by step, a little at a time, rather than all at once. And other studies found that this approach can shift people from hiding to telling, because once they realize you really do know things, holding back stops making sense.

So in your shop, it looks like this. Tyler tells his story. I was on my phone the whole time. I didn't go near the router. You listen. You say, okay. And then you reveal one thing, calmly.
