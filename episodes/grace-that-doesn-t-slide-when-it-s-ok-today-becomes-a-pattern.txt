Grace That Doesn't Slide

It's a weekday morning in your classroom. The desk sheet is out. Someone comes in, drops into a chair, puts their hood up, and lays their head down on their arms. You look at them and you can tell they're wiped. Maybe they worked late. Maybe home was loud. Maybe they just didn't sleep. And you think, okay, today, you need it. Rest. It's fine.

That felt kind. And honestly, it might have been the right call that day.

But then the next day, same chair, same hood, same head on the desk. And the day after that. And now there are two heads down instead of one. And you're standing at the front of the room with the desk sheets in your hand, thinking, how did we get here? And how do I get us back without becoming somebody I don't want to be?

On Thursday morning you said something really honest about this. You said you think the reason you don't like being gracious is that you know yourself. When someone gives you grace, you take a little, then a little more, then a little more. And now you feel like your students are doing the exact same thing. Later that day you said something else. You said, why am I scared to be firm with the students? It's like they've learned how to shut me right down.

So here's the promise for this episode. By the end of this run, you'll understand why one kind exception so often becomes a pattern, why being firm feels so scary right now, and how to reset a norm like sleeping in class in a way that is still full of grace.

We're going to do this in four ideas. First, how a slide happens. Second, the difference between grace and permission. Third, why firmness feels scary. Fourth, how to actually reset the norm. Then we'll land it in your week, and I'll give you one thing to try.

Let's go.

Idea one. How a slide happens.

There's a sociologist named Diane Vaughan who studied the Challenger space shuttle disaster from 1986. She spent years digging into how smart, careful engineers ended up launching a shuttle with a known problem. And what she found wasn't a group of reckless people. It was something quieter.

She called it the normalization of deviance.

Here's what that means in plain words. A small exception happens. Nothing bad follows. So the next time, that exception doesn't feel like an exception anymore. It feels normal. Then a slightly bigger exception happens, and that feels okay too, because the last one was fine. Each step is small. No single step feels like a big decision. But step by step, the line moves. And nobody ever stood up and said, let's move the line. It just moved.

That's the slide.

Now, your classroom is not a space shuttle. Nobody is in danger because a student napped. But the pattern is the same. Day one, you made an exception: you need it, rest. Nothing bad happened. The room was calm. Maybe it was even a little easier. So day two, when the head goes down again, it doesn't feel like a new decision. It feels like continuing yesterday. And for the student, the same thing is happening on their side. Yesterday it was allowed. So today it's just what we do.

That's the key insight. The slide doesn't happen because of one big bad decision. It happens because nobody marked the first exception as an exception.

Here's what I mean by marking it. There's a big difference between "it's okay, you need it" and "today you can rest for twenty minutes, and then I need you back with us." The first one sounds like a new rule. The second one sounds like a one-time gift with an edge on it. Same kindness. Very different message about tomorrow.

And here's the honest part. You said you recognize this in yourself. When you're given grace, you take a little more and a little more. That's not some special flaw in you. That's how humans work. All of us drift toward whatever is easiest unless something marks the line. Your students aren't being uniquely manipulative. They're being human, the same way you are. And knowing that from the inside is actually a gift, because you know exactly how it feels from their side of the desk.

So, idea one in a line: a slide happens when an exception isn't marked as an exception, so it quietly becomes the new normal.

Idea two. Grace is not the same as permission.

I want to push back gently on something you said. You said you don't like being gracious. I don't think that's quite it. I think what you don't like is permission that's wearing grace's clothes.

Let's pull those apart.

Permission says: this is fine now. The standard has changed. Do whatever.

Grace says: you didn't meet the standard, and I'm still for you. The standard is still there. And I'm going to help you get back to it.

Do you hear the difference? Permission lowers the bar. Grace keeps the bar and stays beside the person.

There's a great line of teaching research on this. A researcher named Judith Kleinfeld, back in the 1970s, studied teachers in Alaska who were unusually effective with students many other teachers struggled to reach. She called the best of them warm demanders. Later, two education researchers, Elizabeth Bondy and Dorene Ross, wrote a well known article in 2008 called The Teacher as Warm Demander. The idea is simple. The teachers who do best with kids who've had a hard road are not the softest ones, and they're not the strictest ones. They're the ones who are both. Very warm. And very demanding. At the same time.

Here's the part that matters for you. In that picture, the demanding part is a form of warmth. When a warm demander insists, it's saying: I believe you can do this. I'm not going to give up on you. Low expectations, even kind ones, can quietly send the opposite message: I don't really think you can do more than this.

So when a student puts their head down every day and you let it go every day, part of the message they receive is care. But another part might be: he's stopped expecting anything from me. And for a lot of teenagers in an alternative program, that second message is one they've heard way too many times already.

That's a hard thought. But it's also freeing. Because it means getting firm isn't you stepping away from grace. It's you stepping further into it.

There's also a parenting research idea that lines up with this. Diana Baumrind, a psychologist, described a style she called authoritative. Not authoritarian. Authoritative. High warmth and high expectations together. Authoritarian is cold and demanding. Permissive is warm with few expectations. And across many years of research, the warm and demanding combination tends to come out best for kids. Classroom researchers have found a similar pattern with teachers. Students tend to do better when the adult is caring and also clear.

So, idea two in a line: grace keeps the standard and stays with the person; permission drops the standard and calls it kindness.

Okay. Quick pause. So far we've got two things. One, a slide happens when an exception isn't marked as an exception. Two, grace and permission are different. Grace holds the line and holds the person.

Now let's talk about the part that's probably bothering you the most.

Idea three. Why being firm feels so scary.

You said it yourself: why am I scared to be firm with the students? It's like they've learned how to shut me right down.

I want to take that seriously, because it's real. There are a few reasons this feels scary, and naming them takes away some of their power.

The first reason is the relationship. In a small classroom like yours, relationships are everything. You know these students. You know their stories, or at least some of them. You know that many of them have had adults who were harsh, or who gave up on them, or who were inconsistent. So you've worked hard to be safe. And somewhere in your head, firmness feels like it might undo all that work. Like if you push, they'll pull away, and you'll lose them.

But here's the thing. The relationship is what makes firmness work. Firmness from a stranger feels like control. Firmness from someone who has shown up for you, again and again, feels like being taken seriously. You've already built the relationship. That's not the thing at risk. That's the thing you get to spend.

The second reason is the shutdown. You said they've learned how to shut you right down. Think about what that looks like. Maybe a sigh. A look. A muttered comment. A shrug. "I'm tired." "Leave me alone." A head that goes right back down. And in that moment, you feel a flash of awkwardness, maybe embarrassment, maybe the fear of a confrontation in front of the others. So you back off. And the tension goes away.

Now here's what's happening underneath. When you back off and the tension goes away, you feel relief. And relief is a powerful reward. Your brain learns: when they push back, retreat, and you'll feel better. Meanwhile, on their side, they learn: when he asks, push back a little, and he'll stop. Nobody planned this. It's just two sets of people learning from what works. Behaviour researchers have described this kind of back and forth for decades. Each side's behaviour gets reinforced by the other side's response, and the cycle tightens.

That's what "they've learned how to shut me down" really means. It's not that they're scheming. It's that the shutdown has worked, so it keeps happening. And the good news is, anything that was learned can be unlearned. If the shutdown stops working, it will slowly stop happening.

The third reason, and maybe the deepest one, is about you. You told me something honest: you know you take a little more and a little more when given grace. So when you think about being firm with them, part of you might feel like a hypocrite. Who am I to hold the line, when I'm not great at holding it myself?

I want to say this clearly. You don't have to be perfect to hold a standard. You only have to be honest. In fact, the person who knows how the slide feels from the inside is often the best person to help someone else out of it. You're not standing above them. You're standing next to them, saying, I know this one. I do it too. And I'm not going to let us both drift.

So, idea three in a line: firmness feels scary because of the relationship, the shutdown loop, and your own honesty about yourself, and none of those are reasons to stay soft.

Idea four. How to actually reset the norm.

This is the practical part. And I want to start with the most important thing first. Some of your students may really be exhausted for real reasons. So before we talk about resetting, let's be fair about sleep.

Teenagers really do need a lot of sleep. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends about eight to ten hours a night for teens. That's a lot. And during the teen years, the body clock shifts later. Teens naturally get sleepy later at night and want to wake later in the morning. On top of that, some of your students might be working evening shifts, caring for siblings, dealing with chaos at home, or on medications that make them drowsy. So when a head goes down, sometimes it really is fatigue, not defiance.

That matters, because a good reset doesn't pretend that tiredness isn't real. It just says the classroom still has a shape, even on tired days.

So here's how a reset can go. Three parts. Own it. Name it. Hold it.

Part one, own it. Start by taking responsibility for your part. This is huge, and it's what keeps the reset from feeling like an attack. You could say something like this to the whole group, on a fresh morning, calm voice, not in the middle of a conflict:

"Hey, I want to talk about something, and this one's on me. A few weeks ago someone was really tired, and I said it was fine to put their head down. And I meant it. But I didn't say it was just for that day, and since then sleeping in class has kind of become normal. That's my fault, not yours. So I'm fixing it."

Notice what that does. Nobody is being called out. Nobody is in trouble. You're modelling exactly the thing you want them to learn: noticing a slide and owning it.

Part two, name it. Say the new norm clearly, once, in plain words. Keep it short. Something like:

"Here's how it's going to work. Class time is awake time. If you're really wiped, come tell me privately. I might give you a few minutes, a drink of water, a walk, or a standing job. But heads down for the whole morning isn't an option anymore."

That's it. Not a speech. Not a lecture on responsibility. One clear picture of what's expected, and one clear picture of what to do when you're struggling. The second part is the grace. You're not saying tiredness doesn't matter. You're giving them a better way to handle it than disappearing into their arms.

Part three, hold it. This is where it gets real. Because the first time a head goes down after the reset, everyone is watching to see if you meant it. That moment matters more than the speech.

So what do you do? You go over quietly. You don't announce it to the room. You crouch down or lean in, and you say something low and kind and short. Something like: "Hey. Remember what we talked about. Let's get you up. Want some water or want to stand for a bit?" Then you give them a moment. You don't hover. You don't argue. You don't lecture.

And here's the big one. If you get the shutdown, the sigh, the "I'm tired, leave me alone," you don't take the bait and you don't retreat. You stay calm and repeat the expectation, almost boringly. "I hear you. You're tired. Let's get you up anyway." And then you give it a minute, and come back if you need to. Calm and repeated beats loud and once.

Teaching coaches sometimes talk about this as being both warm and strict, at the same time, in the same sentence. You don't have to pick. "I care about you, and I need you up" is one sentence, not two different moods.

There's also a small thing that makes a big difference. Catch them doing it right. When a student who usually sleeps stays up and works, even for twenty minutes, notice it. Privately is often best with older teens. "Hey, thanks for staying with us this morning. I know you were tired." That tells them the standard isn't about control. It's about you wanting them there.

And expect a little pushback at first. When a habit that used to work stops working, people often push harder for a short time before they give up on it. Behaviour researchers sometimes call that a burst. It can feel like the reset is failing. Usually it means the reset is working. If you hold steady for a week or two, it tends to settle.

So, idea four in a line: reset by owning your part, naming the norm once and clearly, and holding it calmly and privately, with a real path for genuinely tired kids.

Let's recap all four ideas.

One, a slide happens when an exception isn't marked as an exception.

Two, grace keeps the standard and stays with the person. Permission just drops the standard.

Three, firmness feels scary because of the relationship, the shutdown loop, and your own honesty about yourself. None of those are reasons to stay soft.

And four, reset by owning it, naming it, and holding it.

Now let's bring this into your actual week.

In the classroom. Monday morning is a natural fresh start. If you do the reset, do it near the beginning of the day, before anyone has their head down. That way it's a plan, not a reaction. Keep it under a minute. Then go straight into the desk sheet like normal. Don't make it heavier than it is.

Think ahead about your options for tired students so you're not making them up on the spot. Water. Standing at the counter. A quick walk to drop something at the office. A hands-on job. A few minutes with the window open. Having two or three ready means that when a student is really exhausted, your response is grace with a shape, not grace with no edges.

And think about which students you might want to talk to one on one before Monday. Not to scold them. Just to give them a heads up, privately, so they don't feel ambushed in front of the group. Something like: "Hey, I'm going to talk to everyone Monday about sleeping in class. It's not about you specifically. I just want you to know first, because I care about how you're doing. Is there anything going on that's making you this tired?" That last question might open something important. A job, a home situation, a health thing. And if it does, you'll know who to loop in, whether that's your colleagues, the student's support team, or whoever is the right person for that student.

Also, think about the other adults in your room or building. If you have support staff, let them know the plan, so the message is the same no matter who's talking. One adult holding a line while another lets it slide is the fastest way back to where you started.

Now let's talk about faith, because this is where the whole thing comes together.

You're a Christian, and I think part of why this feels tangled is that grace matters so much to you. You don't want to be harsh. You want to treat these students the way God has treated you. That's a good instinct. Hold onto it.

But think about what God's grace actually looks like. It's not permission. Grace doesn't say, "Sin doesn't matter, carry on." It says, "You're forgiven, you're loved, and now come with me." Jesus with the woman caught in adultery is a great picture. He refuses to condemn her. Completely. And then he says, "Go, and from now on sin no more." Both in the same moment. No condemnation, and a real call to change. That's the warm demander, two thousand years before anyone used the phrase.

Or think about how Paul writes to Titus. He says the grace of God has appeared, and it trains us. Not just forgives us. Trains us. Grace in the Bible has a direction to it. It doesn't just accept people where they are. It loves them too much to leave them there.

And here's the part that speaks right to what you said about yourself. You said you take a little more and a little more when given grace. Paul actually saw that coming. In Romans he asks the question out loud: should we keep on sinning so that grace may increase? And his answer is basically, absolutely not. He knew people would try to treat grace as permission. He'd seen it. And his answer wasn't to make grace smaller. It was to show what grace is really for.

So when you hold the line with your students, gently, consistently, with your own failings out in the open, you're not stepping away from grace. You're showing them what grace looks like when it's real. Forgiveness, yes. Patience, yes. Tired days, understood. And also: I believe you're capable of more, and I'm going to stay with you while you get there.

That's also a picture of how God deals with you in your own slides. Not with a lecture. Not with a shrug. With a steady, loving, "Come on. Let's get you up."

At home, by the way, the same thing applies. Anywhere you notice the little-more-and-a-little-more pattern in yourself, whether that's phone time at night, spending, skipping a habit you care about, the fix isn't shame. It's marking the exception. "Tonight, okay. Tomorrow, back to normal." Say the line out loud, even just to yourself. Exceptions that are named stay exceptions.
